## The page

The DPG Standard is a set of specifications and guidelines designed to maximise consensus about whether a digital solution conforms to the definition of a digital public good. The definition is the UN Secretary-General's: digital public goods are open-source software, open datasets, open AI systems, and open content collections that adhere to privacy and other applicable laws and best practices, do no harm by design, and help attain the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs).

The DPG Standard establishes the baseline requirements that must be met in order to earn recognition as a digital public good. When a digital solution is successfully reviewed against the DPG Standard, it is listed in the DPG Registry alongside all other recognised digital public goods.

The nine indicators and requirements that determine if nominated software, data, AI systems, and/or content collections can be considered a DPG:

1. SDG Relevance
2. Open Licencing
3. Clear Ownership
4. Platform Independence
5. Documentation
6. Non-PII Data Extraction
7. Privacy & Applicable Laws
8. Open Standards & Best Practices
9. Do No Harm by Design: 9A Data Privacy & Security, 9B Inappropriate & Illegal Content, 9C Protection from Harassment

Disclaimers: the DPG Standard exclusively focuses on the design of the core solution; the Digital Public Goods Alliance does not evaluate local implementations as part of its review. The Review Policy lays out the policies that govern the review of applications. An eligibility tool lets projects assess readiness before nominating.

The DPG Standard is an open standard licensed under CC-BY-SA 4.0 and maintained by the DPGA Secretariat with contributions from a global community. It was developed through an iterative process, building off the original 51-indicator standard used by the DPGA in the preliminary review of Early Grade Reading projects, and refined through contributions by many experts.

## The indicators, as described in the standard's repository

1. **Relevance to Sustainable Development Goals.** The project must demonstrate how it is relevant to advancing one or more of the UN SDGs.
2. **Use of approved open licenses.** Software must use an OSI-approved license; content must use a Creative Commons license (CC-BY, CC-BY-SA or CC0 preferred); data must use an Open Data Commons-approved license.
3. **Clear ownership.** Ownership of the assets the digital public good produces must be clearly defined, through copyright, trademark or public documentation.
4. **Platform independence.** Where there are closed dependencies, the project must show it is independent of them or identify functional open alternatives.
5. **Documentation.** Documentation of the source code, use cases and/or functional requirements, as fits the type of project (software, content or data).
6. **Mechanism for extracting data and content.** Non-personally identifiable information must be extractable in non-proprietary formats.
7. **Adherence to privacy and applicable laws.** The project must comply with relevant privacy regulations and other applicable laws.
8. **Adherence to standards and best practices.** The design must align with relevant industry standards and established best practices.
9. **Do no harm by design.** The project must anticipate and prevent harm through intentional design: (a) data privacy and security for systems that handle personal data; (b) policies and removal processes for inappropriate and illegal content; (c) protection from harassment, including protections for minors, on interactive platforms.

Requirements 7 to 9 are attested by an authorized representative of the project.

## AI systems

The standard was updated in 2025 to allow rigorous assessment of AI systems as digital public goods. For an AI system the bar is set by the open-licensing indicator applied to the whole system: the training data must be publicly available under an open license, and safety must be prioritised in development, with fairness, accountability and transparency treated as requirements. As of late 2025 only a small number of AI systems had been submitted in the Open AI System category, pending open questions about training-data openness and do-no-harm evidence; the DPGA planned a one-year review of the AI updates in 2026. (From the DPGA's 2025 ecosystem reporting and the standard's public issue tracker; the standard page itself does not spell out the AI-specific requirements.)
