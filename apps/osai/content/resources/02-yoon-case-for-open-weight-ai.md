When the Chinese startup Moonshot released Kimi K3 — a model that nearly matched leading U.S. models at a fraction of their cost — it stoked a passionate debate in Silicon Valley around open-weight AI. At the heart of things was a fundamental question: how would the development of increasingly sophisticated open-weight models affect the concentration and distribution of power in the AI age?

In the weeks and months since Kimi’s release, tech executives including Mark Zuckerberg, Dario Amodei and Bill Gates have all laid out distinct visions of how this technology should be shared and governed. A broad coalition of tech companies, investors, researchers and open-source organizations signed an open letter arguing that open weights are important to American innovation, competition, security and technological leadership. As the debates unfurled in public view, varied positions were often flattened into a false binary: for open-weight AI or against it.

When asked for my view on the subject, I tend to approach the question less from an ideological lens and more from a pragmatic one. To me, the debate is really about scientific progress and what it requires. It is not a matter of being for or against open-weight models – it is about understanding their necessary role in helping this technology reach its full potential.

Long before AI became a commercial product, it was the result of scientific inquiry conducted across universities, government programs and laboratories. Government funding sustained major research programs, while shared resources such as ImageNet gave researchers a common basis for comparing their work. The transformer architecture behind today’s language models entered the field through a paper published by Google researchers.

This spirit of independent inquiry is the only reason we’ve been able to get to where we are with AI in the first place. The rapid advancement of this technology is evidence of what the confluence of shared data, public funding, and the work of researchers inside of *and* beyond the major labs can achieve. Open weights enable that through the level of transparency and experimentation they provide, without revealing every part of a model’s development. Without that openness, a small number of companies could come to control both the most capable AI systems and how much the rest of the world is allowed to learn about them.

Google DeepMind’s work on its AI system AlphaFold shows what openness can make possible. In 2020, its researchers made a major advance on the decades-old problem of predicting how proteins fold into three-dimensional shapes based on the sequence of their amino acids. DeepMind then published its methods, publicly released AlphaFold’s source code and worked with the European Bioinformatics Institute to make predicted structures freely available. The impact was immediate and broad, reaching nearly every country around the world.

Scientists globally began using those structures to study disease, develop medicines, improve crops and investigate antibiotic resistance. By late 2025, more than three million researchers in over 190 countries had used AlphaFold. One breakthrough opened so many new paths for research around the world.

Open-weight models can allow AI research to spread in a similar way. Universities, startups and independent researchers can examine how systems behave, test claims made by developers and adapt models to questions that may not fit a commercial laboratory’s priorities. This democratizes power, giving people outside the leading AI companies a seat at the table.

As an investor in early-stage AI-native companies, I also see an economic case for open weights. Closed models already allow startups to build valuable products, but a company that depends entirely on one provider remains exposed to its prices, access rules and product decisions. Open alternatives give startups more bargaining power.

Still, open weights are not the end-all solution. The largest open models can still be prohibitively expensive to operate, while training advanced systems requires immense capital and computing power. A university may have access to a model’s weights without being able to reproduce its training, and a startup may be able to choose its model while remaining dependent on chips and cloud infrastructure supplied by a handful of companies.

I also believe we should be clear-sighted about the risks of open weights, particularly as models become more capable. Dario Amodei raised a valid concern that once weights are released, they cannot be withdrawn. Additionally, safeguards can be removed and advanced capabilities may be adapted for harmful purposes.

This is where I believe government needs to step in. Once model weights are public, the consequences extend far beyond the developer that released them. Government therefore has a responsibility to decide when the risks of release outweigh the benefits.

Elected officials should make that judgment based on what a model can do and the harm it could cause. Frontier developers should face strict requirements for security testing, independent evaluation, and incident reporting. If a model has capabilities that pose a credible threat to public safety or national security, restrictions on its release may be warranted.

At the same time, closed models should not be presumed safe. They can be breached or misused, and companies may deploy them in sensitive settings without giving outsiders enough access to evaluate them. Developers and the institutions using their models should remain accountable for the risks they create, whether the underlying system is open or closed.

The rules put in place by the public sector should also avoid reinforcing the concentration they are intended to address. Large AI companies can absorb expensive testing and lengthy approval processes, while universities and startups often cannot. If every developer faces requirements designed for a frontier laboratory, fewer institutions will be able to conduct meaningful AI research. The government can mitigate this by working as a liaison between private companies and universities, expanding academic access to computing resources and funding independent testing facilities.

These investments would allow more researchers to scrutinize advanced systems without depending on the companies that built them.

The debate over open weights has been treated as a contest between two fixed positions. In practice, different models may require different judgments. Open weights should be protected when they advance research and competition without creating unacceptable risks. When a model develops capabilities that make its release dangerous, the government should be prepared to restrict access.

The difficult work lies in deciding where to draw that line, and in revisiting it as the technology continues to change. But the stakes are too high to leave that judgment — and the power that comes with it — in the hands of the few, rather than the many. Striking the right balance can’t fall to industry, or to the government, alone. It will take developers, researchers, and policymakers building it together, drawing on the same collaborative spirit that got us this far to carry us forward.
