## The site

artificialintelligenceact.eu is maintained by the Future of Life Institute and serves over 150,000 monthly users. It describes the EU AI Act as "a European regulation on artificial intelligence (AI), the first comprehensive regulation on AI by a major regulator anywhere." It offers:

- the AI Act Explorer, the searchable full text of the regulation;
- a Compliance Checker, a ten-minute assessment of which obligations apply to a given system;
- an implementation timeline of the dates and tasks for the EU and the Member States;
- guides and summaries for businesses, SMEs and specific sectors, and tracking of the General-Purpose AI Code of Practice and the AI Office's guidelines.

## The Act, in the site's high-level summary

**Risk tiers.** The Act sorts AI systems into four tiers.

1. Unacceptable risk, prohibited: systems deploying subliminal, manipulative or deceptive techniques or exploiting vulnerabilities; biometric categorisation that infers sensitive attributes; social scoring; emotion recognition in workplaces and schools; untargeted scraping of facial images; and real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for law enforcement, with narrow exceptions.
2. High risk: safety components of products covered by the EU's product-safety laws (Annex I), and the use cases in Annex III: biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment, access to essential public and private services, law enforcement, migration and border control, and the administration of justice and democratic processes.
3. Limited risk: transparency duties, for example that chatbots tell users they are talking to a machine and that synthetic content is labelled.
4. Minimal risk: unregulated.

**High-risk obligations.** Providers must run a risk-management system across the system's lifecycle, govern their training data, keep technical documentation, design for logging and human oversight, meet accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity standards, run a quality-management system, pass a conformity assessment, affix the CE marking and register the system in the EU database. Deployers have duties of their own, including human oversight and, for public bodies, a fundamental-rights impact assessment.

**General-purpose AI (GPAI) models.** All providers must keep technical documentation, give downstream providers the information they need to comply, respect the Copyright Directive, and publish "a sufficiently detailed summary about the content used for training." Free and open-source GPAI models, with parameters publicly available, need only comply with the copyright and training-summary duties, unless they present systemic risk. A model is presumed to present systemic risk when the cumulative compute used to train it exceeds 10^25 floating-point operations (FLOPs); the Commission can also designate models. Providers of systemic-risk models must additionally run adversarial testing and model evaluations, assess and mitigate systemic risks, track and report serious incidents, and ensure adequate cybersecurity. The Code of Practice is the main route to showing compliance.

**Open source more broadly.** AI systems released under free and open-source licences are outside the Act unless they are placed on the market as high-risk systems, fall under the prohibited practices, or carry the transparency duties.

**Governance.** The AI Office inside the European Commission supervises GPAI models and coordinates implementation; national market-surveillance authorities enforce the rules for AI systems; a European Artificial Intelligence Board, a scientific panel and an advisory forum support them.

**Penalties.** Up to EUR 35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover for prohibited practices; up to EUR 15 million or 3% for most other violations; up to EUR 7.5 million or 1% for supplying incorrect information. Lower caps apply to SMEs and startups.

**Timeline.** The Act entered into force on 1 August 2024. Prohibitions and AI-literacy duties applied from 2 February 2025. The GPAI obligations and most of the governance provisions applied from 2 August 2025. The high-risk obligations, originally due in August 2026, were pushed back by the Digital Omnibus package: Annex III high-risk systems from 2 December 2027, with full application, including Annex I product-safety systems, by 2 August 2028. Member States had to set up at least one regulatory sandbox by 2 August 2026.
