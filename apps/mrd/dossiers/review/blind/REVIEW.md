### 2.2 Findings and sources, by indicator

**q13, exemption from prosecution for trafficked and smuggled migrants**

- **Philippines (A), correct.** RA 9208 §17 as amended by RA 10364 says trafficked persons "shall not be penalized for unlawful acts committed as a direct result of … being trafficked" (lawphil.net/statutes/repacts/ra2013/ra_10364_2013.html). The Philippines has no smuggling statute, and CA 613 §45(4) punishes unlawful entry (lawphil.net/statutes/comacts/ca1940/ca_613_1940.html). Coding "No" because smuggled migrants are not covered is a defensible strict reading of the combined question.
- **Jordan (B), correct.** Law 9/2009 art. 12(c) gives the prosecutor only a discretion to stop prosecuting a victim, and the 2021 amendments added no exemption. The US TIP 2024 report says victims are still penalised for immigration offences.
- **Spain (C), correct.** Penal Code art. 177 bis.11 exempts trafficking victims (BOE-A-1995-25444). Irregular entry is an administrative matter. LOEX art. 59 gives a discretionary administrative exemption to smuggling victims who cooperate. One minor mistake: C describes art. 59 bis as a *criminal* exemption, but it is about residence and recovery.
- **Colombia (D), wrong.** It lists Penal Code art. 188, Law 985/2005 and Decree 1069/2014 with no text, and none of them contained a non-punishment clause in the period the dossier describes. A principle against criminalising victims was added to Law 985 art. 2 only by Ley 2608 of 27 July 2026, and it is interpretive. The 2025 US TIP report says victims were still penalised.
- **Qatar (E), correct.** Law 15/2011 art. 4 matches the dossier's narrow reading (AUC/Al Meezan English text).
- **Portugal (F), correct.** Portugal has no statutory non-punishment clause; GRETA urged one again in its third-round report in 2022.
- **Sweden (G), wrong.** Aliens Act ch. 5 §15 is a temporary permit for victims needed in an investigation, and says nothing about liability. Sweden has no statutory non-punishment rule, and GRETA(2023)14 calls the prosecutors' guidelines limited in scope. The only link is that unlawful stay under ch. 20 §1 is not punishable once a 5:15 permit has been applied for. G's "likely exempt" is speculation, and its "Effectively Always" implementation rating has no basis.
- **Russia (H), wrong.** Criminal Code art. 24 is about forms of guilt. The note to art. 127.1 exempts the *perpetrator* who frees a victim. Art. 322.1 has no note. The only exemption, in the note to art. 322, covers people seeking political asylum (zakonrf.info/uk/127.1, /uk/322).
- **India (I), wrong.** The dossier rests on Smuggling Protocol art. 5, read with Constitution art. 51(c) and *Vishaka*. India has no implementing law. The only protection is a 2012 Home Ministry advisory for women and child victims, and the Foreigners Act still penalises unlawful entry.
- **Costa Rica (J), correct.** Ley 9095 art. 70, "No punibilidad", is quoted accurately (SCIJ norm 74132). Irregular entry is administrative only.
- **Norway (K), correct.** Norway has no statutory clause. Criminal Procedure Act §69 is a general discretion not to prosecute, and GRETA's 2026 recommendation asks for a specific provision. K's implementation rating of "Sometimes" sits oddly with a "No" answer (see consistency).
- **China (L), correct.** It cites Exit-Entry Law art. 71, Criminal Law art. 322 and SPC/SPP Interpretation 2012-17, and there is no non-punishment clause.

**q17, ban on detaining migrant children**

- **Jordan (B), wrong.** The dossier relies on CRC art. 37(b), which allows child detention as a last resort and does not ban it. Jordan's Residence Law 24/1973 places no age limit on detention (WORLD Policy Analysis Center, 2022). The CRC was published in Gazette 4787 of 16 October 2006, as the dossier says.
- **Colombia (D), wrong.** Police Code (Law 1801/2016) art. 168 is "aprehensión con fin judicial", arrest for criminal offences, and says nothing about children or migrants. Decree 1067/2015 art. 2.2.1.13.3.2 allows holding a foreigner for 36 hours with no exception for children. Law 1098 and Ley 2136 contain no ban either.
- **Spain (C), partly correct.** "No" is defensible, because LOEX art. 62 bis 1.i lets children stay with detained parents. But C leaves out the key rule, LOEX art. 62.4: "No podrá acordarse el ingreso de menores en los centros de internamiento" (minors may not be placed in detention centres).
- **Sweden (G), partly correct.** The answer is right, but the sections are wrong. The rules on children are Aliens Act ch. 10 §4 (when a child may be detained), §8 (unaccompanied children, "synnerliga skäl", exceptional reasons only) and §13 (72 + 72 hours). G cites §§2, 3 and 5, which apply to adults.
- **Costa Rica (J), correct.** Decreto 36769-G art. 20 is verified: minors are excluded from migration apprehension and handed to PANI. J itself notes that Ley 8764 art. 31(5) has no statutory ban.
- **Norway (K), correct.** §106 c ("Særlige regler om pågripelse og internering av mindreårige") is verified: last resort, 72 hours at a time, 9 days in total.
- **Portugal (F), correct.** Lei 23/2007 art. 146-A is cited correctly. Lei n.º 62/2026 of 10 September 2026, which adds art. 35-C to Lei 27/2008, is real (files.diariodarepublica.pt/1s/2026/09/17600/0006000111.pdf), and the quotations from 35-C(3) and (6) match.
- **China (L), correct.** Exit-Entry Law arts. 61 and 63 are verified word for word (under-16s exempt from detention for investigation, 60-day limit, art. 63 custody).
- **Qatar (E), correct.** Law 21/2015 art. 28 (30 days, renewable once) is verified, with no exception for children.
- **Philippines (A), Russia (H) and India (I), correct.** I wrongly calls Foreigners Act s. 3(2)(g) an "Article".

**q41, criminal sanction for unauthorised entry**

- **Spain (C), Portugal (F) and Costa Rica (J), correct.** All three code "Yes".
  - Portugal: art. 181 defines illegal entry without a penalty, art. 192 fines overstays €80–€700, and art. 187 makes entry during a ban a crime (pgdlisboa.pt).
  - Costa Rica: Ley 8764 arts. 33, 183 and 249 are verified.
- **Colombia (D), partly correct.** "Yes" is right, since Decree 1067/2015 art. 2.2.1.13.1 and following treat irregular entry administratively. The citations do not support it: Decree 4062/2011 created the migration agency, and "Decree 2357 of 2020" is elsewhere in the same dossier, correctly, called *Resolution* 2357/2020.
- **Jordan (B), partly correct.** "No" is right. But the cited Penal Code "Art. 153 bis" could not be found. The offence is in Residence and Foreigners' Affairs Law 24/1973 art. 31, with one to six months' prison, a fine of JD 10–50, or both.
- **Russia (H), partly correct.** "No" is right under Criminal Code art. 322. H says the exception covers trafficking victims, but the note to art. 322 covers people seeking political asylum.
- **Sweden (G), correct.** Aliens Act ch. 20 §4 (Lag 2015:91) is verified: a fine or up to one year for crossing an external Schengen border in an unauthorised way.
- **Norway (K), correct.** Immigration Act §108(2) is verified: a fine or up to six months for breaching §§ 8 and 9.
- **Philippines (A), Qatar (E), India (I) and China (L), correct.** Qatar's Law 21/2015 art. 38 (up to three years, QAR 50,000) is verified.

**q57, chain refoulement**

- **Sweden (G), correct.** Aliens Act ch. 12 §§1–2 ("inte är skyddad … mot att sändas vidare", not protected against being sent on) is verified, though G gives only the section numbers.
- **Norway (K), correct.** §73 third paragraph is verified word for word.
- **Portugal (F), correct.** Lei 27/2008 art. 2(1)(aa) is verified: "expulsão ou repulsão, direta ou indireta" (expulsion or return, direct or indirect). F translates it as "expulsion or return", which is close enough.
- **The other nine, correct ("No").**
  - Costa Rica: Ley 8764 art. 31(9) ("a otro país, sea o no de origen", to another country, whether or not of origin) is verified, and no chain clause exists.
  - Qatar: Law 11/2018 art. 15 was checked through HRW only.
  - Jordan (B): the answer is correct but has no citation.
  - Russia (H): it cites FSB Order 631, which does not deal with chain refoulement at all.

**q71, dual nationality**

- **Russia (H), correct.** This looked doubtful but checks out. Federal Law 134-FZ of 24 April 2020 repealed the duty to renounce (art. 13(1)(г) of 62-FZ), and 138-FZ (2023) art. 15 has no renunciation requirement (rulaws.ru; zakonrf.info).
- **Qatar (E), correct.** It codes "Conditional". Law 38/2005 art. 18 bans other nationalities except by Emiri decision, and art. 6 allows a small discretionary group to keep their nationality "notwithstanding Articles 2 and 18". The "some other consideration" option fits.
- **Jordan (B), correct.** Nationality Law art. 13(3) is verified (it is numbered 12(3) in the UNHCR translation).
- **Costa Rica (J), correct.** It codes "Conditional". Ley 1155 art. 11 is verified, and Sala Constitucional vote 05270-2011 upheld it.
- **Spain (C), correct.** It codes "Conditional". The renunciation rule and its exception are in Civil Code art. 23(b), which refers to the country list in art. 24.1; C cites only 24.1. The Spain–France treaty has been in force since 1 April 2022 (BOE-A-2022-4916).
- **Colombia (D), correct.** The real basis is Constitution art. 96 ("no estarán obligados a renunciar", not obliged to renounce); D cites only Law 43/1993 art. 14.
- **Portugal (F), correct.** No renunciation is required, and Lei Orgânica 1/2026 (in force 19 May 2026) is verified.
- **Sweden (G), correct but citation thin.** Lag 2001:82 is cited with no section.
- **India (I), correct but no citation.** The basis is Citizenship Act s. 6 and Third Schedule clause (b).
- **Philippines (A), Norway (K) and China (L), correct.** China's Nationality Law arts. 3 and 8 are verified.

**q99, spouse's right to work**

- **Spain (C), correct.** LOEX art. 19.1 is verified: "habilitará para trabajar sin necesidad de ningún otro trámite" (authorises work with no further procedure). C writes "Royal Decree 55/2011", a typo for 557/2011.
- **Portugal (F), correct.** Lei 23/2007 arts. 83(1) and 107 are verified.
- **Norway (K), correct.** Immigration Act §60 is cited correctly.
- **Sweden (G), partly correct.** The family member's permit comes from Aliens Act ch. 6 §3, and the tie to one employer and type of work in §2 a applies only to §2 permits. So "limited by sector or occupation" is not supported. The spouse still applies for the permit, so neither "unrestricted" nor "limited" fits cleanly.
- **Colombia (D), correct.** Res. 5477/2022 art. 93 paragraph 2 is verified: "La visa en calidad de beneficiario no permite trabajar" (a beneficiary visa does not permit work).
- **Philippines (A), correct.** DOLE DO 248 s. 2025 is verified, with spouses of 9(g) visa holders not on the exemption list.
- **China (L), correct.** The 1996 Employment Provisions art. 8 is verified. The text says "employment visa"; the 2017 amendment changed only the first paragraph to "Z visa".
- **Qatar (E) and Costa Rica (J), correct.**
- **Jordan (B), Russia (H) and India (I), correct but uncited.**

**q125, free primary education**

- **Spain (C), Sweden (G), Portugal (F), Costa Rica (J) and Norway (K), correct.** All code "Yes (including irregular migrants)".
  - Spain: LOEX art. 9.
  - Sweden: Education Act ch. 29 §2.
  - Portugal: DL 67/2004.
  - Costa Rica: Decreto 40529-MEP art. 5.
  - Norway: opplæringslova 2023 §2-1.
- **China (L), correct ("No").** Compulsory Education Law art. 4 ("凡具有中华人民共和国国籍的", all with PRC nationality) is verified.
- **Qatar (E), correct ("certain statuses").** Law 25/2001 art. 2 is verified. Ministry admission rules for 2026–27 limit government schools to set groups of non-Qataris.
- **Jordan (B), partly correct.** The Education Law is No. 3 of 1994, not "No. 47". Art. 10(a) is quoted correctly, but the status restriction rests on administrative practice, which B does not cite.
- **India (I), partly correct.** RTE Act s. 3(1) gives the right to "every child", with no status condition (indiacode.nic.in). The claim that "this right does not apply to irregular migrants" has no legal source. On the statute alone, a higher option is arguable.
- **Colombia (D), correct.** "Law 1075 of 2015" is in fact Decree 1075/2015.
- **Russia (H), correct for its coding period.** 273-FZ art. 78(2) is quoted correctly. The 2025 language-test and legal-stay requirements came later.
- **Philippines (A), correct.**

**q137, citizenship for those who would otherwise be stateless**

- **Sweden (G), wrong.** Citizenship Act §6 is verified: "Ett barn som har fötts i Sverige och som sedan födelsen är statslöst förvärvar svenskt medborgarskap efter anmälan …" (a child born in Sweden and stateless since birth acquires Swedish citizenship on notification). Conditions are permanent residence and domicile, with the permanent-residence condition waived after five years. That is an entitlement by notification. G does not mention §6 and says citizenship is "not guaranteed". K codes Norway's weaker, application-based route as "Yes", so G's "No" also contradicts the standard the stronger dossiers apply.
- **Russia (H), partly correct.** H's own citation quotes the safeguard in 62-FZ art. 12(1)(г): a child born in Russia to foreign or stateless parents residing there acquires citizenship if the parents' state does not grant it. 138-FZ art. 13(1)(5) keeps it in a changed form. The "No" answer contradicts the dossier's own text.
- **Colombia (D), partly correct.** D cites only the stateless-person visa. It leaves out Ley 1997/2019 and the Registraduría's "Primero la Niñez" measures for children of Venezuelans, even though it cites Res. 8617/2021 itself under q29. Ley 2332/2023 art. 4 paragraph 2 later created a general safeguard, which may postdate the coding.
- **Spain (C), Portugal (F) and Costa Rica (J), correct.**
  - Spain: Civil Code art. 17.1(c).
  - Portugal: Lei 37/81 art. 1(1)(g), verified.
  - Costa Rica: Constitution art. 13(3), verified.
- **Norway (K), correct ("Yes").** §16 reduces the residence requirement for stateless applicants, which makes this an application-based entitlement; K states its limits openly.
- **Philippines (A), Jordan (B), Qatar (E), India (I) and China (L), correct ("No").**
  - Philippines: RA 11767 §5, verified.
  - Jordan: art. 3(4)–(5), verified.
  - Qatar: art. 2 foundling rule, verified.
  - China: art. 6, verified; it is narrow because the stateless parents must have settled in China.

## 5. Patterns across the dossiers

- **Two styles of dossier.** Six dossiers (A, E, F, J, K, L) are uniform in length (about 1,300–2,000 characters per indicator). Each one names the instrument, quotes the article, states which migrants are covered and closes with the limits. All 48 of their verified items were correct. The other six (B, C, D, G, H, I) vary much more in length and depth, and 11 of their 48 verified items were wrong or only partly correct. Every wrong verdict in this review comes from the second group.
- **Errors in the second group take three repeated forms:**
  - Citing a law that exists but does not say what is claimed: D q13 and q17, H q13, G q13, B q17.
  - Treating a ratified treaty or general constitutional principle as a domestic rule: B q17 and q119, I q3, q13 and q25.
  - Answers that contradict their own citation: C q73, q95 and q97, D q91, H q79 and q137, B q23.
- **Implementation ratings are the weakest part almost everywhere.**
  - Two dossiers rate most items "Effectively Always" (C 38 of 53, G 40 of 52) with no reference to practice.
  - The six detailed dossiers mostly choose "Usually" or "Sometimes", and A, E, L and F cite practice or reports (TIP, GRETA, ministry guidance) far more often.
  - No dossier consistently backs its rating with evidence about practice. The instrument asks for the rating but not its basis, and it shows.
- **The instrument's wording leaves room for different readings, and the dossiers follow different conventions.**
  - q91 and q93 are coded "No" in all twelve, including Sweden, Norway and Colombia, where any foreign resident gets the local vote after a residence period, and where the dossiers' own citations say so. A plain reading of "right to vote in local … elections (50)" would give 50 there. Some dossiers read "being lawfully present" as requiring the right to come from presence alone.
  - q13 (trafficked *and* smuggled) is read strictly by A, E and F (lowest option unless both groups are exempt) and generously by C and J (yes if trafficking victims are exempt and irregular entry is not a crime).
  - q137 is read as a guarantee at birth by some and as an application-based entitlement by K, which is why G's "No" for Sweden conflicts with K's "Yes" for Norway.
  - These conflicts are in the instrument's wording, not mistakes by individual coders. Written rules for coding them would remove most of the disagreement.
- **Some answers use options that are not in the instrument**: q27 "Yes, right implied by existing laws" or "… explicitly specified" (B, C, H, I), q21 "Yes (legality)" (B), q115 "NA" or "Does not apply" (B, I), and annotated variants of q35 and q21 (D, G).
- **Reasoning by analogy to other countries' codings.** Several detailed dossiers justify borderline calls by pointing to how the MRD coded Argentina, Mexico or Jordan (A q13, E q13 and q137, J q85 and q87). That keeps codings consistent across countries, but it can carry a contestable call from one country into another.

## 6. Method and limits

- **How the checks were done.** I checked 96 items (12 dossiers × 8 indicators) against primary law wherever I could reach it: riksdagen.se, lovdata.no, zakonrf.info and rulaws.ru, pgdlisboa.pt and diariodarepublica.pt, SCIJ (pgrweb.go.cr), cancilleria.gov.co, boe.es, lawphil.net, indiacode.nic.in, the Al Meezan translations, Chinese statutes via wikisource and immd.gov.hk, and Jordanian texts via mohamah.net and jordan-lawyer.com. Where I could not reach the primary text, I used reliable secondary sources: GRETA reports and recommendations, US TIP reports, HRW, UN treaty records and the WORLD Policy Analysis Center.
- **Coding dates.** Laws that changed after a dossier's apparent coding date were not held against it:
  - Russia 2025 school rules (H q125).
  - India's Immigration and Foreigners Act 2025, which repealed the Foreigners Act and the Passport (Entry into India) Act (I q13, q41).
  - Colombia's Ley 2332/2023 and Ley 2608/2026 (D q137, q13).
- **What is less certain.** Two items rest on secondary sources (Qatar Law 11/2018 art. 15 via HRW; Russia 62-FZ art. 12(1)(г), whose verbatim text I could not retrieve, though its 138-FZ successor was verified). The verdicts I am least sure of are G q99 (Sweden spouse permit) and C q17 (Spain child detention). They turn on how the instrument's options are read, not on what the law says.
- **What I did not read.** No other repository files, databases, migrantrightsinitiative.org or ola.cx were consulted.
