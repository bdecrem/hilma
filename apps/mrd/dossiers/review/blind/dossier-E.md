# Dossier E — Qatar


## Civil and political life

### q91 · Right of regular migrants to vote in local and/or regional elections.
- Law: No right to vote in any election
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. The Permanent Constitution of the State of Qatar (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر, 2004, as amended in 2024) reserves the franchise to nationals. Article 42 provides that 'The State shall ensure the right of citizens to elect and be elected, in accordance with the law.' National elections no longer exist: after the 2024 constitutional amendments, Article 77 provides that the Shura Council 'shall consist of no fewer than forty-five (45) Members, and the appointment of the Members shall be issued by an Amiri Decision.' Articles 78 and 79, which provided for elected members, were repealed. The only remaining popular vote is for the Central Municipal Council under Law No. 12 of 1998 regulating the Central Municipal Council (قانون رقم (12) لسنة 1998 بتنظيم المجلس البلدي المركزي). Article 4, as replaced by Law No. 1 of 2011, leaves voter qualifications to the electoral law. Pending a new law, that law is Decree No. 17 of 1998 on the system for electing members of the Central Municipal Council (مرسوم رقم (17) لسنة 1998 بنظام انتخاب أعضاء المجلس البلدي المركزي). Its Article 1 gives the right to vote to 'every Qatari man and woman' who meets set conditions. The Interior Ministry's published voter conditions require original Qatari nationality, or 15 years since naturalisation. Lawfully resident migrants, including permanent residents under Law No. 10 of 2018, therefore have no right to vote in national, municipal or any other election. Non-discrimination guarantees (Constitution art. 35) have not been applied to extend the franchise.

### q93 · Right of regular migrants to stand for election.
- Law: No right to run for public office in any election
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Article 42 of the Permanent Constitution of the State of Qatar (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر, 2004, as amended in 2024) guarantees the right 'to elect and be elected' only to 'citizens.' At national level there is no longer any elected office. Amended Article 77 provides that Shura Council members are appointed 'by an Amiri Decision.' Amended Article 80(1) requires every member 'to be a holder of a Qatari nationality.' At municipal level, Article 5 of Law No. 12 of 1998 regulating the Central Municipal Council (قانون رقم (12) لسنة 1998 بتنظيم المجلس البلدي المركزي), as replaced by Law No. 1 of 2011, sets the candidate conditions. Condition 1 is 'that his nationality be Qatari, and for one who acquired Qatari nationality, that his father be born in Qatar.' Condition 6 is that he 'be registered in the voters' lists in the district in which he stands.' Those lists are themselves open only to Qataris. No law lets a lawfully present non-citizen stand for national, municipal or other elected office. That covers temporary workers, long-term residents and permanent-residence card holders under Law No. 10 of 2018. Even naturalised Qataris face extra conditions.


## Due process

### q35 · Right to equal protection of the law before courts.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Qatar protects this right in the Permanent Constitution of the State of Qatar (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر, 2004, as amended in 2024), Articles 35, 39 and 135. Article 35 provides that "All persons shall be equal before the law and there shall be no discrimination of whatsoever on grounds of sex, race, language, or religion." Article 135 provides that "The right of litigation is inviolable and it shall be guaranteed to all people. The law shall specify the procedures and manner of exercising such a right." Article 39 guarantees to any accused person the presumption of innocence and a trial "before a court of law wherein the necessary guarantees of the right of self-defense are secured." These clauses are written for "all persons" and "all people". The citizens-only clauses are worded differently: Article 34 reserves equality in "public rights and duties" to citizens. The equality and litigation guarantees therefore cover migrants regardless of status, before civil, criminal and administrative courts. The Code of Criminal Procedure (Law No. 23 of 2004) gives procedural rights to "the defendant" and "the victim" without any nationality condition. The limits come from elsewhere. Immigration matters, including deportation, are largely decided administratively by the Ministry of Interior under Law No. 21 of 2015 rather than by courts. Labour claims of most migrant workers go to the Labour Dispute Settlement Committees. Article 52 limits protection of person and property to "legal residents".

### q37 · Right to government-provided counsel in proceedings related to migrant status.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم, as amended by Law No. 13 of 2018 and Decree-Law No. 19 of 2020) governs visas, residence permits, transfer of employment and deportation (arts. 24-28). It provides no right to a lawyer, still less one paid by the State, in proceedings on a migrant's status. Cancellation of residence, absconding reports and deportation are handled administratively by the Ministry of Interior, not through a court procedure with assigned counsel. The constitutional right of litigation (Constitution art. 135) and the guarantee of self-defence for an accused person (art. 39) concern court proceedings and do not create state-funded representation in immigration matters. The Code of Criminal Procedure (Law No. 23 of 2004) refers to the defendant's "attorney-at-law" in criminal cases, and government-funded counsel for indigent criminal defendants is reported in practice. Neither applies to migration-status proceedings. The nearest specific provision is Article 6(5) of Law No. 15 of 2011 on combating human trafficking, which gives identified trafficking victims the right to "legal aid including counseling of attorney." That right attaches to their status as crime victims, not to a general right to counsel in residence or deportation proceedings, and it reaches only one small group of migrants.

### q39 · Right to government-provided interpretation in criminal proceedings.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Qatar protects this right in the Code of Criminal Procedure promulgated by Law No. 23 of 2004 (قانون رقم (23) لسنة 2004 بإصدار قانون الإجراءات الجنائية), Article 72. It provides that "The investigation is carried out in Arabic; the member of the Public Prosecution seeks the assistance of interpreters while hearing the statements of litigants or witnesses who do not understand Arabic. The interpreter takes an oath to accomplish his duties honestly and sincerely." Where documents are in another language, "the member of the Public Prosecution designates a translator" under the same oath. The interpreter is appointed by the State (the Public Prosecution), and no fee is placed on the accused or the witness, so interpretation is provided as part of the State's conduct of the proceedings. The duty applies to any "litigant" or witness who does not understand Arabic, whatever their nationality or immigration status, so it covers migrants generally, including those in irregular status. The Constitution reinforces it: Article 35 bars discrimination on grounds of language, and Article 39 requires that an accused be tried with "the necessary guarantees of the right of self-defense." The provision I read governs the prosecution's investigation stage. The trial stage, where interpretation is reported to be provided in courtrooms, is also covered by the general procedural rules. However, the Code does not state in so many words that interpretation is free of charge.

### q41 · Right to be protected from prosecution for unauthorized entry.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم) makes unauthorised entry a crime. Article 2 provides that "an expatriate may not enter or leave the State unless he holds a valid passport or travel document and has obtained an entry visa from the competent authority stating the purpose of entry." Article 3 adds that entry and exit are allowed only through the ports designated by the Minister, with an entry or exit stamp. Article 38 provides that, "without prejudice to any more severe penalty provided by another law, anyone who violates any of the provisions of Articles 2 (first paragraph), 3 ... shall be punished by imprisonment for a period not exceeding three years and a fine not exceeding 50,000 riyals, or by one of these two penalties." Repeat offences carry 30 days to three years and fines of QAR 20,000 to 100,000. The only exemption from criminal sanction is narrow. Article 4 of Law No. 15 of 2011 on combating human trafficking provides that a victim "shall not be subject to criminal or civil liability" for trafficking-related crimes "initiated or directly associated with such person as being a victim". This covers only identified trafficking victims. There is no general exemption for migrants, asylum seekers or refugees. Qatar is not party to the 1951 Refugee Convention, so its Article 31 non-penalisation rule has no domestic effect.


## Education

### q127 · Right to equal access to public adult education institutions.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. The Permanent Constitution guarantees the right to education only to "all citizens" (art. 49). Article 25 makes education a basic pillar of social progress for the State to foster, but grants no individual right to non-nationals. No statute gives migrants access to public higher education or vocational training on the same terms as nationals. On the contrary, Law No. 15 of 1999 (قانون رقم (15) لسنة 1999 بشأن تحصيل رسوم الدراسة والسكن من الطلاب غير القطريين بجامعة قطر) makes non-Qatari students at Qatar University, the main public university, pay tuition per credit hour and accommodation fees. Qataris study free. Only GCC nationals, scholarship holders and one child per university staff member are exempt (art. 1), and current fees are set by university decision (QR 1,200-1,400 per credit hour for non-Qatari undergraduates from 2023). Admission is also ordered by nationality. The university first admits every Qatari applicant who meets the requirements and only then lets non-Qataris compete for the remaining seats, so many qualified resident non-Qataris are refused. As in the Jordanian example, migrants may enrol in public adult education but face lower priority and higher fees than citizens. That is not access commensurate with citizens, and irregular migrants have no route at all.


## Equal protection

### q131 · Right to be free from discrimination on the basis of migrant status.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. The Permanent Constitution (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر, 2004, as amended in 2024), Article 35, provides that "All persons shall be equal before the law and there shall be no discrimination of whatsoever on grounds of sex, race, language, or religion." The list of grounds is closed and does not include nationality, migration or residence status. Article 34 confines equality in "public rights and duties" to citizens. Article 52 extends protection of person and property only to a person "who is a legal resident of the State", a distinction drawn on immigration status itself. Ordinary legislation builds in the citizen/non-citizen distinction rather than prohibiting it. The Labour Law (قانون رقم (14) لسنة 2004 بإصدار قانون العمل, Law No. 14 of 2004), Article 18, provides that "Qatari Workers shall be given priority in employment; only in cases of necessity should non-Qatari Workers be employed", and Article 116 limits union membership to Qatari workers. No general anti-discrimination statute exists, and no labour or immigration provision prohibits discrimination against a person because they are a migrant. The non-discriminatory minimum wage in Law No. 17 of 2020 covers one entitlement only and is not a general ban on migrant-status discrimination. Qatar relies on CERD Article 1(2), which excludes distinctions between citizens and non-citizens, so its CERD obligations do not supply the missing ground.


## Expulsion, asylum, and non-refoulement

### q47 · Right to maintain immigration status after convictions.
- Law: any conviction (even minor administrative offenses) can lead to loss of residence status
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. National law lets any criminal conviction, and also conduct that has led to no conviction, end a migrant's residence. Under the Penal Code (قانون العقوبات الصادر بالقانون رقم (11) لسنة 2004), deporting a foreigner is a secondary and complementary penalty (art. 65(7)). Article 77 lets a court that imposes a custodial sentence for any felony or misdemeanour (جنحة) order the foreigner's deportation once the sentence is served. Deportation is mandatory where the offence concerns honour or trust. Article 78 lets the court, in a misdemeanour case, order deportation instead of the penalty itself. Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم) goes further. Article 25 provides that, "as an exception to the provisions of any other law, the Minister may issue a repatriation order for any Foreign National whose presence is proven to imperil the safety or security of the state ... or harm the national economy, public health, or public morals". No conviction is needed. Article 37 revokes the residence permit on the same grounds, or when the purpose of stay is breached. Article 26 bars a person deported by court ruling from returning unless the Minister decides otherwise. It also imposes a four-year re-entry ban on workers dismissed on disciplinary grounds who do not challenge the dismissal successfully. Breaches of the residence law itself, such as overstaying (arts. 24, 38), are offences punishable by imprisonment. They can therefore become the basis for judicial deportation. The law requires no proportionality test and does not weigh length of residence or family ties. It makes no exception for long-term residents or for minor offences. These powers apply to all non-Qataris, whatever their permit class.

### q49 · Right to not return to a risk of torture or inhumane treatment.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Qatar protects this right in the Permanent Constitution (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر), arts. 58 and 68, and Law No. 11 of 2018 regulating political asylum (قانون رقم (11) لسنة 2018 بتنظيم اللجوء السياسي), arts. 1 and 15. Article 58 of the Constitution provides that "extradition of political refugees shall be prohibited". Law 11/2018 defines a political refugee in art. 1 as a person outside his country who cannot or will not return "because of a well-founded fear of exposure to the risk of a death sentence or corporal punishment, or torture, or brutal or degrading treatment, or persecution", on account of race, religion, membership of a particular social group or political opinion. Article 15 prohibits returning the refugee "to his state, or to any other state where he fears exposure to danger or persecution". The ban is stated without exceptions. Qatar also acceded to the Convention against Torture in 2000. Under Constitution art. 68, a ratified and published treaty "shall have the power of law", which gives CAT art. 3's absolute non-refoulement obligation the rank of statute. The protection has limits. The statutory ban covers recognised political refugees and those within the asylum procedure. The torture ground is tied to the five-ground nexus in art. 1, so a person facing torture for other reasons relies only on CAT art. 3 as domestic law. No provision of Law 21/2015 requires a risk assessment before administrative deportation under art. 25. Article 11 of the asylum law lets the Minister of Interior deport refugees who engage in political activity to a country of their choice. Asylum is also excluded for serious non-political crimes and war crimes (art. 3).

### q51 · Right to not return to a risk of persecution.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes; Usually

National law in Qatar protects this right in the Permanent Constitution (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر), art. 58, and Law No. 11 of 2018 regulating political asylum (قانون رقم (11) لسنة 2018 بتنظيم اللجوء السياسي), arts. 1 and 15. Article 58 of the Constitution states that "extradition of political refugees shall be prohibited; and the law shall specify conditions of granting political asylum". Law 11/2018 was the first asylum statute in the Gulf. Article 1 defines a political refugee as a person outside his country of nationality, or habitual residence if stateless, who cannot or will not return owing to a well-founded fear of persecution, among other harms, "on account of his race, religion, membership of a particular social group or political opinions". Article 15 forbids returning the refugee "to his state, or to any other state where he fears exposure to danger or persecution". It sets out no security or criminality exception of the kind found in art. 33(2) of the 1951 Convention. The protection has limits. Nationality is not listed as a persecution ground, so the ban covers four of the five Convention grounds. It protects recognised political refugees, and arguably applicants in the procedure; there is no general rule for irregular migrants facing administrative deportation under art. 25 of Law 21/2015. Article 3 excludes from asylum persons who committed serious non-political crimes, war crimes or crimes against humanity, or acts contrary to UN purposes, and dual nationals protected by another state. Article 11 lets the Minister of Interior deport a refugee who engages in political activity to a country of the refugee's choosing, which keeps the return ban in place. Applicants may be given temporary residence while their claim is decided (art. 6). A rejection can be challenged only before the Prime Minister, not a court (art. 8).

### q53 · Right to not return to a deprivations of fundamental human rights.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Qatar protects this right, with limits, in Law No. 11 of 2018 regulating political asylum (قانون رقم (11) لسنة 2018 بتنظيم اللجوء السياسي), arts. 1 and 15, and in the Permanent Constitution (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر), arts. 58 and 68. Article 1 of Law 11/2018 extends refugee protection to a person with a well-founded fear of "the risk of a death sentence or corporal punishment, or torture, or brutal or degrading treatment, or persecution". That expressly reaches threats to the right to life and physical integrity, beyond persecution as such. Article 15 forbids returning a refugee "to his state, or to any other state where he fears exposure to danger (الخطر) or persecution". The word 'danger' is broader than persecution, and a Qatar University study reads art. 15 as covering anyone whose life, liberty or safety would be endangered. Constitution art. 58 bans the extradition of political refugees. Under art. 68, ratified and published treaties "shall have the power of law". That brings in ICCPR arts. 6 and 7 (acceded 2018) and CAT art. 3, subject to Qatar's Sharia-based reservations. The protection has real limits. The art. 1 definition ties each harm, including the death penalty risk, to race, religion, social group or political opinion. A person facing generalised violence or a non-political threat to life is not clearly covered by the statute. The ban protects recognised political refugees, and arguably applicants. Administrative deportation under art. 25 of Law 21/2015 contains no requirement to assess risks to life on return. Exclusion grounds in art. 3 and the deportation power for political activity in art. 11 narrow the scope further.

### q55 · Right to be advised of availability of asylum before expulsion.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم) governs refusal of entry, departure and removal (Chapter 6, arts. 24-28). It says an expatriate without a valid residence permit "must leave the country" (art. 24), and that the Minister may order removal (art. 25), detain a person pending removal (art. 28) or grant up to 90 days to settle affairs (art. 27). None of these provisions, nor any provision on entry at the border (arts. 2-7), requires officials to tell the person that asylum may be available. Law No. 11 of 2018 regulating political asylum (قانون رقم (11) لسنة 2018 بتنظيم اللجوء السياسي) sets up a petition procedure: a Committee for Political Asylee Affairs, an Interior Minister's decision within three months and an appeal to the Prime Minister (arts. 6-8, as summarised by Human Rights Watch). It does not, as far as could be established, require that people facing removal or refusal of entry be informed of that procedure. The UN Committee against Torture asked Qatar in 2018 to "guarantee that all persons on the territory or under the jurisdiction of the State party have effective access to the procedure for determining refugee status". That request confirms the gap; it does not fill it. The right is therefore not provided for any category of migrant, including those refused entry at Hamad International Airport or removed from inside the country.

### q57 · Right to be protected from chain refoulement.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Law No. 11 of 2018 regulating political asylum (قانون رقم (11) لسنة 2018 بتنظيم اللجوء السياسي) contains the only statutory non-refoulement clause. According to Human Rights Watch, art. 15 prohibits returning a political refugee "to his country of origin or to another country in which he fears he will be in danger or subject to persecution". The clause protects recognised political refugees, and possibly asylum seekers, against direct return to a dangerous country. It does not ban expulsion to a safe third country that is likely to send the person onward. It also requires no assurance of protection against further expulsion or extradition. The same law (art. 11) lets the Interior Minister deport asylum seekers and refugees who engage in political activity to another country, with no stated safeguard against onward removal. Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم) allows the Minister, "as an exception to the provisions of any other law", to order removal of any expatriate (art. 25), with no non-refoulement limit at all. Migrants outside the asylum law, including irregular migrants and workers whose residence is cancelled, therefore have no statutory protection against direct or chain refoulement. No court decision applying CAT art. 3 to onward removal was found.

### q59 · Right to protection against expulsion except following a legal judgment.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم) gives the executive a broad removal power that overrides other law. Art. 25 provides that "as an exception to the provisions of any other law, the Minister may issue a repatriation order for any Foreign National whose presence is proven to imperil the safety or security of the state within the country or abroad, or harm the national economy, public health, or public morals". The law sets no procedure for reaching such a decision, no duty to give reasons and no standard of proof. Art. 28 lets the Minister detain the person for 30 days, renewable, pending removal. Art. 24 also obliges any expatriate whose residence permit expires or is cancelled to leave within 90 days. Because the employer-recruiter controls the permit, this status-based removal can follow from an employer's decision. Law No. 7 of 2007 on the settlement of administrative disputes (قانون رقم (7) لسنة 2007 بشأن الفصل في المنازعات الإدارية, as amended by Law 12/2013), art. 3(3)(f), excludes from the administrative courts "decisions issued under the laws on the entry, residence and deportation of foreigners". Nobody can therefore test whether a removal order is justified by individual facts or lawfully made. Only expulsion ordered by a criminal court as part of a sentence (Law 21/2015, art. 26) comes with judicial safeguards. Like the MRD's Jordan coding, this wide, unreviewable ministerial discretion does not meet the standard for any category of migrant.

### q61 · Right to submit reasons against expulsion and have case reviewed before deportation.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم) lets the Minister issue a removal order "as an exception to the provisions of any other law" (art. 25) and detain the person pending removal (art. 28). It does not give the person any opportunity to be heard, to submit reasons against expulsion or to have the decision reviewed. The only accommodation is discretionary: the Minister "may afford" up to 90 days to settle personal affairs against a security (art. 27). Review by a court is barred. Law No. 7 of 2007 on the settlement of administrative disputes (قانون رقم (7) لسنة 2007 بشأن الفصل في المنازعات الإدارية, as amended by Law 12/2013), art. 3(3)(f), excludes from the administrative circuit's jurisdiction "decisions issued under the laws on the entry, residence and deportation of foreigners". Under Law No. 11 of 2018 regulating political asylum (قانون رقم (11) لسنة 2018 بتنظيم اللجوء السياسي), art. 8, rejected asylum applicants may appeal only to the Prime Minister, not to a court (per Human Rights Watch). That remedy concerns asylum status, not expulsion. Foreigners sentenced to expulsion by a criminal court can contest it through the criminal trial and appeal, but administrative removal, the main route for migrant workers, irregular migrants and those whose residence is cancelled, carries no right to submit reasons or obtain review.


## Family

### q73 · Right of settled migrants to family reunification.
- Law: Conditional
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Qatar conditionally protects this right in Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم), Article 12, and in Law No. 11 of 2018 regulating political asylum (قانون رقم (11) لسنة 2018 بتنظيم اللجوء السياسي), Article 9. Under Article 12, the competent authority "may issue Residency Permits to the spouse of the Foreign National who has received a Residency Permit, and to his male children who have not completed university-level studies and are under 25 years old, and to his female children who are not married." The Minister may make exceptions, and parents may receive permits "if there is reason to do so" with the Minister's authorisation. The conditions are left to a ministerial decision. Article 13 adds a 90-day deadline for registering newborns, and Article 33 lets self-sponsored residents (investors, property owners) sponsor their spouse, children and parents. The avenue is discretionary, not a right. The Ministry of Interior's December 2023 rules attach it to occupation and income. Private-sector sponsors must hold a "technical or specialized occupation" and earn at least QAR 10,000 a month, or QAR 6,000 with family housing stated in the contract. Government-sector sponsors need employer housing or QAR 10,000. In practice this excludes most low-wage workers in labour occupations. Non-dependent relatives such as siblings, adult sons and married daughters have no residence route, only visit visas. Recognised political refugees "may also be permitted to bring along their spouse and first degree family members" (Law 11/2018, Art. 9). The right therefore exists only for some migrants and on conditions, so the answer is Conditional.

### q75 · Right to judicial remedy for denials of right to family reunification.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Law No. 7 of 2007 on the settlement of administrative disputes (قانون رقم (7) لسنة 2007 بشأن الفصل في المنازعات الإدارية), Article 3 as amended by Law No. 12 of 2013, gives the administrative circuit of the Court of First Instance jurisdiction over applications to annul final administrative decisions. It expressly excludes "decisions issued under the laws on the entry, residence and deportation of foreigners" (Art. 3(3)(f)). Refusing a family residence permit under Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015), Article 12, is exactly such a decision. Article 12 is itself framed as a discretion: the authority "may issue" permits, with conditions set by ministerial decision. Neither Law 21/2015 nor the 2023 Ministry of Interior rules create an appeal body or a judicial remedy against a refusal of family residence. A refused applicant can only reapply or petition the Ministry administratively. No court has been found reviewing such refusals on constitutional grounds; Article 35 of the Constitution guarantees equality before the law. Sponsored, self-sponsored and Qatari-sponsored family cases all fall under the same exclusion, so no category of migrant has a judicial remedy against a refusal of family formation or reunification.

### q77 · Right to recognition of same-sex marital relationships.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. The family reunification provision, Article 12 of Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم), refers only to the "spouse" of a residence-permit holder and does not define the term. Qatari family law defines marriage exclusively as a union of a man and a woman. Law No. 22 of 2006 promulgating the Family Law (قانون رقم (22) لسنة 2006 بإصدار قانون الأسرة), Article 9, provides: "Marriage is a legitimate contract between a man and woman on the basis of sustainability, and its aim is cohabitation and securing chastity." Article 4 applies non-Muslims' own provisions to their family matters, but that is subject to public order and does not open recognition of same-sex unions. No statute, regulation or court decision recognises same-sex marriages or partnerships concluded abroad for any purpose, including residence. The Penal Code also criminalises consensual same-sex sexual conduct, so recognising such a relationship for family residence would conflict with public order. No category of migrant, including refugees under Law No. 11 of 2018, can sponsor a same-sex spouse or partner.

### q79 · Right to family reunification without resource conditions
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Article 12 of Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم) only lets the authority grant family residence permits ("may issue"), and it leaves "the conditions of issuing Residency Permits" to a decision of the Minister. Those conditions are financial. Under the Ministry of Interior rules announced in December 2023, private-sector sponsors must hold a technical or specialised occupation "with a salary not less than QR 10,000, or QR 6,000 along with family housing verified in the employment contract." Government and semi-government employees must have employer-provided family accommodation or a salary of at least QAR 10,000. Family members also need health insurance cover; Law No. 22 of 2021 makes basic health insurance compulsory for expatriates, paid by the recruiter or head of family, and links residence permits to it. School-age children must be enrolled in licensed schools before a permit is issued or renewed. Family residence therefore depends on income, housing and insurance. The only route with no stated resource test is for recognised political refugees, who may bring a spouse and first-degree relatives under Law No. 11 of 2018 regulating political asylum (قانون رقم (11) لسنة 2018), Article 9. That covers a very small group and does not change the answer for migrants generally.

### q137 · Right to citizenship for stateless migrants.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Law No. 38 of 2005 on Qatari Nationality (قانون رقم (38) لسنة 2005 بشأن الجنسية القطرية) contains no general guarantee of nationality for stateless migrants or for children who would otherwise be stateless. Nationality by origin passes only through the father: Article 1(4) covers 'any person born in Qatar or in a foreign country to a Qatari father'. A child of a Qatari mother and a stateless or foreign father therefore acquires no Qatari nationality at birth. Article 2 gives such applicants only 'priority' in discretionary naturalisation after 25 years of residence. The one safeguard is for foundlings. Article 2 provides that 'those born in Qatar to unknown parents shall also be deemed to be a naturalised Qatari. Foundlings shall be considered as born in Qatar unless proven otherwise.' This protects abandoned children of unknown parentage. It does not reach children born in Qatar to known stateless parents (including the Bidoon) or stateless adult migrants. They can only seek ordinary naturalisation under Article 2, at the Emir's discretion, under the same strict conditions as other foreigners. Statelessness is not recognised as a ground for facilitated naturalisation. As in the MRD's Jordan coding, the foundling rule alone does not amount to a guarantee of citizenship for persons who would otherwise be stateless.


## Health

### q119 · Right of access to health care services.
- Law: Yes, but only for certain visa types and migrant statuses (e.g., economic, family, humanitarian, temporary or regular migrants)
- Implementation: Sometimes; Usually

National law in Qatar protects this right, for lawfully resident and visiting migrants only, in Law No. 22 of 2021 Regulating Health Care Services within the State (قانون رقم (22) لسنة 2021 بتنظيم خدمات الرعاية الصحية داخل الدولة) and its Executive Regulations, issued by Minister of Public Health Decision No. 8 of 2022 (قرار وزير الصحة العامة رقم (8) لسنة 2022), and in Law No. 10 of 2018 on Permanent Residence (قانون رقم (10) لسنة 2018 بشأن الإقامة الدائمة), Article 6. The 2021 Law sets up two tracks. Health care in government facilities is free for Qatari citizens and for categories the Council of Ministers designates. Every non-citizen resident or visitor must hold compulsory health insurance covering at least a package of basic health services, which includes emergency and accident treatment, maternity care, in-patient care, diagnostics and medicines. Employers must pay the premium for their non-Qatari workers and for family members residing in the country. Recruiters must cover the people they recruit and give them health care before the residence permit is issued (Executive Regulations, Article 26). Lawful residents may also obtain a fee-based public Health Card (Law, Chapter 3 and Article 6). Under Article 5 of the Executive Regulations, non-Qataris without a Health Card pay full charges at government facilities, except children up to one year old. Providers must give emergency care without first demanding payment, and Article 29 of the Law fines a provider that fails to provide emergency services. Permanent-residence card holders 'may be entitled to treatment and education in governmental institutions' on conditions set by the Council of Ministers (Law 10/2018, Article 6); fewer than 100 people a year receive this status. Access is therefore tied to a valid residence or visa and to employer or sponsor insurance. It is not on the same terms as citizens. Irregular migrants, including workers whose employer has cancelled or never issued their residence permit, cannot get insurance or a Health Card and have only emergency care.

### q123 · Right of access to public housing programs.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. The public housing scheme is set out in Law No. 2 of 2007 on the Housing System (قانون رقم (2) لسنة 2007 بشأن نظام الإسكان), and it is reserved for citizens. Article 2 states that the system aims 'to contribute to providing suitable housing for citizens'. It does so through grants for buying land, interest-free housing loans, and housing units provided by purchase or lease. Article 6 makes being 'of Qatari nationality' and at least 22 years old a condition for any benefit, and leaves the Council of Ministers to set priorities and the conditions for naturalised citizens. The same rule applies to housing for people in need, which the Qatar Development Bank builds and leases to the Ministry for allocation, and to the rent allowance paid when no unit is available (Article 12). Article 13 again requires the beneficiary to be 'of Qatari nationality' and to prove need through a social assessment under the Social Security Law (Law No. 38 of 1995). No migrant category is eligible, whatever their residence status or length of stay; only naturalised citizens may benefit, on conditions. The closest provisions for migrants concern employer-provided worker accommodation. Ministerial Order No. 18 of 2014 sets minimum standards for workers' housing, and Law No. 17 of 2020 requires a housing allowance of at least QAR 500 a month when the employer provides no accommodation. These are employer duties under labour law, not access to publicly supported housing. Permanent residence under Law No. 10 of 2018 opens government health care and education but not the housing system.

### q125 · Right to equal access to free primary education for migrant children.
- Law: Yes, but only for certain visa types and migrant statuses (e.g., economic, family, humanitarian, temporary or regular migrants)
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Qatar protects this right in Law No. 25 of 2001 on Compulsory Education (قانون رقم (25) لسنة 2001 بشأن التعليم الإلزامي), Article 2, which provides that "education shall be compulsory and free for all children from the beginning of the primary stage until the end of the preparatory stage or the age of eighteen, whichever comes first, and the Ministry shall provide the requirements for this." Article 3 obliges the person responsible for any child to enrol them at age six. The statute is not limited by nationality. The Permanent Constitution, by contrast, gives the right to education only to "all citizens" (art. 49) and otherwise sets education as a general state objective (art. 25). In practice, access for non-nationals is narrowed by Ministry of Education and Higher Education admission rules. Non-Qatari children are admitted to government schools only in defined categories: GCC nationals, children of Qatari mothers, children of non-Qatari government and approved charity employees, holders of permanent residence (Law No. 10 of 2018), and, for 2026-27, private-sector families who actually live in designated outlying areas, where places allow. Admission depends on a valid residence ID, so irregular migrants are effectively excluded. Law No. 12 of 1996 also requires non-Qatari, non-GCC pupils in public schools to pay textbook and school-transport fees. These are now set by Ministerial Decision No. 20 of 2023, with exemptions for children of Qatari women, children with disabilities and permanent residents. Most children of private-sector migrant workers therefore have to attend fee-charging private schools. The protection is real but reaches only some categories of regular migrants.


## Labor

### q95 · Right to equal pay for the same work.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Qatari law does not require that migrant workers be paid the same as Qatari citizens for the same work or for work of equal value. The closest provisions fall short. Law No. 17 of 2020 Determining the National Minimum Wage for Workers and Domestic Workers (قانون رقم (17) لسنة 2020 بشأن تحديد الحد الأدنى لأجور العمال والمستخدمين في المنازل) sets one wage floor for every "worker" and "domestic worker", defined without reference to nationality. It describes the minimum wage as "the lowest amount that workers and domestic workers can receive by virtue of this law regardless of their tasks, earnings or skills levels" (art. 1). Article 3 declares void any agreement for less. This floor is non-discriminatory, but it guarantees only a minimum (QAR 1,000 basic plus allowances). It does not require pay parity above that level. The Labour Law, Law No. 14 of 2004 (قانون رقم (14) لسنة 2004 بإصدار قانون العمل), contains an equal-pay rule only by sex: a female worker "shall be offered a Remuneration equivalent to a male Worker when she performs similar work" (art. 93). It has no rule against pay differences based on nationality. The Labour Law also openly treats Qatari and non-Qatari workers differently: Qatari workers "shall be given priority in employment" (art. 18), and non-Qataris may only work with a permit (art. 23). Article 35 of the Constitution bans discrimination on grounds of sex, race, language or religion. It does not list nationality or national origin, and no court decision applying it to pay was found. In practice wages are commonly set by nationality, and this is lawful. Domestic, farm and government workers fall outside the Labour Law entirely (art. 3).

### q97 · Right to freedom of choice of employment.
- Law: Employment is tied to specific employer; change of employer possible, but requires new work permit application
- Implementation: Sometimes; Usually

National law in Qatar protects this right, in a limited way, in Law No. 19 of 2020 amending certain provisions of Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (19) لسنة 2020 بتعديل بعض أحكام القانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015), together with Law No. 14 of 2004 promulgating the Labour Law (قانون رقم (14) لسنة 2004 بإصدار قانون العمل). Law 19/2020 replaced article 21 of Law 21/2015. Article 21 now reads: "An expatriate worker can change employer, as per the established procedures at the Ministry of Administrative Development, Labour and Social Affairs." Law 19/2020 also deleted article 22 and removed the earlier requirement of the current employer's consent (the no-objection certificate). The worker remains tied to a specific employer, however. Under the Labour Law, non-Qatari workers "shall not be employed unless approved by the Department" and must hold a work permit, which depends on a residence permit (art. 23). The Minister may cancel the permit for "working with another Employer other than that for whose employment the permit was issued" (art. 25). The replaced article 23 of Law 21/2015 bars employing an expatriate without a contract or hiring them out to another party, unless the Ministry authorises a temporary secondment. Each change of employer therefore needs a new Ministry approval, a new work permit and a transfer of residence, applied for through the Ministry's online system. Notice periods are set by Decree-Law 18/2020 and the procedure by Ministerial Decision 51/2020; neither was read. The rule covers Labour Law workers and, by the same Ministry procedure, domestic workers. The Labour Law excludes government employees, whose mobility follows other rules.

### q99 · Right of spouse/partner of principal migrant to work.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. The spouse of a migrant worker has no automatic right to work. Under Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم), a spouse admitted on family residence is sponsored by the head of the family, who is the responsible "recruiter" for dependants (arts. 12 and 17, per the GLMM abstract). Family residence carries no permission to work. Law No. 14 of 2004 promulgating the Labour Law (قانون رقم (14) لسنة 2004 بإصدار قانون العمل) provides that "Non-Qatari Workers shall not be employed unless approved by the Department" and that they "shall also obtain Work permits in Qatar in accordance with the rules and procedures prescribed by the Ministry" (art. 23). This applies to every non-Qatari, including a spouse on family residence. The Minister may cancel a work permit for work with an employer other than the one named in the permit (art. 25). Article 23 of Law 21/2015, as replaced by Law No. 19 of 2020, also forbids anyone to "employ an expatriate worker without concluding an employment contract" under Ministry-regulated procedures. In practice the Ministry of Labour gives family-residence holders two routes: a special work permit for a named employer while keeping family residence, or conversion of family residence into work residence. Both need an employer's application and Ministry approval, so the spouse must apply for permission and is limited to the approved employer. No category of migrant spouse was found to be exempt.

### q101 · Right to maintain immigration status after loss of employment.
- Law: Loss of employment implies loss of immigration status unless a new job is found within a specified time period
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Qatar protects this right, in a limited way, in Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم), arts. 17, 19 and 24, and in its Executive Regulations as amended by Minister of Interior Decision No. 51 of 2020. A worker's residence permit is tied to the recruiter (mustaqdim), who is responsible for it (art. 17). The recruiter must notify the authorities within fourteen days if the expatriate leaves the job (art. 19). The expatriate must 'depart the country within 90 days if his residence permit has expired or is cancelled' (art. 24). Decision 51/2020 allows a change of employer, notified to the Ministry of Labour, 'during the validity period of the residence permit or within 90 days following its expiration'. Decree-Law No. 19 of 2020 rewrote art. 21 so that workers can move to a new employer without the current employer's consent. In practice a worker who loses a job keeps lawful status only while the permit stays valid, or for a limited period after it lapses, and must find a new recruiter in that time or leave. A Ministry of Interior rule of June 2026, reported by practitioners but not read in an official text, cut the departure window after cancellation to 14 days. Under that rule a local transfer is no longer possible once the permit has been cancelled, so the worker has to apply again from abroad. Dependants' permits follow the principal's. Workers outside the Labour Law are covered by the same residence rules. Holders of a permanent residence card under Law No. 10 of 2018 do not depend on an employer.

### q103 · Right to equal employment conditions and protection.
- Law: Migrants have a right to most of the employment conditions and protections for citizens
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Qatar protects this right, in part, in Law No. 14 of 2004 promulgating the Labour Law (قانون رقم (14) لسنة 2004 بإصدار قانون العمل) and Law No. 15 of 2017 on domestic workers (قانون رقم (15) لسنة 2017 بشأن المستخدمين في المنازل). The Labour Law "applies to Employers and Workers" (art. 2) without distinguishing Qatari from non-Qatari workers on core conditions. It sets maximum ordinary hours of "forty-eight hours per week, at the rate of eight hours per day" (art. 73). Overtime is paid at no less than the basic wage plus 25% (art. 74). Workers get a paid weekly rest of at least 24 consecutive hours (art. 75), paid public holidays (art. 78), annual leave of three weeks, or four after five years (art. 79), and paid sick leave (art. 82). The law also imposes health and safety duties on employers (arts. 99-100) and regulates dismissal and end-of-service gratuity (arts. 54, 61). Migrants holding work permits under the Labour Law therefore enjoy most of the same protections as citizens. The equality is incomplete in four ways. First, the Labour Law excludes domestic workers, farm workers, casual workers and government employees (art. 3). Domestic workers, almost all of them migrants, fall under the separate and weaker Law No. 15 of 2017 (text not read). Second, Qatari workers have priority in employment (art. 18), and a migrant's right to work depends on a permit that can be cancelled (arts. 23, 25). Third, according to the law map, workers' committees and unions are open only to Qatari workers (art. 116, not re-read). Fourth, no Qatari law prohibits discrimination in employment on grounds of nationality.

### q109 · Right to equal access to social security protections.
- Law: Access to some, but not all types of social security protections (e.g., no equal access under this program; or access depending on visa type)
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Qatar protects this right only partially, through employer-liability provisions of the Labour Law (قانون رقم (14) لسنة 2004 بإصدار قانون العمل, Law No. 14 of 2004), while the state social insurance system is reserved to nationals. The Social Insurance Law promulgated by Law No. 1 of 2022 (قانون رقم (1) لسنة 2022 بإصدار قانون التأمينات الاجتماعية), art. 2, provides that 'the provisions of this law apply to Qataris' in the government sector and to private-sector workers subject to the Labour Law. Art. 3 sets conditions 'for Qatari employees and workers'. Non-Qatari migrant workers are therefore excluded from the state scheme's old-age, disability and survivors' pensions, whatever their permit. GCC nationals are handled through the separate GCC insurance-extension system. What migrant workers covered by the Labour Law do receive is employer-funded. Art. 54 obliges the employer to pay an end-of-service gratuity of 'not less than a three-week Remuneration for every year of employment' after one year of service. Art. 109 gives a worker with an occupational injury treatment at the employer's cost, full pay for up to six months and then half pay. Art. 110 gives compensation for death or for whole or partial disability caused by work. Employer-paid basic health insurance for expatriates is mandatory under Law No. 22 of 2021 (secondary sources only). These are lump-sum or employer-liability benefits, not social insurance on equal terms with citizens. Domestic workers are excluded from the Labour Law (art. 3) and rely on the weaker Law No. 15 of 2017. Government-sector migrant workers fall under the human resources legislation. Because migrants have some, but not all, types of protection, the middle option applies.

### q111 · Right of equal access to unemployment benefits.
- Law: No access to any unemployment benefits
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. No Qatari statute grants migrant workers an unemployment benefit. The Social Insurance Law promulgated by Law No. 1 of 2022 (قانون رقم (1) لسنة 2022 بإصدار قانون التأمينات الاجتماعية) applies, under art. 2, only 'to Qataris' in the government and private sectors. It covers retirement, disability and survivors' pensions and contains no unemployment insurance branch. The Labour Law (قانون رقم (14) لسنة 2004 بإصدار قانون العمل, Law No. 14 of 2004) provides only employer-paid entitlements on termination. These are pay during the notice period and an end-of-service gratuity of at least three weeks' wage per year of service (art. 54). Neither is an income-replacement benefit paid during unemployment. Art. 50 shows the national orientation of job-seeking support: it requires the employer to let 'the Qatari Worker' be absent during the notice period to register with the employment department. The Workers' Support and Insurance Fund (Law No. 17 of 2018) pays wages and end-of-service dues that employers fail to pay when labour committees order them. It compensates employer default and is not an unemployment benefit. A migrant who loses a job must find a new employer within the period the immigration rules allow or leave the country, and receives no state income support in the meantime. Migrant workers of every permit category, including domestic workers under Law No. 15 of 2017, therefore have no access to unemployment benefits.

### q113 · Right of temporary migrants to become lawfully settled.
- Law: Yes, there is an opportunity to switch to permanent status after certain conditions are
- Implementation: Rarely

National law in Qatar protects this right in Law No. 10 of 2018 on permanent residence (قانون رقم (10) لسنة 2018 بشأن الإقامة الدائمة), arts. 1, 2, 4 and 5 (Official Gazette No. 15 of 21 October 2018; in force 22 October 2018). Article 1 makes any non-Qatari eligible for a permanent residence permit who has 'legally resided in Qatar for twenty years if born outside of Qatar and ten years if born in Qatar'. The applicant also needs an income that meets his needs and those of his dependants, a good record with no prior conviction in or outside Qatar, and 'sufficient knowledge of the Arabic language'. Article 2 exempts certain groups from these conditions: children of Qatari mothers, foreign spouses of Qataris, children of naturalised citizens, and persons who have rendered special services or have special qualifications. Article 4 caps grants at 'one hundred individuals ... every year'; the Amir may raise the number. Holders may leave and re-enter without permission (art. 5). The card also gives access to state education and health care, subject to Cabinet conditions (art. 6), and extends to the spouse and minor children. Long-resident temporary workers can therefore in law move to permanent status. The route is narrow, however: it needs two decades of lawful residence, income and Arabic thresholds, a Ministry of Interior committee review, ministerial discretion and a small annual quota. Naturalisation under Law No. 38 of 2005 is a separate and even more restricted route.

### q115 · Right of temporary migrants to settle regardless of initial status.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Rarely

National law in Qatar protects this right in Law No. 10 of 2018 on permanent residence (قانون رقم (10) لسنة 2018 بشأن الإقامة الدائمة), art. 1. The general route is defined by length of 'legal' residence, not by the type of residence permit. Any non-Qatari who has 'legally resided in Qatar for twenty years if born outside of Qatar and ten years if born in Qatar' may apply, provided he has adequate income, a clean record and sufficient Arabic. The summarised text read for this coding names no excluded permit class. Holders of work residence under the Labour Law, domestic workers under Law No. 15 of 2017, dependants under art. 12 of Law No. 21 of 2015, and self-sponsored residents can all count lawful residence toward the threshold. Article 2 adds privileged categories that do not need to meet the general conditions. Art. 4's annual quota of one hundred grants limits the number of grants, not which visa types qualify. Irregular periods do not count, because the residence must be legal. Refugees granted asylum under Law No. 11 of 2018 are not expressly excluded either. In practice, the income and Arabic-language conditions, the discretion of the Ministry of Interior committee and the quota make the route far harder for low-wage workers than for professionals. These are conditions on the applicant, however, not exclusions by visa type.

### q117 · Right to redress following employer breach of employment contract.
- Law: Yes, same as citizens
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Qatar protects this right in Law No. 14 of 2004 promulgating the Labour Law (قانون رقم (14) لسنة 2004 بإصدار قانون العمل), art. 10. It also does so in chapter 11 bis (arts. 115 bis to 115 bis/6), added by Law No. 13 of 2017 (قانون رقم (13) لسنة 2017), and in Law No. 17 of 2018 establishing the Workers' Support and Insurance Fund. Under art. 10, all lawsuits by workers claiming benefits under the Law 'and their Employment Contract, shall be executed promptly free from judicial fees', within one year of the contract's end. Claims first go to the Ministry of Labour for amicable settlement (art. 115 bis). If that fails, they go to a Labour Dispute Settlement Committee chaired by a first-instance judge. The committee has competence over 'all individual disputes resulting from the application of the provisions of the Labour Law or a labour contract' and must decide within three weeks of its first session (art. 115 bis/2). Its decisions have executory force (art. 115 bis/5) and can be appealed to the Court of Appeal within fifteen days (art. 115 bis/6). The committee can also order reinstatement or compensation for arbitrary dismissal. The Fund pays sums awarded by the committees when employers default and then recovers them from the employer; a 2022 amendment caps those payments. None of these provisions distinguishes by nationality, so migrant and Qatari private-sector workers use the same forum. Domestic workers, who are outside the Labour Law, have a separate Law No. 15 of 2017 and are reported to use the same committees, with lower Fund caps. Government employees of any nationality fall under the civil service regime. Under art. 22 of Law No. 21 of 2015, a worker with a pending case may be transferred temporarily to another employer.

### q135 · Right to be free from discrimination on the basis of race or ethnicity.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Rarely

National law in Qatar protects this right in the Permanent Constitution of the State of Qatar (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر), article 35. Article 35 provides: "All persons shall be equal before the law and there shall be no discrimination of whatsoever on grounds of sex, race, language, or religion." The official Explanatory Memorandum to the Constitution makes clear that this covers migrants. It explains that article 34 is limited to citizens, while article 35 uses "the generalized and non-specific terms 'persons', 'people'", meaning "all residents in the territory of the State". It adds that "the Constitution prohibits discrimination between people because of their racial origins." The ban therefore applies to every non-national in Qatar regardless of status. It covers race and, through "racial origins", ethnicity. The limits are real. The listed grounds do not include nationality or national origin. No dedicated anti-discrimination statute was found that defines racial discrimination, provides remedies in employment, housing or services, or creates an enforcement body. The Labour Law contains no general non-discrimination clause; its only equal-pay rule concerns sex (art. 93). The constitutional ban is backed by the Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination, to which Qatar acceded in 1976 and which has the force of law once ratified and published. Qatar relies on the Convention's citizen/non-citizen distinction (art. 1(2)) to justify differences between nationals and non-nationals.


## Legal personhood

### q27 · Right not to have identity documents confiscated.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Qatar protects this right in Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم), Articles 8 and 39. Article 8(3) requires that the employer "must give the Passport or Travel Document to the Foreign National after completing the licensing or renewal procedures" of the residence permit. The only exception is where the foreign national has asked in writing for the employer to keep it, and even then "the employer must give the Foreign National his Passport or Travel Document upon request." Article 39 makes breach of Article 8(3) an offence punishable by a fine of up to QAR 25,000, "without prejudice to any other law carrying a more severe penalty." Article 1 defines the "foreign national" (wafid) as any non-Qatari who enters the country to work, reside, visit or for any other purpose. The duty therefore applies to every recruiter, whether a company, a household employer of domestic workers or a host. Article 15 keeps the public authority's power: during residence the foreign national must show his passport, travel document or residence permit to the competent authorities when asked. The prohibition is aimed at the recruiter or employer, not at third parties in general. The worker's written consent can lawfully let the employer hold the document, although the worker may demand it back at any time. The penalty is a fine only; there is no prison term.

### q29 · Right to birth registration.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Qatar protects this right in Law No. 3 of 2016 regulating the registration of births and deaths (قانون رقم (3) لسنة 2016 بشأن تنظيم قيد المواليد والوفيات), Articles 2 to 4. Article 2 provides: "The competent authority must be notified of every person born within the State, and of Qataris born while travelling or residing outside the State, using the forms prepared for this purpose." The duty covers every birth on Qatari territory whatever the nationality or migration status of the parents. Nationality matters only for births abroad, where the duty is limited to Qataris. Article 3 lists who must report: the father if present, adult male and then female relatives present at the birth, the physician or licensed birth attendant, and directors of public and private hospitals, penal institutions and other places of birth. It also lists the mother, a court-appointed person, and the captain of a ship or aircraft. Article 4 requires notification within 15 days and sets the details to be recorded, including the names and details of both parents. The competent authority is the Ministry of Public Health's health offices (Art. 1). The law creates criminal penalties for failing to report and for false reporting. Separately, Law No. 21 of 2015, Article 13, requires resident parents to apply for a residence permit for a newborn within 90 days. The text sets no marital-status condition for registration.

### q31 · Right to government document recognizing residency, work status, and identity.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Qatar protects this right in Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم), Articles 1, 8 and 9. Article 1 defines the residence permit as "a permit that establishes the identity of the Foreign National and permits him to reside within the country." Article 8 provides that foreign nationals "residing in the country for whatever purpose must obtain a Residency Permit," and requires the recruiter to complete the procedures and renew the permit within 90 days. Article 9 obliges the state to issue it: "The Competent Authorities shall issue the Residency Permit for the Foreign National in accordance with this law. It will contain the Foreign National's name, image, signature, and personal information." Article 10 requires the recruiter to bring the foreign national to the authorities within 30 days of entry to complete residence procedures. Article 4 ties an entry visa for work to the labour contract. Article 13 provides for permits for newborn children of residents. The mandated document thus records both residence and identity, and for workers it is issued on the basis of the recruiter and employment relationship. The law does not explicitly require the permit to state work status. The duty to obtain and renew the permit sits with the recruiter, not the migrant. Irregular migrants and visitors staying under 30 days (Art. 11) are not covered.

### q33 · Right against destruction of document recognizing residency, work status, and identity.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. No provision of national law specifically makes it an offence for anyone to destroy a migrant's passport, travel document or residence permit. Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015) penalises retaining the passport (Arts. 8(3) and 39) but says nothing about destroying it or the residence permit. The closest provisions are in the Penal Code, Law No. 11 of 2004 (قانون رقم (11) لسنة 2004 بإصدار قانون العقوبات), and fall short. Article 193 punishes whoever "unlawfully destroys papers, deeds or official records deposited in government places and intended for storing, or delivered to the public officer assigned to take custody" of them. It protects documents in official custody, not documents held by migrants. Article 108 covers destroying papers relating to state security. Articles 204 to 206 deal with forging official documents, not destroying them. Article 389 is a general property-damage offence against whoever "intentionally destroys, damages or spoils another person's movable or immovable properties, or makes them … invalid." It could in principle reach an employer who destroys a worker's passport or ID card, but it is not a document-specific protection. Following the MRD's approach, where general criminal-damage law does not count without a document-specific rule, the right is coded as absent.


## Liberty

### q15 · Right to communicate with consular officials.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Qatar protects this right in Article 36 of the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations of 1963 (اتفاقية فيينا للعلاقات القنصلية لسنة 1963). Qatar acceded on 4 November 1998, after a Council of Ministers approval in September 1998. Al Meezan's listing gives the approving instrument as Decree No. 13 of 1999 (مرسوم رقم (13) لسنة 1999), dated 26 May 1999. The Convention takes effect domestically through Article 68 of the Permanent Constitution (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر), under which a treaty 'shall have the power of law after ratification and publication in The Official Gazette'. Article 36(1)(a) provides that 'consular officers shall be free to communicate with nationals of the sending State and to have access to them', and that nationals have 'the same freedom'. Article 36(1)(b) requires the authorities, if the detained person so requests, to inform the consular post 'without delay' of an arrest or detention. They must forward the detainee's communications and tell the person of these rights. Article 36(1)(c) gives consular officers the right to visit, converse and correspond with detained nationals and to arrange legal representation. The right covers every foreign national, whatever their residence status, including people held in criminal custody and in the Interior Ministry's deportation centre. No Qatari statute repeats the obligation on its own. Article 36 of the Constitution guarantees personal freedom for 'no person' to be arrested save under law, but it says nothing on consular contact. Protection therefore rests on the treaty having the force of law, with the same rank as a statute.

### q17 · Right of child migrants to be free from detention on the basis of migrant status.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم) governs immigration detention. Its Article 28 provides that 'the Minister may, when necessary, place an expatriate against whom a judicial judgment of deportation or an order of repatriation has been issued in the place designated for that purpose for thirty days, renewable for another similar period'. The provision applies to any 'expatriate' (any non-Qatari, art. 1), and no exemption or special rule is made for minors. Its alternative, an order to reside at a set location, applies only when deportation cannot be carried out. It is not a child-protection rule. Article 36 of the Permanent Constitution (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر) allows detention 'under the provisions of the law', so it does not bar this detention. Qatar ratified the Convention on the Rights of the Child in 1995, and under Constitution art. 68 the CRC has the force of law. But CRC art. 37(b) requires only that child detention be a measure of last resort and for the shortest time. It does not forbid detaining children on migration grounds, and Qatar's general reservation subordinates provisions incompatible with Sharia. I found no statute, regulation or court decision prohibiting the detention of migrant children. Children who accompany parents held for deportation therefore have no legal protection against detention.

### q19 · Right of migrants to be free from detention on the basis of migrant status.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم) expressly authorises detention on migration grounds. Article 28 states that 'the Minister may, when necessary, place an expatriate against whom a judicial judgment of deportation or an order of repatriation from the State has been issued in the place designated for that purpose for thirty days, renewable for another similar period'. Repatriation orders can follow the expiry or cancellation of a residence permit, or a finding that a person's presence threatens security, the economy, public health or morals (arts. 24-25, per secondary sources). Detention is therefore available for irregular and regular migrants alike. Only when deportation cannot be carried out does Article 28 allow the Minister to order the person to live in a set location for renewable two-week periods and report to the security department. The law also makes several residence violations criminal offences punishable by imprisonment, such as overstaying, failing to leave after a permit lapses, or working for someone other than the recruiter. Article 36 of the Permanent Constitution (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر) guarantees personal freedom but allows detention 'under the provisions of the law'. It does not exclude administrative immigration detention. No statute, regulation or court ruling prohibits detaining migrants because of their migration status. The law sets no individual test of necessity, though the detention is time-limited (30 days, renewable once).

### q21 · Right to appeal legality, conditions, and length of detention
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Detention before removal is governed by Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم). Article 28 lets the Minister of Interior "consign, when necessary, a Foreign National who has received a deportation order from a court ruling or repatriation order from the state to a place designated for that purpose for a period not in excess of 30 days, subject to extensions of varying lengths of time". If removal cannot be carried out, he may instead order residence in a designated place for two weeks, also extendable. The law gives the detainee no way to appeal or seek review of the legality, conditions or length of this detention, and it sets no overall maximum. Law No. 7 of 2007 on the settlement of administrative disputes (قانون رقم (7) لسنة 2007 بشأن الفصل في المنازعات الإدارية), as amended by Law 12/2013, closes the general route to the courts. Article 3(3) lets the Administrative Circuit annul final administrative decisions, but it excludes "(f) Decisions issued under the laws on the entry, residence and deportation of foreigners." Ministerial repatriation orders under art. 25, and the detention ordered to enforce them, therefore cannot be challenged in court. Article 36 of the Constitution says that no one may be arrested or detained "save under the provisions of the law". It creates no habeas corpus or review procedure for immigration detention. Migrants held under the Code of Criminal Procedure have the judicial controls on criminal custody, and a judicial deportation sentence can be appealed in criminal proceedings. Neither of these is a remedy against administrative immigration detention. This applies to all expatriates, regular or irregular, held for deportation.

### q23 · Right to an individualized detention decision.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم) contains the only detention power for expatriates facing removal. Under Article 28 the Minister of Interior "may consign, when necessary," anyone with a judicial deportation order or a ministerial repatriation order "to a place designated for that purpose for a period not in excess of 30 days, subject to extensions". Detention is therefore discretionary, not mandatory. The words "when necessary" are the only limit, and the law does not say what necessity means. Nothing requires the authority to assess the individual: no examination of flight risk, vulnerability, family situation or alternatives, no written reasons and no proportionality test. The alternative of designated residence for two weeks (art. 28, para. 2) applies only once deportation "is not possible to execute". It is not presented as a less restrictive option to be weighed first. Repatriation itself may be ordered under art. 25 "as an exception to the provisions of any other law" on broad public-order grounds. The decision cannot be reviewed in court (Law No. 7 of 2007, art. 3(3)(f)), so no judge tests whether detention was necessary in a particular case. The Constitution, art. 36, only requires that detention be "under the provisions of the law". This general legality principle does not amount to a requirement of individualised detention decisions. These rules cover all expatriates subject to removal, including workers reported as 'absconding' whose residence has been cancelled.

### q25 · Right to be free from torture and inhumane treatment.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Qatar protects this right in the Permanent Constitution of the State of Qatar (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر), Article 36, and in the UN Convention against Torture, to which Qatar acceded in 2000 and which takes effect domestically after ratification and publication. Article 36(2) provides: "No person shall be subjected to torture, or any degrading treatment; and torture shall be considered as a crime punishable by law." The guarantee is worded for 'no person', not only citizens. It therefore covers all migrants, whatever their status, including irregular migrants, asylum seekers and people in immigration detention. The Constitution expressly bans torture and degrading treatment and makes torture a crime. It does not name 'cruel' or 'inhuman' treatment separately. The CAT, including art. 16 on cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment, fills that gap, subject to Qatar's reservation on arts. 1 and 16, which the Committee against Torture calls vague and of imprecise scope. The Penal Code, as amended by Law No. 8 of 2010, makes torture by public officials a crime and adopts the CAT definition (art. 159 bis, as recorded by the Committee against Torture). One gap remains. Penal Code art. 1 still allows Sharia corporal punishments such as flogging as criminal sanctions. The government says these are not applied, but they sit uneasily with the ban on degrading punishment. Protection is otherwise the same for nationals and non-nationals.


## Life

### q9 · Right to be free from use of force to prevent entry to territory.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. No Qatari statute expressly prohibits officers or agents from using force to stop migrants entering the national territory. The closest rules sit in the Police Force Law (قانون رقم (23) لسنة 1993 بشأن قوة الشرطة, Law No. 23 of 1993 on the Police Force). These rules permit force rather than forbid it. Article 6 provides that "police officers may use force without using weapons, to the extent necessary to carry out their duties, provided that force is the only means of doing so." Article 7 then lets police use their firearms in listed cases, including arresting persons "who attempt to resist or flee". Nothing in the law excludes border control or the interception of irregular entrants from these powers. Article 36 of the Permanent Constitution (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر, 2004) protects every person from torture and degrading treatment, which limits how force may be used. It does not prohibit force as a means of preventing entry. Article 161 of the Penal Code (Law No. 11 of 2004) punishes a public servant who uses harshness "except in those cases where the law authorizes such acts". That offence assumes force is lawful where a statute authorises it. Law No. 21 of 2015 on the entry, exit and residence of expatriates regulates admission, refusal and deportation, but it says nothing about force at the frontier. Taken together, Qatari law permits necessary and proportionate force, so the specific protection the MRD asks about is absent.

### q11 · Right to be free from use of lethal force to prevent entry to territory.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. No Qatari law expressly forbids officers or agents from using lethal force to stop migrants entering the country. Firearms use by the police is governed by article 7 of the Police Force Law (قانون رقم (23) لسنة 1993 بشأن قوة الشرطة, Law No. 23 of 1993 on the Police Force). It lists three permitted cases: (a) lawful defence of one's own or another's life, honour or property; (b) arresting persons "who attempt to resist or flee", subject to criteria set by the Minister of Interior; and (c) dispersing gatherings of ten or more people that aim to commit a crime or endanger public security. Shooting must be "the only means" of achieving these aims. The officer must first warn, fire the first shot into the air and only then fire "towards the leg". These rules set graduated steps and favour non-lethal outcomes. They do not prohibit lethal force, and they do not exclude border situations. A migrant who resists or flees while being apprehended at the frontier falls within case (b). The constitutional ban on torture and degrading treatment (art. 36) and the Penal Code offence of unlawful harshness by public servants (art. 161) apply generally, not specifically at borders. Law No. 21 of 2015 on the entry and residence of expatriates is silent on the use of force. The specific prohibition the MRD looks for therefore does not exist in Qatari law.

### q13 · Right to be protected from trafficking or smuggling.
- Law: No, government may punish trafficked or smuggled migrants
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar for both trafficked and smuggled migrants. Article 4 of the Law on Combating Human Trafficking (قانون رقم (15) لسنة 2011 بشأن مكافحة الاتجار بالبشر, Law No. 15 of 2011) protects trafficked persons only in part. It provides that "the victim shall not be considered criminally or civilly liable for any of the crimes of trafficking in persons, when it arose from or was directly connected to his being a victim." The exemption is limited to trafficking offences. It does not cover other offences a victim commits as a direct result of being trafficked, such as immigration, residence, 'absconding' or prostitution offences. Chapter Three (arts. 5 ff.) obliges the authorities to protect victims, provide them with health, educational and social care, and arrange their safe return. Qatari law has no provision at all on smuggled migrants. There is no smuggling statute with a non-punishment clause. Smuggled persons who enter or stay irregularly remain exposed to the penalties of Law No. 21 of 2015 on the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (penalty chapter, arts. 38-41) and to detention and deportation. Because smuggled migrants are not exempted, and the exemption for trafficked persons is narrow, the MRD's lowest option applies.


## Nationality

### q63 · Right to maintain citizenship status after convictions.
- Law: Only major convictions (e.g., criminal offenses) can lead to loss of citizenship status
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Qatar provides that only serious convictions can lead to withdrawal of the nationality of naturalised citizens. The rule is in the Law on Qatari Nationality (قانون رقم (38) لسنة 2005 بشأن الجنسية القطرية, Law No. 38 of 2005), Articles 11 and 12. Under Article 12, nationality "may be withdrawn by Emiri decision from a naturalised Qatari" on any Article 11 ground or where "he has been convicted by a final judgment of a felony (jinaya) or of a crime involving honour or integrity" (Art. 12(2)). The other Article 12 grounds are fraud in acquisition, dismissal from public office for reasons of honour or integrity, and more than one year's unjustified absence. Article 11 applies to all Qataris. It allows removal of nationality for a conviction for "a crime which impugns his loyalty to the State," among other grounds. Minor offences and administrative violations are not grounds for withdrawal, so the middle option fits. The protection is weak, however. Withdrawal is discretionary ("may") and is ordered by Emiri decision. Article 12 also lets nationality be withdrawn "in all cases" on the Interior Minister's proposal, "for reasons of public interest, if there are strong justifications," with no conviction needed. Under Article 3(3)(a) of the Law on Administrative Disputes (قانون رقم (7) لسنة 2007 بشأن الفصل في المنازعات الإدارية, Law No. 7 of 2007, as amended by Law 12/2013), Emiri decrees and decisions cannot be challenged in the administrative courts. A naturalised person therefore has no judicial remedy against withdrawal. Under Article 14, withdrawal takes effect only from the date of the decision. Under Article 13, it applies only to the person concerned unless the decision says otherwise.

### q65 · Right of economic migrants to citizenship.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. The only general route to naturalisation is in the Law on Qatari Nationality (قانون رقم (38) لسنة 2005 بشأن الجنسية القطرية, Law No. 38 of 2005), Article 2. It is a discretionary grant, not a pathway the migrant can claim. Article 2 provides that "Qatari nationality may be granted by Emiri decision to a non-Qatari" who has lawfully resided in Qatar "for not less than twenty-five consecutive years" before applying. Absences of up to two months a year are tolerated but deducted from the total. The applicant must also have a lawful and sufficient means of living, be of good conduct and repute with no final conviction for a crime involving honour or integrity, and have adequate knowledge of Arabic. Even an applicant who meets every condition has no entitlement. Article 17 states that nationality under Articles 2 to 6 "shall not be granted to more than fifty persons in one year." Article 6 allows nationality to be granted, notwithstanding Article 2, to people who have rendered great service or have skills the country needs. That too is a discretionary Emiri grant, not a pathway. Grants take the form of Emiri decisions, which Article 3(3)(a) of the Law on Administrative Disputes (Law No. 7 of 2007, as amended by Law 12/2013) excludes from review by the administrative courts. Under Article 16, naturalised citizens also may not vote, stand for election or be appointed to a legislative body. As with Jordan's comparable discretionary regime, the existence of a discretionary, quota-capped grant does not amount to a pathway for economic migrants.

### q67 · Right of humanitarian migrants to citizenship.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Qatar has no specific route to citizenship for refugees or other humanitarian migrants. The Law on Qatari Nationality (قانون رقم (38) لسنة 2005 بشأن الجنسية القطرية, Law No. 38 of 2005) contains no facilitated naturalisation for refugees, asylum seekers or stateless persons. Its general Article 2 is discretionary: nationality "may be granted by Emiri decision to a non-Qatari" after "not less than twenty-five consecutive years" of lawful habitual residence, with sufficient lawful income, good conduct and no conviction for a crime involving honour or integrity, and adequate Arabic. Under Article 17, grants under Articles 2 to 6 are capped at "fifty persons in one year." Emiri decisions are excluded from administrative-court review by Article 3(3)(a) of the Law on Administrative Disputes (Law No. 7 of 2007, as amended by Law 12/2013). Article 2 deems a foundling or a child born in Qatar to unknown parents a naturalised Qatari, which prevents statelessness at birth. That rule does not create a pathway for humanitarian migrants. The Political Asylum Law (قانون رقم (11) لسنة 2018 بتنظيم اللجوء السياسي, Law No. 11 of 2018) gives recognised political refugees residence-related rights. On the first-pass reading recorded in the law map, it contains no naturalisation provision. Like the Jordanian regime in the MRD examples, a humanitarian migrant who met every Article 2 condition would still depend entirely on the Emir's discretion within the annual quota.

### q69 · Right of family migrants to citizenship.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes; Usually

National law in Qatar provides a pathway to citizenship for some family migrants. The pathway is in the Law on Qatari Nationality (قانون رقم (38) لسنة 2005 بشأن الجنسية القطرية, Law No. 38 of 2005), Articles 1(4), 4, 5 and 8. Article 8 provides that a woman who marries a Qatari in accordance with the Law regulating marriage to foreigners (Law No. 21 of 1989) "becomes Qatari if she declares in writing to the Minister of the Interior her wish to acquire Qatari nationality, and the marriage continues for five years from the date of the declaration." If the marriage ends earlier through divorce or the husband's death and she has a child by him, she may be granted nationality by Emiri decision, provided she kept living in Qatar until the five years were up. The Interior Minister may defer her entry into nationality for renewable one-year periods on public-interest grounds. Article 8 is not subject to the 50-person annual cap in Article 17. Under Article 9, a wife who acquired nationality keeps it if the marriage ends. Children born to a Qatari father anywhere are Qatari by origin (Art. 1(4)). The wife of a naturalised man may be granted nationality after five years' residence with him (Art. 5), and his minor children follow his status (Art. 4). Article 5 grants, unlike Article 8, are discretionary and count toward the quota. The pathway is strongly gendered. Foreign husbands of Qatari women have no route at all. Children of a Qatari mother and a foreign father do not acquire nationality by descent. They get only "priority" under the discretionary 25-year Article 2 route. Naturalised family members also may not vote or stand for election (Art. 16).

### q71 · Right to dual nationality.
- Law: Conditional, depending on the country of origin or some other consideration
- Implementation: Rarely

National law in Qatar protects this right only conditionally, in Law No. 38 of 2005 on Qatari Nationality (قانون رقم (38) لسنة 2005 بشأن الجنسية القطرية), Articles 2, 6 and 18. The general rule in Article 18 is a prohibition: 'Save as by a decision of the Emir, it shall be prohibited for any person to have in addition to the Qatari nationality another nationality.' Ordinary naturalisation under Article 2 requires 25 consecutive years of lawful residence, lawful income, good repute and knowledge of Arabic, and is granted by Emiri decree. It therefore requires the applicant to give up the nationality of origin. Minister of Interior Decision No. 14 of 2007 requires every applicant to file 'a written sworn affidavit confirming the applicant's willingness to renounce his/her original citizenship once Qatari citizenship is awarded.' The only statutory exception is Article 6. By Emiri decision, and notwithstanding Articles 2 and 18, nationality may be granted 'to those who have rendered great service to the country, or who have particular skills the country need, or students who excel'. Such persons 'may also retain their original nationality' where the public interest requires. Retention is thus possible only for a small class chosen at the Emir's discretion, never as of right. The ordinary migrant who naturalises after long residence must renounce. Article 11(5) also allows a Qatari who acquires another nationality to be stripped of Qatari nationality. No treaty to which Qatar is party requires it to allow dual nationality.

### q133 · Right of irregular migrants to regularize their status.
- Law: Yes, there is an opportunity to regularize after certain conditions are met
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Qatar protects this right, with conditions, in Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم), Articles 8 and 42-45. Article 8 obliges the recruiter (employer) to complete the residence-permit procedure and its renewal 'within a period not exceeding ninety days from the date of its expiry'. Failing to obtain or renew a permit, overstaying and similar breaches are offences under the law. Chapter Nine (Articles 42-45) lets them be settled rather than prosecuted. Under Article 42, 'the Minister or his delegate may approve conciliation in the offences specified in the conciliation schedule annexed to this Law, before a final judgment ... in return for the accused paying the amount fixed in the schedule'. Under Article 43, 'the criminal action lapses by conciliation'. Article 45 allows the Minister, 'according to the requirements of the public interest or for humanitarian considerations', to exempt the accused from the conciliation amount or reduce it. Once the violation is settled, the permit can be issued or renewed through the employer (or a new employer), and the migrant becomes lawful again. The Interior Ministry has used these articles for time-limited status-correction windows. The mechanism has clear limits. It is discretionary, not an entitlement. It covers mainly migrants who entered lawfully and then fell out of status (expired or unrenewed permits, overstayed visit or work visas). Regularization still depends on an employer or sponsor taking the migrant on and completing the permit. There is no route for persons who entered irregularly. A migrant who does not pay may have the permit cancelled and be deported (Article 44).


## Opinion and expression

### q83 · Right to freedom to hold opinion.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Qatar protects this right in the Permanent Constitution of the State of Qatar (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر, 2004, as amended in 2024), Article 47: "Freedom of expression of opinion and scientific research shall be guaranteed in accordance with the conditions and circumstances set forth in the law." Article 47 does not limit the right to citizens. The Constitution explicitly restricts the next-door Articles 42, 44 and 45 to "citizens", so Article 47 belongs to the group of rights that run to everyone in Qatar, alongside Article 35 (equality of "all persons") and Article 50 (worship for "all persons"). Migrants and Qataris therefore hold the same constitutional freedom of opinion. The ICCPR, including Article 19(1) on the right to hold opinions without interference, was acceded to in 2018. Under Constitution Article 68 it has "the power of law after ratification and publication in The Official Gazette", subject to Qatar's reservations and declarations. The constitutional text joins opinion and expression and qualifies both with "in accordance with the conditions and circumstances set forth in the law". Ordinary legislation therefore sets the limits, notably the Penal Code (Law No. 11 of 2004) and the Cybercrime Law (Law No. 14 of 2014). Those limits target the outward expression of opinions rather than holding them, and they apply to nationals and non-nationals alike. No provision denies non-citizens the freedom to hold opinions.

### q85 · Right to freedom of expression.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Rarely

National law in Qatar protects this right in the Permanent Constitution of the State of Qatar (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر, 2004, as amended in 2024), Articles 47 and 48. Article 47 provides that "Freedom of expression of opinion and scientific research shall be guaranteed in accordance with the conditions and circumstances set forth in the law". Article 48 provides that "Freedom of press, printing and publication shall be guaranteed in accordance with the law." Neither article is limited to citizens. Elsewhere in Part Three the Constitution says "citizens" when it means to restrict a right (Articles 34, 42, 44, 45, 49), so on its face this guarantee covers migrants on the same terms as Qataris. Article 46 likewise gives "each individual" the right to address public authorities. Under Article 68 the ICCPR (Article 19) has the force of law once ratified and published, subject to Qatar's reservations. The guarantee is subject to law and therefore heavily qualified. The Penal Code (Law No. 11 of 2004) and the Cybercrime Law (Law No. 14 of 2014, amended in 2025) criminalise broadly worded speech offences that apply to everyone. Migrants carry an extra risk that citizens do not: administrative deportation, and the loss of residence that their employer or recruiter controls under Law No. 21 of 2015. That makes their exposure heavier in practice, though the formal guarantee is the same.


## Peaceful assembly and association

### q87 · Right to freedom of association.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. The Permanent Constitution (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر, 2004, as amended in 2024), Article 45, guarantees only "The right of citizens to establish association … in accordance with the conditions and circumstances set forth in the law." Article 34 confines equality in public rights to citizens. Trade union rights are expressly reserved to nationals. The Labour Law (قانون رقم (14) لسنة 2004 بإصدار قانون العمل, Law No. 14 of 2004), Article 116, allows a "Workers Committee" only in an establishment where "the number of Qatari Workers is not less than one hundred". It then provides that "Membership in the two aforesaid committees and the General Union of Qatari Workers shall be limited to Qatari Workers." Migrants make up the vast majority of the workforce, and they cannot form or join any workers' organisation under the Labour Law. Domestic, farm and government workers are excluded from the Labour Law altogether (Article 3). For civil associations, Law No. 12 of 2004 on Private Associations and Foundations (قانون رقم (12) لسنة 2004 بشأن الجمعيات والمؤسسات الخاصة), Article 2, requires every founding or joining member to "Hold a Qatari nationality". The Council of Ministers may waive this only where the association's activity requires non-Qatari participation, and non-Qatari members may not exceed 20 percent. The joint labour committees introduced by ministerial regulation give migrant workers elected representation at enterprise level, but they are not trade unions and do not create a right of association. Migrants' freedom of association is therefore neither recognised nor commensurate with that of citizens.

### q89 · Right to freedom of assembly.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. The Permanent Constitution (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر, 2004, as amended in 2024), Article 44, guarantees assembly to nationals only: "The right of the citizens to assemble shall be guaranteed in accordance with the provisions of the law." Its Explanatory Memorandum presents the Part Three political freedoms, such as association (Article 45) and the press (Article 48), as "public rights and freedoms of the citizen". No other constitutional or statutory provision extends a right of peaceful assembly to non-citizens. The governing statute, Law No. 18 of 2004 on Public Meetings and Demonstrations (قانون رقم (18) لسنة 2004 بشأن الاجتماعات العامة والمسيرات), is a prior-licence regime rather than a rights guarantee. Article 1 defines a public meeting as any gathering of more than 20 persons to discuss public issues. Article 3 provides that "No convening, organisation, invitation, announcement or coverage of news on any public meeting shall take place without obtaining a licence". Articles 4-5 give the Director General for Security at the Ministry of Interior power to grant the licence; silence means refusal, and the Minister's decision on appeal is final. The statute does not exclude foreigners in terms. But it gives migrants no right to assemble, only a discretionary permission, and gatherings without a licence are unlawful. Because the constitutional right belongs to citizens alone, migrants' freedom of peaceful assembly is not recognised on a basis commensurate with citizens.


## Thought, conscience, and religion or belief

### q81 · Right of freedom of thought, conscience and religion or belief.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Qatar protects this right in the Permanent Constitution of the State of Qatar (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر, 2004, as amended in 2024), Article 50: "Freedom to practice religious rites shall be guaranteed to all persons in accordance with the law and the requirements of the maintenance of public order and morality." The guarantee covers "all persons". It is not limited to citizens, so migrants hold it on the same textual terms as Qataris. That sets it apart from neighbouring provisions such as Article 34 ("Citizens shall be equal in public rights and duties") and Articles 44-45 (assembly and association for citizens only). Article 35 also bars discrimination on grounds of religion for "all persons". The guarantee is narrow and qualified. It covers the practice of religious rites, not a full freedom of thought, conscience and belief, and it applies only "in accordance with the law" and public order and morality. In practice that brings in limits that fall mainly on non-Muslims, who are overwhelmingly migrants: non-Muslim groups must register to have an official presence; public worship and religious symbols are restricted; the Penal Code (Law No. 11 of 2004) punishes insulting religion; and apostasy from Islam is not recognised. These limits apply to citizens and non-citizens alike, so the MRD's commensurability test is met at the level of the constitutional text. The ICCPR, acceded to in 2018, has the force of law under Article 68 of the Constitution once ratified and published. Its effect is limited by Qatar's Sharia-related reservations and declarations.


## Victims of crime

### q43 · Right of victims of crime to access criminal proceedings.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes; Usually

National law in Qatar protects this right in the Code of Criminal Procedure promulgated by Law No. 23 of 2004 (قانون رقم (23) لسنة 2004 بإصدار قانون الإجراءات الجنائية), Articles 3, 19, 31 and 34, read with the Permanent Constitution of the State of Qatar (الدستور الدائم لدولة قطر), Articles 35 and 135. Under Article 3, for certain offences prosecution depends on a complaint "by the victim or whoever is legally representing him", made orally or in writing to the Public Prosecution or investigation officers. Article 31 obliges investigation officers to "accept reports and complaints referred to them about crimes". Article 34 allows "the defendant, his attorney-at-law, and the victim to attend these procedures whenever possible." Article 19 provides that "whoever experienced direct personal damage in consequence of a crime may sue the defendant in respect of his civil rights" before the criminal court during the investigation or at any stage of the trial. None of these provisions contains a nationality or residence condition. Constitution Article 135 guarantees the right of litigation "to all people", and Article 35 makes all persons equal before the law. Migrant victims therefore have the same standing as citizens to complain, to attend, and to join as civil claimants. For trafficking victims, Law No. 15 of 2011 adds the right to state their position (art. 6(2)), legal aid (art. 6(5)), compensation (art. 6(6)), and civil claims decided by the criminal court (art. 10).

### q45 · Right of victims of crime to immigration status or relief from expulsion.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Qatar protects this right, for victims of human trafficking only, in Law No. 15 of 2011 on combating human trafficking (قانون رقم (15) لسنة 2011 بشأن مكافحة الاتجار بالبشر), Articles 4, 5 and 6. Article 6 requires the competent authorities to ensure victims' rights, including "4- Remaining in the state's territory until the conclusion of investigation and trial", "5- Obtaining legal aid including counseling of attorney" and "7- Obtaining the necessary security protection." Article 4 shields the victim from "criminal or civil liability" for trafficking-related crimes "directly associated with such person as being a victim". This covers immigration offences that would otherwise lead to prosecution and removal. Article 5 obliges the authorities to protect victims, provide medical, educational and social care, and arrange a safe return home. The 2011 law thus gives trafficking victims relief from expulsion for the length of the criminal proceedings. According to the US State Department, amendments enacted in December 2024 go further. They require the authorities to regularise the residence of identified victims who wish to stay and work in Qatar, to facilitate their admission to the shelter, and to have the Ministry of Interior cover repatriation costs. There is no comparable avenue for migrant victims of other crimes, such as domestic violence, labour exploitation short of trafficking, or assault. For them, residence stays tied to the recruiter under Law No. 21 of 2015.


## Vulnerable migrants

### q3 · Right of child migrants to special protections.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes; Usually

National law in Qatar protects this right in Law No. 15 of 2017 on Domestic Workers (قانون رقم (15) لسنة 2017 بشأن المستخدمين في المنازل), article 5, and Law No. 15 of 2011 on Combating Human Trafficking (قانون رقم (15) لسنة 2011 بشأن مكافحة الاتجار بالبشر), articles 1, 3, 5 and 15. Article 5 of the Domestic Workers Law says: "It is prohibited to recruit and employ domestic workers of either sex who are under eighteen years of age or over sixty." The Minister may waive only the upper age limit. Article 2 of the same law applies the general rules on recruiting, medically examining and licensing the residence of expatriate workers, so the age bar operates within the labour-migration system. Nearly all domestic workers in Qatar are recruited from abroad. The Trafficking Law defines a child as anyone under 18 (art. 1). Article 2 covers recruitment and transport "across its national borders". Under article 3, coercive means need not be proven "for the commission of the crime of trafficking in children". Article 5 obliges the authorities to give victims care and rehabilitation "appropriate to their needs, human dignity, age and gender" and to return them home safely in cooperation with their countries. Article 15(1) raises the maximum penalty to 15 years where the victim is a child. Limits: the protection is narrow. There is no best-interests clause, and nothing in Law No. 21 of 2015 on the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015) protects children in particular. Article 12 of that law ties children's residence to the sponsoring parent (sons up to 25 and unmarried daughters). There is no rule on unaccompanied minors, no bar on detaining or deporting children, and Qatar has no general child-rights statute that addresses non-citizen children. The protections reach children only as trafficking victims or as prospective recruits.

### q5 · Right of women migrants to special protections.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Neither the immigration statute nor the labour-migration statutes contain a provision that protects vulnerable migrant women in particular. Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم) distinguishes by sex only in dependency terms. Article 12 lets unmarried daughters be sponsored without the age limit that applies to sons. Article 17 makes the head of family responsible for dependants and allows that responsibility to continue for women after they find work in Qatar. Neither provision offers protection against abuse, exploitation or loss of status. Law No. 15 of 2017 on Domestic Workers (قانون رقم (15) لسنة 2017 بشأن المستخدمين في المنازل) covers a mostly female migrant workforce but is sex-neutral. Its article 5, for example, applies to workers "of either sex", and the law has no gender-specific safeguards. The closest provisions are in Law No. 15 of 2011 on Combating Human Trafficking (قانون رقم (15) لسنة 2011 بشأن مكافحة الاتجار بالبشر). Article 5 requires victim care "appropriate to their needs, human dignity, age and gender", and article 15(1) raises the penalty where "the victim was a female". These are general criminal-law rules that protect female trafficking victims of any nationality. They do not provide a migration-specific protection, such as a status, residence permit or visa, for migrant women at risk compared with other migrants. The Labour Law's general rules on working women apply to migrant and Qatari women alike, so they are general protections and do not meet the indicator. Constitutional equality (art. 35) does not fill the gap, and no court decision applying it to migrant women was found.

### q7 · Rights of migrants with disabilities to special protection.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Qatar. Law No. 21 of 2015 regulating the entry, exit and residence of expatriates (قانون رقم (21) لسنة 2015 بتنظيم دخول وخروج الوافدين وإقامتهم) contains no provision for migrants with disabilities. Nothing was found on accommodation in immigration procedures, residence on humanitarian or disability grounds, exemption from deportation, or extending residence to carers. The system works the other way. Under article 2 of Law No. 15 of 2017 on Domestic Workers (قانون رقم (15) لسنة 2017 بشأن المستخدمين في المنازل), the rules on recruiting expatriate workers, "examining them medically" and licensing their residence apply to domestic workers. Residence therefore depends on medical fitness, as it does for other expatriate workers. The only express references to disability that bear on migrants are in Law No. 15 of 2011 on Combating Human Trafficking (قانون رقم (15) لسنة 2011 بشأن مكافحة الاتجار بالبشر). Article 3 makes coercive means unnecessary for trafficking in "persons who lack capacity". Article 15(1) raises the maximum penalty to 15 years where the victim is "a person with disabilities", and article 5 requires care suited to victims' needs. These are general criminal-law rules that protect disabled trafficking victims of any nationality. They create no migration-specific protection for disabled migrants compared with other migrants. The Law No. 11 of 2018 on political asylum (قانون رقم (11) لسنة 2018), as summarised, lists health care among refugee rights but has no provision on disability. Constitutional equality (art. 35) does not list disability and has not been applied by courts to disabled migrants.
