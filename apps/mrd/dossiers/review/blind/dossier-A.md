# Dossier A — Philippines


## Civil and political life

### q91 · Right of regular migrants to vote in local and/or regional elections.
- Law: No right to vote in any election
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. Under the 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines (Saligang Batas ng Republika ng Pilipinas), Article V, Section 1, the vote belongs to citizens only: "Suffrage may be exercised by all citizens of the Philippines, not otherwise disqualified by law, who are at least eighteen years of age, and who shall have resided in the Philippines for at least one year and in the place wherein they propose to vote, for at least six months." Statute repeats the citizenship requirement. The Omnibus Election Code (Batas Pambansa Blg. 881), Section 117, says that "Every citizen of the Philippines ... may be registered as a voter". The Voter's Registration Act of 1996 (Republic Act No. 8189), Section 9, likewise limits registration to "All citizens of the Philippines not otherwise disqualified by law". The Omnibus Election Code governs "all election of public officers" (Section 2), so the bar covers national, provincial, city, municipal and barangay elections. No statute gives a separate local franchise to foreigners. Lawful residence, permanent residence (for example, Section 13 immigrant visas under Commonwealth Act No. 613) and length of stay make no difference. The law goes further and restricts foreigners' political participation. Section 81 of the Omnibus Election Code makes it unlawful "for any foreigner ... to aid any candidate or political party, directly or indirectly, or take part in or influence in any manner any election". Section 95(h) bars contributions from foreigners. A migrant can vote only after naturalization under Commonwealth Act No. 473 or Republic Act No. 9139, which makes them a citizen.

### q93 · Right of regular migrants to stand for election.
- Law: No right to run for public office in any election
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. Every elective office requires Philippine citizenship. Under the 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines (Saligang Batas ng Republika ng Pilipinas), the President and Vice-President must be "a natural-born citizen of the Philippines, a registered voter" (Art. VII, Secs. 2-3). Senators must be natural-born citizens (Art. VI, Sec. 3), and so must Members of the House of Representatives (Art. VI, Sec. 6). The same rule applies locally. The Local Government Code of 1991 (Republic Act No. 7160), Section 39(a), provides that "An elective local official must be a citizen of the Philippines; a registered voter in the barangay, municipality, city, or province". This covers governors, mayors, members of the sanggunian (local councils) and barangay officials. Every candidate must also be a registered voter, and only citizens can register (Constitution Art. V, Sec. 1; Omnibus Election Code, Batas Pambansa Blg. 881, Sec. 117). This adds a second bar. Lawful presence, permanent residence or long residence gives a non-citizen no right to stand for office at any level. Foreigners are also barred from aiding candidates or influencing elections (Omnibus Election Code, Sec. 81). Only migrants who naturalize can run, and then only for local office, since national elective offices require natural-born status. Courts enforce the rule. In 2025 the Manila Regional Trial Court granted the Solicitor General's quo warranto petition and voided the 2022 proclamation of Bamban mayor Alice Guo after finding she was the Chinese national Guo Hua Ping.


## Due process

### q35 · Right to equal protection of the law before courts.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in the 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines (Saligang Batas ng Republika ng Pilipinas), Article III (Bill of Rights), Sections 1, 11, 14 and 16. Section 1 provides that "No person shall be deprived of life, liberty, or property without due process of law, nor shall any person be denied the equal protection of the laws." Section 11 guarantees that "free access to the courts and quasi-judicial bodies and adequate legal assistance shall not be denied to any person by reason of poverty". Section 16 gives "all persons" the right to a speedy disposition of their cases "before all judicial, quasi-judicial, or administrative bodies." Section 14 sets out the rights of "the accused" in criminal prosecutions without any nationality condition. These guarantees are framed for "any person" or "all persons", not for citizens, so they protect migrants whatever their status. The Supreme Court has applied the Bill of Rights to foreigners. In Mejoff v. Director of Prisons (G.R. No. L-4254, 1951) it held that the protection against deprivation of liberty without due process "is not limited to Philippine citizens but extends to all residents, except enemy aliens, regardless of nationality", relying also on the incorporation of international law (now Art. II §2). This gives migrants equal standing before administrative, civil and criminal courts and tribunals. There are limits. Constitutional nationality reservations (suffrage, professions, parts of the economy) are substantive exclusions, not denials of equal protection before tribunals. Deportation is decided administratively by the Bureau of Immigration Board of Commissioners under Commonwealth Act No. 613, Sec. 37, and judicial review is available through habeas corpus and certiorari.

### q37 · Right to government-provided counsel in proceedings related to migrant status.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. No statute or regulation gives migrants a right to counsel at government expense in proceedings on their immigration status: exclusion, deportation before the Bureau of Immigration (BI) Board of Commissioners under Commonwealth Act No. 613 (Philippine Immigration Act of 1940), Sec. 37, or visa cancellation. The closest provisions are general. Article III, Section 11 of the 1987 Constitution states that "free access to the courts and quasi-judicial bodies and adequate legal assistance shall not be denied to any person by reason of poverty". Republic Act No. 9406 (2007), Sec. 3, inserting Sec. 14-A in the Administrative Code, directs the Public Attorney's Office (PAO) to "render, free of charge, legal representation, assistance, and counselling to indigent persons in criminal, civil, labor, administrative and other quasi-judicial cases." Neither provision creates an entitlement specific to migration-status proceedings. PAO help depends on meeting indigency tests and on PAO taking the case. The mandatory, state-provided counsel guaranteed by Art. III §12 and Republic Act No. 7438, Sec. 2(b), covers only persons under custodial investigation "for the commission of an offense", not administrative immigration proceedings. Executive Order No. 163 (2022), Sec. 1, sets a policy of "legal assistance and access to courts" for refugees, stateless persons and asylum seekers. That is a policy commitment for this group only, not an enforceable right to free counsel for migrants generally.

### q39 · Right to government-provided interpretation in criminal proceedings.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Philippines protects this right in the 1987 Constitution, Article III, Section 14(2); the Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure, Rule 116, Section 1(a); and Republic Act No. 7438 (1992), Section 2(b)-(c). Article III §14(2) gives every accused the right "to be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation against him" and "to be heard by himself and counsel". Rule 116 §1(a) gives effect to this: arraignment is made "by furnishing the accused with a copy of the complaint or information, reading the same in the language or dialect known to him". Interpretation is therefore a duty of the court, carried out through court interpreters, who are judiciary personnel. The accused does not pay for it. RA 7438 §2(b) requires arresting and investigating officers to inform any person "in a language known to and understood by him" of the right to silence and to counsel. §2(c) makes a custodial investigation report "null and void" unless it was read and explained "in the language or dialect known to such arrested or detained person". For foreign trafficking victims, Republic Act No. 9208, Sec. 19, as amended by RA 11862 (2022), Sec. 12, adds an entitlement to "the provision of interpreters". These rules apply to every accused, citizen or not. The limit is that no provision expressly says "at government expense" or provides a free interpreter for every stage and every foreign language. Cost-free provision follows from the court's duty and court staffing. Supreme Court accreditation of interpreters constrains availability for uncommon foreign languages.

### q41 · Right to be protected from prosecution for unauthorized entry.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. Unauthorized entry is a crime under Commonwealth Act No. 613 (Philippine Immigration Act of 1940), Section 45. The section penalises any individual who, "being an alien, enters the Philippines at any time or place other than as designated by the immigration officials, or obtains entry into the Philippines by willful, false, or misleading representation or willful concealment of a material fact". It also covers using false immigration documents and attempts or conspiracies. Such a person "shall be guilty of an offense, and upon conviction thereof, shall be fined not more than one thousand pesos, or imprisonment for not more than two years, or both." Section 46 separately criminalises bringing in, concealing or harbouring aliens not duly admitted. Unauthorized entry is also an administrative ground for arrest and deportation: Section 37(a)(1) covers "any alien who enters the Philippines ... by means of false and misleading statements or without inspection and admission by the immigration authorities at a designated port of entry". The anti-trafficking law gives partial relief. Republic Act No. 9208, Section 17, provides that trafficked persons "shall not be penalized for crimes directly related to the acts of trafficking", which can cover irregular entry procured by traffickers. Asylum seekers may rely on non-refoulement and the 1951 Refugee Convention as applied by the DOJ (EO 163; Sabir v. DOJ-RSPPU, 2022). No statute exempts migrants generally from criminal sanctions for unauthorized entry.


## Education

### q127 · Right to equal access to public adult education institutions.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. Republic Act No. 10931 (Universal Access to Quality Tertiary Education Act, 2017) is the main statute on free public tertiary and technical-vocational education. It declares that "quality education is an inalienable right of all Filipinos" (Sec. 2). Sec. 4 grants free tuition and other school fees in state universities and colleges (SUCs) and local universities and colleges (LUCs) only to "All Filipino students". The Act also covers state-run technical-vocational institutions, so non-citizens are excluded from the subsidy that defines citizens' access to public degree-level and vocational education. Article XIV of the 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines (Saligang Batas ng Republika ng Pilipinas) speaks of "the right of all citizens to quality education at all levels" (Sec. 1). Sec. 2(5) directs the State to "provide adult citizens, the disabled, and out-of-school youth with training in civics, vocational efficiency, and other skills". Foreign nationals may enrol in public universities if they hold a Sec. 9(f) student visa or a Bureau of Immigration Special Study Permit under the Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613), but they pay full fees and are not eligible for the free-tuition programme. Executive Order No. 163 (2022), Sec. 1, assures refugees, stateless persons and asylum seekers general "access to ... education". The implementing Technical Education and Skills Development Authority (TESDA) Circular No. 24, s. 2018 reportedly limits their access to programmes "not subsidized by government funds". Access is therefore not commensurate with citizens for any migrant category.


## Equal protection

### q131 · Right to be free from discrimination on the basis of migrant status.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. The 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines (Saligang Batas ng Republika ng Pilipinas), Article III, Section 1, provides that no person shall "be denied the equal protection of the laws." This is a general equality clause and does not list migration status or nationality as a prohibited ground. The Constitution itself draws many citizenship-based distinctions, such as suffrage, professions, land and mass media. Article XIII, Section 3 commits the State to "equality of employment opportunities for all" as a policy, not as a ban on discrimination against migrants. There is no comprehensive anti-discrimination statute; anti-discrimination bills remain pending in Congress. No statute prohibits discrimination on the ground of being a migrant or of immigration status. Sectoral laws, such as the Universal Health Care Act's automatic coverage of "every Filipino citizen", the Labor Code's permit and reciprocity conditions for foreign workers' union rights (Art. 269/284), and the employment-permit regime (Art. 40), themselves distinguish on the basis of nationality and status. Executive Order No. 163 (2022) commits the State to minimum standards of treatment for refugees, stateless persons and asylum seekers, but it contains no non-discrimination clause and covers only those groups. ICERD (ratified 1967) and the ICRMW (Art. 7, ratified 1995) have statute rank, but no implementing legislation turns them into an enforceable ban on discrimination against migrants because of their status.


## Expulsion, asylum, and non-refoulement

### q47 · Right to maintain immigration status after convictions.
- Law: any conviction (even minor administrative offenses) can lead to loss of residence status
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. Commonwealth Act No. 613 (Philippine Immigration Act of 1940, as amended), Sec. 37(a), lists the grounds on which the Board of Commissioners of the Bureau of Immigration (BI) may arrest and deport "any alien". These grounds cover all foreigners, including permanent residents. Some require a serious conviction. Clause 3 covers an alien "convicted in the Philippines and sentenced for a term of one year or more for a crime involving moral turpitude committed within five years after his entry", or so convicted "more than once". Others do not. Clause 4 covers any alien "convicted and sentenced for a violation of the law governing traffic in prohibited drugs", whatever the sentence. Clause 7 covers an alien who "remains in the Philippines in violation of any limitation or condition under which he was admitted as a nonimmigrant", which is a purely administrative breach. Clause 5 (prostitution) and clause 8 (subversive advocacy) need no conviction at all. RA 503 added further grounds that are not in the base text on lawphil; secondary sources describe them as including repeated convictions under the Alien Registration Act and profiteering "independent of any criminal action". There are limited safeguards. Sec. 37(b) bars deportation on most grounds unless the arrest is made within five years after the cause arises. It also lets the sentencing court recommend against deportation for clauses 3 and 4. Sec. 37(c) requires notice of the grounds and a hearing. Beyond the statutory list, the BI routinely declares foreigners "undesirable aliens" because of their conduct, then deports and blacklists them, even for petty public-order acts. This works much like a character test. No proportionality test or residence-length protection shields long-term residents.

### q49 · Right to not return to a risk of torture or inhumane treatment.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in Republic Act No. 9745 (Anti-Torture Act of 2009), Sec. 17, "Applicability of Refouler". It provides that "No person shall be expelled, returned or extradited to another State where there are substantial grounds to believe that such person shall be in danger of being subjected to torture." To decide whether such grounds exist, the Secretaries of Foreign Affairs and Justice, in coordination with the Chairperson of the Commission on Human Rights (CHR), must "take into account all relevant considerations including ... the existence in the requesting State of a consistent pattern of gross, flagrant or mass violations of human rights." The ban is absolute and covers any person, citizen or foreigner, whatever their immigration status, criminal record or refugee claim. It applies to expulsion, return and extradition alike, so it limits deportation by the Bureau of Immigration under Commonwealth Act No. 613, Sec. 37, as well as extradition. The statute's wording is limited to torture. It does not expressly mention cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment, although RA 9745 separately criminalises "other cruel, inhuman and degrading treatment". No dedicated procedure exists for raising a torture-risk claim before removal. In practice such claims go through DOJ Department Circular No. 024 (2022), which governs refugee status determination by the DOJ Refugees and Stateless Persons Protection Unit (RSPPU). Rule IV Sec. 4 of that circular protects applicants from "any forcible return in any manner whatsoever" until their case is finally decided. They can also go to the courts through habeas corpus or the writ of amparo. The Supreme Court in Sabir v. DOJ-RSPPU (G.R. No. 249387, 2022; Resolution 2023) treated non-refoulement as binding on the State.

### q51 · Right to not return to a risk of persecution.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in Executive Order No. 163 (2022), DOJ Department Circular No. 024 (2022) and the directly applied 1951 Refugee Convention. EO 163, which institutionalises protection services for refugees, stateless persons and asylum seekers, defines the principle in Sec. 2(c). Non-refoulement is "a principle of customary law which provides that no State shall expel or return ('refouler') a refugee in any manner whatsoever, to the frontiers of territories where his or her life or freedom would be threatened on account of his or her race, religion, nationality, membership in a particular social group or political opinion." Sec. 1 commits the State to "ensure full protection of the rights" of these persons of concern. DC 024-2022 (Strengthening the Refugees and Stateless Persons Protection Unit, Enhancing the Rules for Refugee and Stateless Status Determination) has been in force since 19 August 2022. Rule I Sec. 3(a) expressly applies non-refoulement. Rule IV Sec. 4 gives every applicant, from filing until the decision is final, "protection from any forcible return in any manner whatsoever, to a country where he or she may face persecution on account of his or her race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion." Under the procedure, deportation cases against an applicant are suspended while the claim is examined. The Supreme Court in Sabir v. DOJ-RSPPU (G.R. No. 249387, 2022; Resolution 2023) held that status determination must follow the Convention and the UNHCR Handbook, and that officials must act "in accordance with the principle of non-refoulement." Protection covers recognised refugees and asylum seekers. Only Art. 33(2)-type security exceptions under the Convention apply. No statute codifies the ban; it rests on executive and DOJ issuances and the Convention, which the Philippines acceded to in 1981.

### q53 · Right to not return to a deprivations of fundamental human rights.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Philippines protects this right, though weakly, in DOJ Department Circular No. 024 (2022) read with the Constitution's incorporation clause. The Supreme Court described the circular in Sabir v. DOJ-RSPPU (G.R. No. 249387, Resolution of 8 March 2023). It said Rule I Sec. 3(a) "expressly acknowledges the application of the principle of non-refoulement". It also "recognizes that, pursuant to customary international law, the principle of non-refoulement may apply to other individuals whose lives or freedoms may be threatened", meaning people beyond Convention refugees. The 1987 Constitution, Art. II §2, makes "generally accepted principles of international law" part of the law of the land. That brings in customary non-refoulement and the ICCPR-based ban on return to a real risk to life. Executive Order No. 163 (2022), Sec. 1, commits the State to "ensure full protection of the rights" of refugees, stateless persons and asylum seekers "to liberty and security". The protection has clear limits. No statute or regulation sets up complementary or humanitarian protection status for people at risk of the death penalty, extrajudicial killing or other serious harm unconnected to a Convention ground. The operative protection in DC 024 Rule IV Sec. 4 against "forcible return" during the procedure is tied to persecution on the five Convention grounds. Republic Act No. 9745 (Anti-Torture Act), Sec. 17, covers torture only. Commonwealth Act No. 613, Sec. 37, contains no general human-rights bar to deportation. The DOJ, the Bureau of Immigration or the courts (through habeas corpus or the writ of amparo, which protects life, liberty and security) would therefore have to apply protection for people outside the Convention case by case.

### q55 · Right to be advised of availability of asylum before expulsion.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. No statute, regulation or court decision requires immigration officers to tell a foreigner that asylum may be available before refusing entry or deporting them. The Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613) sets out exclusion by boards of special inquiry (Secs. 27-28) and deportation by the Board of Commissioners (Sec. 37). Neither procedure includes any duty to inform about asylum. Section 47(b) only lets the President admit refugees "for religious, political, or racial reasons". Department of Justice (DOJ) Department Circular No. 024, s. 2022, which governs refugee and stateless status determination, gives an applicant the right "to be informed and have access to the procedure" (Sec. 4(b)). That right runs only "from the time of the filing of the Application". It protects people already inside the procedure. It does not require officials to tell a person facing exclusion or deportation that they could claim asylum. The circular, as reported, lets a claim be lodged with the Bureau of Immigration (BI) at the port of entry, but it places no duty on officers to raise the possibility. Executive Order No. 163 (2022) defines non-refoulement and commits agencies, including the BI, to providing protection services to asylum seekers, refugees and stateless persons. It contains no information-on-asylum duty either. Practitioner guidance describes BI officers referring people who ask for protection at the airport to the Refugees and Stateless Persons Protection Unit (RSPPU). This depends on the person raising the claim, not on a legal duty to advise.

### q57 · Right to be protected from chain refoulement.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. Philippine law has no explicit ban on sending a person to a country that would then send them on to persecution or torture (chain or indirect refoulement). The Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613), Sec. 38, lets the Commissioner remove a deportee "to the country whence he came, or to the foreign port at which he embarked for the Philippines, or to the country of his nativity or of which he is a citizen or subject", or to other countries. It says nothing about whether the receiving country might expel the person onward. The Anti-Torture Act of 2009 (Republic Act No. 9745), Sec. 17, bars expelling, returning or extraditing anyone "to another State where there are substantial grounds to believe that such person shall be in danger of being subjected to torture". That covers only direct return to the state where torture is feared. Executive Order No. 163 (2022), Sec. 2(c), defines non-refoulement as a ban on returning a refugee "to the frontiers of territories where his or her life or freedom would be threatened". Department of Justice (DOJ) Department Circular No. 024, s. 2022, Sec. 4(f), protects applicants from "any forcible return in any manner whatsoever" to a country where they may face persecution. The words "in any manner whatsoever" could be read to reach indirect removal, but neither the circular nor any court has said that a third country must be checked for onward refoulement. Sabir v. DOJ-RSPPU (2022/2023) confirms that non-refoulement binds the Philippines but does not deal with third-country or chain removal.

### q59 · Right to protection against expulsion except following a legal judgment.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613), Sec. 37(a) and (c), read with the due process clause of the 1987 Constitution, Art. III §1. Under Sec. 37(a), aliens in the statutory categories may be "deported upon the warrant of the Commissioner of Immigration after a determination by the Board of Commissioners of the existence of the grounds for deportation as charged against the alien". Those categories include entry by fraud or without inspection, certain criminal convictions and overstaying. Deportation therefore needs an individual finding, by the competent body, that a ground set by law exists in the person's case. Sec. 37(b) adds time limits for most grounds and lets a sentencing court recommend against deportation. Sec. 37(c) requires that the alien be "informed of the specific grounds for deportation". In Domingo v. Scheer (G.R. No. 154745, 29 January 2004) the Supreme Court annulled a Board summary deportation order. The Board had "simply concluded that the respondent committed insurance fraud and illegal activities... without any evidence". The Court held that an alien "cannot... be arrested and deported without due process of law as required by the Bill of Rights". It also confirmed that courts may review deportation orders for lack of evidence or arbitrariness. The protection covers all aliens subject to deportation, whether regular or irregular. Summary procedures under BI rules for undocumented or overstaying aliens still require an individual Board decision. The separate executive power of the President to deport persists, but it too is exercised through individual orders based on investigation.

### q61 · Right to submit reasons against expulsion and have case reviewed before deportation.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes; Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613), Sec. 37(c), (d) and (e), together with the Bureau of Immigration (BI) procedural rules and the 1987 Constitution, Art. III §1. Sec. 37(c) provides that "no alien shall be deported without being informed of the specific grounds for deportation nor without being given a hearing under rules of procedure to be prescribed by the Commissioner of Immigration." In entry-related cases Sec. 37(d) gives the alien a right to the BI's records of their arrival. Sec. 37(e) allows release on bond while proceedings are pending. The BI's Office Memorandum Order No. 34, quoted by the Supreme Court in Domingo v. Scheer (G.R. No. 154745, 2004), guarantees in all cases "the right of the alien to be informed of the charges against him, to be notified of the time and place of hearing, when necessary, to examine the evidence against him, and to present evidence in his own behalf". Cases other than the summary categories go to a full deportation hearing. A Board of Commissioners order may be challenged by motion for reconsideration and then reviewed in the courts by certiorari or prohibition in the Court of Appeals, as in Domingo v. Scheer, where a deportation order was annulled for lack of a hearing. For exclusion at the border, Sec. 27(c) gives an excluded alien an appeal to the Board of Commissioners, with counsel who has access to the record. Asylum applicants also have a reconsideration request and an appeal to the Office of the President under DOJ Department Circular No. 024, s. 2022. Under the BI rules, summary deportation of undocumented or overstaying aliens has a reduced hearing.


## Family

### q73 · Right of settled migrants to family reunification.
- Law: Conditional
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines conditionally protects this right in the Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613, as amended by Republic Act No. 503), Sections 9(d), 9(g), 13 and 19. Sec. 13 caps 'quota immigrants' at 'fifty (50) of any one nationality or without nationality for any one calendar year'. Under Sec. 19, when quota numbers are allotted, the Commissioner of Immigration 'shall accord preference to … the wives, husbands, and unmarried children under twenty-one years of age, of aliens lawfully admitted into the Philippines for permanent residence and residing therein', on petition. Temporary residents have a separate route. Sec. 9(g) admits an alien coming to prearranged employment 'and his wife, and his unmarried children under twenty-one years of age, if accompanying him or if following to join him within a period of six months'. Sec. 9(d) does the same for treaty traders and investors, subject to reciprocity. The non-quota route in Sec. 13(f) for the families of permanent residents only covered aliens admitted before the 1940 Act took effect, so it no longer applies. Sec. 13(a) non-quota status is limited to the spouses and children of Philippine citizens. These avenues are therefore conditional. They cover only a spouse and unmarried children under 21. Permanent residents' families must compete for the annual 50-per-nationality quota, with preference but no entitlement. The statute gives no route for non-dependent relatives (parents, siblings, adult children) of foreign residents. The general exclusion grounds in Sec. 29 also apply, including the public-charge grounds.

### q75 · Right to judicial remedy for denials of right to family reunification.
- Law: Conditional
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Philippines conditionally protects this right through general judicial review of Bureau of Immigration decisions under Rule 43 of the Rules of Court, as applied by the Supreme Court in Tze Sun Wong v. Kenny Wong, G.R. No. 180364 (3 December 2014). That decision confirms that decisions of the BI Board of Commissioners may be appealed to the Court of Appeals by petition for review under Rule 43. It relies on Dwikarna v. Domingo: 'If his motion is denied, then he can elevate his case by way of a petition for review before the Court of Appeals, pursuant to Section 1, Rule 43'. Because the Bureau sits under the Department of Justice, a party must first exhaust administrative remedies by appealing to the Secretary of Justice, and may go on to the Office of the President, before going to court. Certiorari to the Court of Appeals is available 'strictly on jurisdictional grounds' (Kiani v. BID, cited in Tze Sun Wong). The Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613) contains no specific judicial appeal against refusal of a family-based visa. Sec. 27(c) makes the Board of Commissioners' decision on appeal from an exclusion at the border 'final'. Visa refusals by consular officers abroad have no stated remedy. The courts treat admission of aliens as a privilege and a matter of grace, which narrows review. The remedy therefore exists only through general administrative-law channels, after exhaustion, and with deference to the Bureau.

### q77 · Right to recognition of same-sex marital relationships.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. The family-reunification provisions of the Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613) refer to 'his wife' (Secs. 9(d), 9(g)) and to 'wives, husbands' (Sec. 19). Those terms are read against the Family Code of the Philippines (Executive Order No. 209, 1987). Article 1 of the Family Code defines marriage as 'a special contract of permanent union between a man and a woman'. Article 2 requires 'legal capacity of the contracting parties who must be a male and a female'. Article 26 recognises marriages validly celebrated abroad, but it does so within this framework. No statute, regulation or Bureau of Immigration issuance recognises same-sex spouses or partners as dependants for any visa. The Supreme Court dismissed a direct challenge to Articles 1 and 2 in Falcis v. Civil Registrar-General, G.R. No. 217910 (2019), leaving the opposite-sex definition in force. A same-sex spouse of a resident foreigner therefore cannot obtain a dependant or spousal visa and must qualify for a visa in their own right. There is also no civil-union or partner category.

### q79 · Right to family reunification without resource conditions
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. The Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613) sets no numeric income or insurance threshold for family members. It does attach resource conditions to every admission. Sec. 29(a) requires exclusion of '5. Persons likely to become a public charge' and '6. Paupers, vagrants, and beggars'. Sec. 37(a)(6) makes deportable an alien 'who becomes a public charge within five years after entry'. Sec. 40(a)(2) lets the Commissioner of Immigration demand bonds 'to insure against alien passengers liable to be excluded as likely to become public charges'. These grounds apply to the spouses and children admitted under Secs. 9(d), 9(g) and 13. The quota route that permanent residents' family members must use (Secs. 13 and 19) requires proof of financial capacity to support the applicant and any dependants. Family reunification is therefore not available regardless of resources. Applicants must show they will not become a public charge and may be required to post a bond.

### q137 · Right to citizenship for stateless migrants.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in the Philippines. The 1987 Constitution, Art. IV, Sec. 1, grants citizenship by descent: "those whose fathers or mothers are citizens of the Philippines". It has no rule granting nationality to children born in the territory who would otherwise be stateless, or to stateless migrants. The nearest protection is the Foundling Recognition and Protection Act (Republic Act No. 11767, 2022): - **Foundlings.** Sec. 5 provides that "a foundling found in the Philippines ... is presumed a natural-born Filipino citizen regardless of the status or circumstances of birth." - **Parentage later established.** Sec. 11 provides that if a foundling's parentage is established but the foundling "cannot acquire the citizenship of the parents which will result in statelessness, the foundling shall retain Philippine citizenship." - **Limit.** These safeguards cover only children whose parents are unknown. Children born in the Philippines to known stateless or undocumented parents get no automatic Philippine nationality, for example the descendants of Indonesian migrants and the Sama-Bajau in Mindanao. Other routes facilitate naturalization but do not guarantee it: - **Facilitated naturalization.** Stateless persons recognized under DOJ Department Circular No. 024 (2022) can use the Supreme Court's Rule on Facilitated Naturalization of Refugees and Stateless Persons (A.M. No. 21-07-22-SC, 2022). It eases documentation, publication and fees, but the petitioner must still meet all the qualifications of Commonwealth Act No. 473, including residence, character, property or occupation and language. The court decides at its discretion. - **Administrative naturalization.** Republic Act No. 9139 is limited to persons born and resident in the Philippines since birth who meet education and income tests. The Philippines acceded to the 1961 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness in 2022. Its Art. 1 safeguard for otherwise-stateless children born in the territory has not been put into statute, and the treaty is not self-executing on this point.


## Health

### q119 · Right of access to health care services.
- Law: Yes, but only for certain visa types and migrant statuses (e.g., economic, family, humanitarian, temporary or regular migrants)
- Implementation: Sometimes; Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right only partially, in the Universal Health Care Act (Republic Act No. 11223), Section 8 and its Implementing Rules and Regulations (IRR), Section 8.2; Executive Order No. 163 (2022), Section 1; and Batas Pambansa Blg. 702 as amended by Republic Act No. 10932 (Anti-Hospital Deposit Law), Section 1. RA 11223 is framed around citizens: Section 5 provides that "Every Filipino citizen shall be automatically included into the NHIP" and Section 6 grants "Every Filipino" immediate eligibility for preventive, curative and emergency services. Non-citizens therefore get no automatic or subsidised coverage. They can join the National Health Insurance Program (NHIP) as direct contributors. Under IRR Section 8.2, direct contributors are "composed of, but not limited to" employees "with formal employment characterized by the existence of an employer-employee relationship", whatever their nationality. PhilHealth in practice enrols "citizens of other countries working/residing/studying in the Philippines" who hold an Alien Certificate of Registration (ACR) I-Card, but they must pay their own premiums. EO 163 assures refugees, stateless persons and asylum seekers "access to socioeconomic services, social security benefits", and PhilHealth has agreed to enrol them through its Sponsored Program or group enrolment. Irregular migrants have no access to the NHIP. They are protected only in emergencies: the Anti-Hospital Deposit Law makes it unlawful to demand a deposit "as a prerequisite for administering basic emergency care to any patient". Access commensurate with citizens therefore exists only for lawfully resident and employed migrants and for persons of concern, and even for them it is contributory rather than automatic.

### q123 · Right of access to public housing programs.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. The main statute on publicly supported housing is the Urban Development and Housing Act of 1992 (Republic Act No. 7279). It expressly limits socialized housing to citizens. Section 16 sets the eligibility criteria for socialized housing program beneficiaries and provides that a beneficiary "Must be a Filipino citizen" and "Must be an underprivileged and homeless citizen". Section 3(t) defines the Act's beneficiaries as "underprivileged and homeless citizens", and Section 3(r) defines socialized housing as programs "for the underprivileged and homeless citizens". Resettlement areas under Section 3(n) are likewise reserved for "underprivileged and homeless citizens". Non-citizens are thus excluded by law from the National Housing Authority's socialized housing, resettlement and related programs, whatever their visa type or residence status. Executive Order No. 163 (2022) makes the General Manager of the National Housing Authority a member of the Inter-Agency Committee on the Protection of Refugees, Stateless Persons and Asylum Seekers. It also commits the State in general terms to the minimum standards of treatment of refugees, including access to socioeconomic services. However, it creates no entitlement to public housing and cannot override the citizenship requirement in RA 7279. I found no court decision opening public housing to migrants. The constitutional reservation of land ownership to citizens (Art. XII) further limits housing-related programs tied to land titling.

### q125 · Right to equal access to free primary education for migrant children.
- Law: Yes, but only for certain visa types and migrant statuses (e.g., economic, family, humanitarian, temporary or regular migrants)
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right for lawfully present migrant children and for refugees, stateless persons and asylum seekers in the 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines (Saligang Batas ng Republika ng Pilipinas), Art. XIV, Sec. 2(2), and Executive Order No. 163 (2022). Article XIV, Sec. 2(2) obliges the State to "establish and maintain a system of free public education in the elementary and high school levels" and provides that "elementary education is compulsory for all children of school age". This mandate is not limited to citizens, unlike Sec. 1, which speaks of "the right of all citizens to quality education". Republic Act No. 10533 (Enhanced Basic Education Act of 2013), Sec. 2, commits the State to a complete basic education system "relevant to the needs of the people", with no nationality condition on enrolment in tuition-free public schools. For refugees, stateless persons and asylum seekers, EO 163, Sec. 1, assures "access to ... education" as part of the minimum standards of treatment, and names the Department of Education (DepEd) to the inter-agency committee (Sec. 3). Access is in practice conditional on immigration status. Under the Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613) framework and Bureau of Immigration (BI) Memorandum Circular No. SBM-2015-007, a foreign child below 18 who enrols in preschool, elementary or high school needs a BI Special Study Permit (SSP). The SSP is issued together with a valid temporary visitor or other authorised stay, so children without lawful status cannot obtain one. Children holding certain resident or dependent statuses may be exempt. Irregular migrant children therefore have no guaranteed access on equal terms with citizens.


## Labor

### q95 · Right to equal pay for the same work.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right through the 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines (Saligang Batas ng Republika ng Pilipinas), Art. XIII §3, the Labor Code of the Philippines (Presidential Decree No. 442, as amended), Art. 3, and the binding Supreme Court decision in International School Alliance of Educators v. Quisumbing, G.R. No. 128845 (1 June 2000). Under Art. XIII §3 the State shall "afford full protection to labor, local and overseas, organized and unorganized, and promote full employment and equality of employment opportunities for all" and guarantee "humane conditions of work, and a living wage." Labor Code Art. 3 directs the State to "ensure equal work opportunities regardless of sex, race or creed." In ISAE v. Quisumbing the Supreme Court struck down a pay scale that paid locally hired teachers less than foreign hires for the same work. It read these provisions together with Art. 7 of the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (ICESCR), which guarantees "fair wages and equal remuneration for work of equal value without distinction of any kind." The Court held that "persons who work with substantially equal qualifications, skill, effort and responsibility, under similar conditions, should be paid similar salaries." It added that an employer who gives employees the same position and rank must justify any pay gap. The principle is nationality-neutral and binds every employer, so it protects foreign workers holding an Alien Employment Permit (AEP) as well as citizens. The Labor Code's minimum-wage and wage-protection rules apply to all employees without distinction of nationality. No statute specifically prohibits pay discrimination on grounds of nationality or migrant status; the only express equal-pay offence in the Labor Code (Art. 135) concerns sex. The protection therefore rests on the constitutional labour clause and binding case law.

### q97 · Right to freedom of choice of employment.
- Law: Employment is tied to specific employer; change of employer possible, but requires new work permit application
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right only in a limited way, in the Labor Code of the Philippines (Presidential Decree No. 442, as amended), Arts. 40-41, and the alien employment permit rules (DOLE Department Order No. 248, series of 2025, as amended). Art. 40 requires any alien seeking admission for employment, and any employer wishing to engage one, to "obtain an employment permit from the Department of Labor." The permit is issued only after "a determination of the non-availability of a person in the Philippines who is competent, able and willing" to do the job, a labour-market test. Art. 41 provides that "after the issuance of an employment permit, the alien shall not transfer to another job or change his employer without prior approval of the Secretary of Labor." Working in breach of this is punishable and leads to deportation after the sentence is served. Under DO 248-25, an Alien Employment Permit (AEP) is valid only for the position and employer for which it is issued. An additional position is allowed only with the same employer or a related company, and only after a fresh labour-market test and publication. A foreign worker can therefore change employer only by having the new employer obtain a new AEP, and in practice a new or amended 9(g) pre-arranged employment visa. Exemptions apply to resident-visa holders (such as 13(a) spouses of Filipinos), recognised refugees and stateless persons, and certain diplomatic and international-organisation staff. These groups need only a Certificate of Exemption and are not tied to one employer. Constitutional nationality reservations under Art. XII also close certain professions and economic activities to foreigners.

### q99 · Right of spouse/partner of principal migrant to work.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. Under the Labor Code of the Philippines (Presidential Decree No. 442, as amended), Art. 40, "any alien seeking admission to the Philippines for employment purposes and any domestic or foreign employer who desires to engage an alien" must obtain an employment permit from the Department of Labor and Employment (DOLE). The permit follows a labour-market test showing that no competent, able and willing person in the Philippines is available. The Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613) allows the spouse and minor children of a 9(g) pre-arranged employee to be admitted as dependents. Dependent status, however, carries no work authorisation. A dependent spouse who wants to work must have an employer apply for an Alien Employment Permit (AEP) in their own name under DOLE Department Order No. 248, series of 2025, including the labour-market test. Only narrow categories are exempt under DO 248's exemption rules: the dependent spouse of a member of the diplomatic corps, staff of international organisations, resident-visa holders (such as spouses of Filipino citizens holding 13(a) visas), and recognised refugees and stateless persons. Since 2025 even these exempt persons must obtain a Certificate of Exemption from DOLE. The spouse of an ordinary principal migrant worker therefore has no automatic right to work.

### q101 · Right to maintain immigration status after loss of employment.
- Law: Loss of employment automatically implies the loss of immigration status
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. Under the Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613, as amended by Republic Act No. 503), Sec. 9(g), the work visa is granted only to "an alien coming to prearranged employment, for whom the issuance of a visa has been authorized in accordance with section twenty". Under Sec. 20(a), as amended, that authorisation follows a petition by "the prospective employer", which must show that no Filipino is available for the specific job. The status therefore exists only for one job with one employer. The Labor Code (Presidential Decree No. 442), Art. 40, requires an employment permit for the alien and employer, and Art. 41 provides that "the alien shall not transfer to another job or change his employer without prior approval of the Secretary of Labor". Sec. 37(a)(7) of CA 613 makes deportable "an alien who remains in the Philippines in violation of any limitation or condition under which he was admitted as a nonimmigrant". No statute or regulation we read gives a grace period in which a dismissed or resigning 9(g) worker keeps work status while looking for a new job. In Bureau of Immigration (BI) practice, the 9(g) visa is cancelled or "downgraded" to a 9(a) temporary-visitor status that does not allow work, and the person is then expected to leave. A new job requires a fresh employer petition, a new Alien Employment Permit (AEP) and a new 9(g) visa. Holders of permanent (Sec. 13) visas, such as spouses of Filipinos or quota immigrants, do not depend on employment and are unaffected.

### q103 · Right to equal employment conditions and protection.
- Law: Yes, migrants have a right to the same employment conditions and protections
- Implementation: Sometimes; Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Labor Code of the Philippines (Presidential Decree No. 442, as amended), Arts. 3 and 6, and in the 1987 Constitution (Saligang Batas ng Republika ng Pilipinas), Art. XIII §3. Art. 6 provides that "all rights and benefits granted to workers under this Code shall, except as may otherwise be provided herein, apply alike to all workers." The Code's labour standards contain no nationality condition. These include hours of work and overtime, weekly rest days, holiday and service-incentive leave, night-shift differential, occupational safety and health, maternity protection, and security of tenure with just-cause dismissal procedures. Foreign employees with a valid Alien Employment Permit (AEP) are therefore entitled to the same conditions as Filipino employees. Art. XIII §3 of the Constitution guarantees "full protection to labor" and entitles all workers to "security of tenure, humane conditions of work, and a living wage." In International School Alliance of Educators v. Quisumbing (G.R. No. 128845, 2000), the Supreme Court held that humane conditions "include as well the manner by which employers treat their employees" and condemned "unequal and discriminatory terms and conditions of employment." The main formal differences fall outside individual working conditions. Under Art. 269 (renumbered 284), aliens with valid DOLE permits may join unions only if their country grants reciprocal rights to Filipinos. In addition, the duration of employment is tied to the validity of the AEP.

### q109 · Right to equal access to social security protections.
- Law: Access to some, but not all types of social security protections (e.g., no equal access under this program; or access depending on visa type)
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Social Security Act of 2018 (Republic Act No. 11199), Sections 8(c)-(d), 9(a) and 10, and the Universal Health Care Act (Republic Act No. 11223), Section 5. The SSS Act defines an employee as "any person who performs services for an employer", and an employer as any person, "domestic or foreign", carrying on business in the Philippines. Section 9(a) makes SSS coverage "compulsory upon all employees". Section 10 says employee coverage takes effect "on the day of his employment". There is no nationality condition, so a foreign worker with an Alien Employment Permit is covered from the first day. That coverage brings sickness, maternity, disability, retirement, death and unemployment benefits on the same contribution terms as citizens. Employees of foreign governments and international organisations are excluded (Sec. 8(j)(3)). Equality is not complete, for two reasons. First, Section 15 bars benefits for a "beneficiary who is a national of a foreign country which does not extend benefits to a Filipino beneficiary residing in the Philippines", unless the Social Security Commission directs payment "without regard to nationality". Payment therefore depends on reciprocity. Second, the Universal Health Care Act automatically enrols only "every Filipino citizen" in the National Health Insurance Program (Sec. 5). Foreign nationals reach PhilHealth only through employer-shared premiums under a formal contract, or as self-paying Informal Economy members holding an ACR I-Card (PhilHealth Circular No. 2017-0003). Tax-financed and citizenship-based programmes do not cover foreign workers.

### q111 · Right of equal access to unemployment benefits.
- Law: Access to some, but not all types of unemployment benefits; OR not to the same extent as citizens; OR it depends on their visa type (e.g., temporary vs. permanent, high-skill vs. low skill)
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Social Security Act of 2018 (Republic Act No. 11199), Section 14-B, read with Sections 8(d), 9(a) and 15. The only statutory unemployment benefit is the SSS "Unemployment Insurance or Involuntary Separation Benefit". Section 14-B grants it to "a member who is not over sixty (60) years of age who has paid at least thirty-six (36) months contributions twelve (12) months of which should be in the eighteen-month period immediately preceding the involuntary unemployment or separation". It is paid monthly at "fifty percent (50%) of the average monthly salary credit for a maximum of two (2) months", and can be claimed once every three years. SSS membership is compulsory for "all employees" whatever their nationality (Secs. 8(d), 9(a)). A foreign worker holding an Alien Employment Permit therefore becomes a member on the first day of work and can in principle qualify on the same contribution conditions as a Filipino. Access is still not fully equal. Section 15 denies "any benefit" to a beneficiary who is a national of a country that does not grant benefits to Filipinos, unless the Social Security Commission directs payment "without regard to nationality". Entitlement can therefore depend on the worker's nationality and on reciprocity. In practice, the 36-month contribution requirement and the tie between the AEP and the 9(g) visa and a single employer also make the benefit hard for temporary foreign workers to reach. When employment ends, the permit lapses and the worker must regularise or leave.

### q113 · Right of temporary migrants to become lawfully settled.
- Law: Yes, there is an opportunity to switch to permanent status after certain conditions are
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613), Sec. 13, as amended by Republic Act No. 503. The section allows "quota immigrants" up to "fifty (50) of any one nationality or without nationality for any one calendar year". It expressly adds that "Qualified and desirable aliens who are in the Philippines under temporary stay may be admitted within the quota". Bureau of Immigration (BI) Memorandum Order No. RADJR-11-004 (2011) sets out how a person already in the country applies for "change of admission status" to quota immigrant. Applicants need a valid passport and visa, must not be excludable or deportable, and must come from a country with a reciprocity arrangement (waived for stateless persons). Preference goes to people with "qualifications, skills, or scientific, educational or technical knowledge" beneficial to the national interest, or with "sufficient capital for a viable and sustainable investment". Temporary workers on 9(g) visas can also become permanent residents on non-quota grounds, notably by marrying a Filipino citizen (Sec. 13(a)). Naturalization under Commonwealth Act No. 473 after ten years' residence is a further, longer route. There is no route based simply on years of temporary work. The quota route is capped and discretionary. The last paragraph of Sec. 9, as amended, still says a nonimmigrant "cannot remain in the Philippines permanently" without departing and getting an immigrant visa abroad, although BI processes quota conversions inside the country.

### q115 · Right of temporary migrants to settle regardless of initial status.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613), Sec. 13, as amended by Republic Act No. 503. The quota route is open to "qualified and desirable aliens who are in the Philippines under temporary stay". It is not limited to any one nonimmigrant category under Sec. 9, so 9(g) workers, 47(a)(2) workers, treaty traders and others can all apply. Bureau of Immigration (BI) Memorandum Order No. RADJR-11-004 (2011) requires only that the applicant hold "a valid passport (or equivalent document) and visa" when applying. The other conditions concern the person, not the visa type: not excludable or deportable, reciprocity with the home country, and preference for skills or investment. The non-quota family routes in Sec. 13(a), such as marriage to a Filipino citizen, are likewise open whatever temporary visa the person holds. Pathways are therefore not tied to visa type. They depend on personal qualifications, family ties and a nationality quota of 50 a year. That is a real constraint, but it is not a visa-type restriction.

### q117 · Right to redress following employer breach of employment contract.
- Law: Yes, but limited
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in the 1987 Constitution, Art. XIII Sec. 3, which affords "full protection to labor, local and overseas". It is also protected by the Labor Code (Presidential Decree No. 442), Art. 217 (renumbered Art. 224), which gives Labor Arbiters jurisdiction over termination disputes, claims on "wages, rates of pay, hours of work and other terms and conditions of employment", and damages "involving all workers". In Rouche v. French Chamber of Commerce in the Philippines (G.R. No. 238581, 7 December 2022), the Supreme Court held that this protection "extends to all workers, even to aliens engaged for local employment", that "they, too, have the right to security of tenure", and that "alien employment is regulated by the State does not remove them from constitutional and statutory protections". It awarded full backwages to a French employee. Foreign workers holding an Alien Employment Permit (AEP) and a 9(g) visa can therefore bring contract and illegal-dismissal claims before the National Labor Relations Commission (NLRC) on the same terms as Filipinos. The right is limited for workers without valid permits. Under Labor Code Art. 41, an alien may not change job or employer without approval of the Secretary of Labor. In WPP Marketing Communications v. Galera (G.R. No. 169207, 2010), the Court held that a foreign worker employed without a permit "cannot come to this Court with unclean hands" and could not claim full labour-law benefits. Rouche only carves out cases where the missing permit is not the worker's fault. Irregular or unpermitted migrant workers can therefore lose redress against an employer who breached the contract.

### q135 · Right to be free from discrimination on the basis of race or ethnicity.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Philippines protects this right in the 1987 Constitution (Saligang Batas ng Republika ng Pilipinas), Art. III §1, the Labor Code of the Philippines (Presidential Decree No. 442, as amended), Art. 3, and International School Alliance of Educators v. Quisumbing, G.R. No. 128845 (2000). Art. III §1 provides that no "person" shall "be denied the equal protection of the laws," and the Supreme Court has long extended the Bill of Rights to aliens. Labor Code Art. 3 obliges the State to "ensure equal work opportunities regardless of sex, race or creed." In ISAE v. Quisumbing, the Court considered a claim that a pay scale based on point of hire amounted to racial discrimination. It held that "public policy abhors inequality and discrimination" and relied on the International Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination (ICERD), ILO Convention No. 111 and the ICESCR. The Court said the Philippines "through its Constitution, has incorporated this principle as part of its national laws" and struck down the discriminatory terms of employment. The protection covers everyone in the territory, including migrants. However, the Philippines has no comprehensive anti-discrimination statute defining and penalising racial or ethnic discrimination in employment, housing or services. Bills covering race and ethnicity remain pending in Congress. The prohibition therefore rests on the constitutional equality clause, a Labor Code policy declaration and case law, with no dedicated enforcement mechanism or sanctions.


## Legal personhood

### q27 · Right not to have identity documents confiscated.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act of 2003 (Republic Act No. 9208), Section 5(f), and Section 5(i), which the Expanded Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10364, Sec. 8) inserted and the 2022 amendment (Republic Act No. 11862) left unchanged. Section 5 lists "acts which promote or facilitate trafficking in persons" and makes them unlawful. Section 5(f) covers acts done "to confiscate, conceal, or destroy the passport, travel documents, or personal documents or belongings of trafficked persons in furtherance of trafficking or to prevent them from leaving the country or seeking redress". Section 5(i) makes it unlawful "to destroy, conceal, remove, confiscate or possess ... any actual or purported passport or other travel, immigration or working permit or document, or any other actual or purported government identification, of any person in order to prevent or restrict, without lawful authority, the person's liberty to move or travel in order to maintain the labor or services of that person." The prohibition binds anyone (employers, recruiters, private individuals) and protects "any person", so it covers foreign workers whatever their status. The words "without lawful authority" keep the exception for authorised officials. Under the Alien Registration Act of 1950 (Republic Act No. 562), Secs. 7-8, only immigration and peace officers may demand to see an alien's registration certificate, and it is surrendered to the registering official on departure. The protection has a limit: the statute ties confiscation to a purpose of restricting movement to keep a person's labour, or to trafficking. No general labour-code rule bars employers from simply holding workers' passports.

### q29 · Right to birth registration.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Civil Registry Law (Act No. 3753, An Act to Establish a Civil Register, 1930), Sections 1, 4 and 5. Section 1 sets up a civil register in which "births" shall be entered. Under Section 5, the declaration of the attending physician or midwife, or failing that of either parent, "shall be sent to the local civil registrar not later than thirty days after the birth". The declaration must state the "sex and nationality of infant" and the "names, citizenship, and religion of parents". The duty attaches to every birth in the territory. The law makes no exception for children of foreign nationals or for the immigration status of the parents, and it expects the infant's and parents' nationality to be recorded. So children of regular and irregular migrants, refugees and stateless persons all fall under it. The Foundling Recognition and Protection Act (Republic Act No. 11767, 2022) adds registration duties for foundlings and treats them as natural-born citizens, which helps prevent statelessness. Executive Order No. 163 (2022) directs agencies to improve refugees', stateless persons' and asylum seekers' access to "documentation". Neither Act 3753 nor other statutes create a separate foreigners' birth registry. Children of foreign parents are registered in the ordinary local civil registry, and late registration is available.

### q31 · Right to government document recognizing residency, work status, and identity.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Alien Registration Act of 1950 (Republic Act No. 562, as amended by Republic Act No. 7919), Sections 1-4, and in the Labor Code of the Philippines (Presidential Decree No. 442), Article 40. Section 1 of RA 562 requires aliens residing in the Philippines to apply for registration with the Bureau of Immigration. Section 2 lets the Commissioner extend registration to other classes, including "aliens of any other class not lawfully admitted into the Philippines for permanent residence". Section 4 provides that "as evidence of such registration, a certificate shall be issued which shall contain the name, age, date and place of birth, civil status, local residence, occupation and photograph of the alien". In practice this is the Alien Certificate of Registration (ACR I-Card), which records identity and residence status. Work status is documented separately. Under Labor Code Art. 40, any alien seeking employment and any employer wishing to engage one "shall obtain an employment permit from the Department of Labor", the Alien Employment Permit (AEP), now regulated by DOLE Department Order 248 (2025). Coverage centres on lawfully resident and admitted foreigners. Irregular migrants get no document recognising their status. Recognised refugees are documented through the DOJ Refugees and Stateless Persons Protection Unit (RSPPU) and the Bureau of Immigration.

### q33 · Right against destruction of document recognizing residency, work status, and identity.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act of 2003 (Republic Act No. 9208), Section 5(f), and Section 5(i), inserted by Republic Act No. 10364 (2012) and kept by Republic Act No. 11862 (2022). It is also protected in the Revised Penal Code (Act No. 3815), Article 226. Section 5(f) makes it unlawful "to confiscate, conceal, or destroy the passport, travel documents, or personal documents" of trafficked persons. Section 5(i) makes it unlawful "to destroy, conceal, remove, confiscate or possess ... any actual or purported passport or other travel, immigration or working permit or document, or any other actual or purported government identification, of any person" in order to restrict, without lawful authority, that person's freedom of movement so as to keep their labour or services. These provisions apply to anyone and cover the documents that record a migrant's residence (immigration documents, the ACR), work status (working permit or AEP) and identity (passport, government ID). For officials, Revised Penal Code Art. 226 punishes "any public officer who shall remove, destroy or conceal documents or papers officially entrusted to him". The private-actor prohibition is limited to the purposes the anti-trafficking law names. Outside that context only general property offences apply, and they are not specific to migrants' documents.


## Liberty

### q15 · Right to communicate with consular officials.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Bureau of Immigration Omnibus Rules of Procedure of 2015 (Immigration Memorandum Circular No. SBM-2015-010), Rule 5, Section 4, and in the Bureau of Corrections Operating Manual 2024 (registered with the Office of the National Administrative Register on 10 October 2024), Chapter 14, Section 7. These sit alongside the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations, which the Philippines ratified in 1965 with Senate concurrence and which therefore has the rank of domestic law. The Omnibus Rules govern arrests in deportation proceedings. On the day of arrest, "the Chief of the Intelligence Division, or his duly authorized representative, shall inform the foreigner's embassy or consular office in the Philippines, if there is any, of the arrest". Rule 5, Section 6 adds that the foreigner's embassy shall receive copies of the Charge Sheet or Order of Release, and Rule 9 requires that deportation orders be furnished to the respondent's embassy or consular office. For persons deprived of liberty in national prisons, the BuCor Manual provides that "a PDL of foreign nationality shall be allowed to communicate with the diplomatic and consular representative of the State of which he/she is a national". Section 8 extends this to stateless persons and refugees, who may communicate with the state or organisation protecting their interests. The protection covers all foreign nationals arrested by the Bureau of Immigration (BI) or imprisoned by BuCor, whatever their immigration status. The BI rule is framed as automatic notice to the embassy, not as a choice offered to the detainee. Refugees and asylum seekers are not expressly exempted from it, a gap given the risk that notice may reach their country of origin. No single statute codifies Article 36 VCCR for all arresting agencies. Police and lower-court practice rests on internal Philippine National Police (PNP) procedures and court circulars.

### q17 · Right of child migrants to be free from detention on the basis of migrant status.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. The Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613, as amended) allows immigration officers to detain arriving aliens (Secs. 25-26). Sec. 37(a) provides that aliens falling within the deportation grounds "shall be arrested upon the warrant of the Commissioner of Immigration" and deported. None of these provisions exempts children. The Bureau of Immigration Omnibus Rules of Procedure of 2015 (Immigration Memorandum Circular No. SBM-2015-010) govern turnover to the BI Warden Facility and discretionary bail (Rule 7, "may be released under cash bond... and under such other conditions that the Commissioner may impose"). They contain no age-based bar on detention and no rule on minors. Executive Order No. 163 (2022) commits the State to protect the "liberty and security, and freedom of movement" of refugees, stateless persons and asylum seekers. It is limited to those groups, operates "subject to applicable laws", and does not prohibit detaining children. The Juvenile Justice and Welfare Act (Republic Act No. 9344, as amended) and the Special Protection of Children Act (RA 7610) protect children in conflict with the law and child victims. They do not prohibit administrative immigration detention of children because of their migration status. The Anti-Trafficking Act (RA 9208, as amended) protects trafficked persons from punishment, but only that group. The Convention on the Rights of the Child (Arts. 3 and 37) binds the Philippines and has statute rank. No statute, regulation or court decision translates it into a prohibition on detaining migrant children. In practice, minors found in raids are usually referred to social welfare services, but that is a matter of discretion, not a legal bar.

### q19 · Right of migrants to be free from detention on the basis of migrant status.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. The Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613, as amended) authorises detention based on migration status. Under Sec. 25, examining officers "may order such aliens detained" to determine whether they are excludable. Sec. 26 provides that an alien not clearly entitled to land "shall be detained for examination". Sec. 37(a) provides that aliens who enter without inspection, were inadmissible at entry, or otherwise fall within the deportation grounds "shall be arrested upon the warrant of the Commissioner of Immigration" and deported. The Bureau of Immigration Omnibus Rules of Procedure of 2015 (Immigration Memorandum Circular No. SBM-2015-010) provide for turnover of arrested foreigners to the BI Warden Facility (Rule 5, Sec. 7) and for summary deportation of overstaying and undocumented foreigners. Release on bail is at the Commissioner's discretion (Rule 7, Sec. 1). There is no statutory maximum period, no requirement that detention be a last resort, and no general presumption of liberty. The main limit is judicial. In Mejoff v. Director of Prisons (G.R. No. L-4254, 1951), the Supreme Court held that aliens may not be kept in detention indefinitely when deportation cannot be carried out. Habeas corpus is available. Executive Order No. 163 (2022) promises protection of the "liberty and security, and freedom of movement" of refugees, stateless persons and asylum seekers "subject to applicable laws". It is a policy commitment limited to those groups and does not prohibit their detention. Migrants in the Philippines can therefore lawfully be detained solely on the basis of their immigration status.

### q21 · Right to appeal legality, conditions, and length of detention
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Philippines protects this right in the 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines (Saligang Batas ng Republika ng Pilipinas), Art. III §§1, 15 and 19(2), in Commonwealth Act No. 613 (Philippine Immigration Act of 1940), Sec. 37(e), and in binding Supreme Court decisions. - **Who is covered:** Art. III §1 protects "any person" against deprivation of liberty without due process. Mejoff v. Director of Prisons (G.R. No. L-4254, 1951) applies this to foreigners, "regardless of nationality." - **Legality:** Art. III §15 preserves the writ of habeas corpus. Under Board of Commissioners v. Yuan Wenle (G.R. No. 242957, 28 Feb 2023, en banc), a detainee must first use the Bureau of Immigration's own remedies once deportation proceedings are pending. Court review follows afterwards. Sec. 37(e) lets the Commissioner release any alien arrested in deportation proceedings "under bond or under such other conditions." - **Length:** Mejoff holds that foreign nationals "may not indefinitely be kept in detention" and that "too long a detention may justify the issuance of a writ of habeas corpus." - **Conditions:** Alejano v. Cabuay (G.R. No. 160792, 2005) holds that detention conditions that violate constitutional rights "will be reviewed by the courts on a case-by-case basis." Courts "could afford injunctive relief or damages" for "arbitrary and inhumane conditions," although habeas corpus itself is limited to "the fact or duration of confinement." Art. III §19(2) adds that degrading punishment of detainees and "substandard or inadequate penal facilities under subhuman conditions shall be dealt with by law." All three aspects can therefore be challenged, by every migrant in detention, through different procedures.

### q23 · Right to an individualized detention decision.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Philippines protects this right in Commonwealth Act No. 613 (Philippine Immigration Act of 1940), Sec. 37(a), (c) and (e), as interpreted in Board of Commissioners of the Bureau of Immigration v. Yuan Wenle (G.R. No. 242957, 28 Feb 2023, en banc). - **Individual determination:** Under Sec. 37(a), aliens are arrested and deported on a Commissioner's warrant only "after a determination by the Board of Commissioners of the existence of the grounds for deportation as charged against the alien." - **Notice and hearing:** Sec. 37(c) bars deportation "without being informed of the specific grounds for deportation nor without being given a hearing." - **Detention not automatic:** Sec. 37(e) lets each arrested alien be "released under bond or under such other conditions as may be imposed by the Commissioner," which means a case-by-case decision on custody. - **Test for warrants:** In Yuan Wenle the Supreme Court held that administrative authorities, before issuing any warrant, "must first determine from the applicant" two things. First, whether there is a pressing need to enforce the law swiftly. Second, whether "the public harm is greater than the damage to be suffered by the person subject of the warrant." Any deprivation must stay "temporary or provisional" and subject to procedural due process. These protections apply to all foreigners in deportation proceedings, including irregular migrants. One limit: summary deportation orders against undocumented foreigners and fugitives were upheld, so for those groups the individual assessment is brief.

### q25 · Right to be free from torture and inhumane treatment.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes; Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in the 1987 Constitution, Art. III §§12(2) and 19, and in Republic Act No. 9745 (Anti-Torture Act of 2009), Secs. 2, 3, 5, 6 and 17. - **Constitution:** Art. III §12(2) provides that "No torture, force, violence, threat, intimidation, or any other means which vitiate the free will shall be used against" a person under investigation. It also bans secret or incommunicado detention. Art. III §19(1) prohibits "cruel, degrading or inhuman punishment." Art. III §19(2) requires the law to deal with degrading punishment of "any prisoner or detainee" and with "substandard or inadequate penal facilities under subhuman conditions." - **RA 9745, scope:** Sec. 2(b) aims to ensure that "the human rights of all persons, including suspects, detainees and prisoners are respected at all times." The Act applies regardless of nationality. - **RA 9745, definitions:** Sec. 3(a) defines torture. Secs. 3(b) and 5 separately define "other cruel, inhuman and degrading treatment or punishment" inflicted on a person in custody. - **RA 9745, absolute bar and non-return:** Sec. 6 declares freedom from both "an absolute right" that cannot be suspended in war or emergency. Sec. 17 bars expelling or returning any person to a State where there are substantial grounds to believe they would face torture. Migrants are therefore protected against both torture and cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment, in police custody, in immigration detention and against removal to torture.


## Life

### q9 · Right to be free from use of force to prevent entry to territory.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. No statute, regulation or binding decision prohibits officers or agents from using force to prevent migrants from entering the national territory. The Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613, as amended) deals with entry in terms of inspection, exclusion and removal. Section 37(a) provides for arrest and deportation of 'any alien who enters the Philippines ... without inspection and admission by the immigration authorities at a designated port of entry'. Section 45(4) makes it an offence for an alien to enter 'at any time or place other than as designated by the immigration officials'. The Act says nothing about how much force border officials may or may not use. The Philippine Coast Guard Law (Republic Act No. 9993), Section 3(l), empowers the Coast Guard, an 'armed and uniformed service' (Sec. 2), 'to assist in the enforcement of laws on ... immigration ... human trafficking ... within the maritime jurisdiction'. Section 3(m) lets it board and inspect vessels, and Section 3(c) lets it detain, stop or prevent ships from sailing. None of these provisions limits or prohibits force against people trying to enter. The closest protections are general ones: the 1987 Constitution, Art. III §1 (no person deprived of life or liberty without due process), the Anti-Torture Act (RA 9745), and the PNP's internal Police Operational Procedures on necessity and proportionality, which are an administrative manual, not law specific to border entry. Under MRD rules these general principles do not amount to a specific prohibition on using force to prevent entry.

### q11 · Right to be free from use of lethal force to prevent entry to territory.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. No national law specifically prohibits officers or agents from using lethal force to stop migrants entering the territory. The Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613, as amended) treats unauthorised entry as grounds for arrest and deportation (Sec. 37(a)(1)) and as a minor offence punishable by a fine of up to one thousand pesos or up to two years' imprisonment (Sec. 45). It does not regulate the use of force or firearms by immigration officers. The Philippine Coast Guard Law (Republic Act No. 9993) sets up the Coast Guard as 'an armed and uniformed service' (Sec. 2) and tasks it with assisting in enforcing immigration and anti-trafficking laws at sea (Sec. 3(l)). It contains no rule limiting or banning lethal force against migrants or the vessels carrying them. The 1987 Constitution (Art. III §1) protects the right to life of 'any person', and the Revised Penal Code allows fulfilment of duty as a justification only where force is necessary. These are general guarantees, not a specific bar on lethal force to prevent entry. The PNP's internal operational procedures reportedly allow firearms only against imminent threats, but they are an administrative manual, not statute, and say nothing about border control. Under the MRD's coding rules these general principles do not produce a 'Yes'.

### q13 · Right to be protected from trafficking or smuggling.
- Law: No, government may punish trafficked or smuggled migrants
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines for both trafficked and smuggled migrants. Trafficked persons are protected: the Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act of 2003 (Republic Act No. 9208, as amended by RA 10364 and RA 11862), Section 17, provides that 'Trafficked persons shall be recognized as victims of the act or acts of trafficking and as such shall not be penalized for crimes directly related to the acts of trafficking enumerated in this Act or in obedience to the order made by the trafficker in relation thereto', and that their consent to the exploitation 'shall be irrelevant'. Section 19, as amended by RA 11862, extends protection, assistance, interpreters and 'continued presence in the Philippines' for the time needed to prosecute offenders to 'trafficked persons in the Philippines who are nationals of a foreign country'. Smuggled migrants have no equivalent protection. The Philippines has no migrant-smuggling statute. The Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613), Section 45(4), punishes any alien who 'enters the Philippines at any time or place other than as designated by the immigration officials, or obtains entry ... by willful, false, or misleading representation' with a fine or up to two years' imprisonment. Section 37(a) makes such entry grounds for arrest and deportation. Section 46 punishes those who bring in or harbour such aliens, but it does not exempt the smuggled person. The Smuggling of Migrants Protocol (ratified 2002), Art. 5, has not been carried into a domestic statute. As with Argentina, where trafficking victims are exempt but smuggled migrants are not, the MRD's combined indicator is coded at its lowest option.


## Nationality

### q63 · Right to maintain citizenship status after convictions.
- Law: Convictions do not affect citizenship status
- Implementation: Effectively Always

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Revised Naturalization Law (Commonwealth Act No. 473), Section 18, the Administrative Naturalization Law of 2000 (Republic Act No. 9139), Section 13, and Commonwealth Act No. 63, Section 1. None of these makes a criminal or administrative conviction a ground for losing citizenship once it has been acquired. Under CA 473 Sec. 18 a court may cancel a naturalization certificate only if it "was obtained fraudulently or illegally". The other grounds are: - the naturalized person sets up permanent residence abroad within five years; - the petition rested on an invalid declaration of intention; - the person's minor children, through the parents' fault, fail to finish a recognised high school; - the person "has allowed himself to be used as a dummy" in violation of nationality-restricted rights. RA 9139 Sec. 13 adds false statements in the petition, residence abroad within five years, dummy arrangements, and "any act inimical to national security". None of these requires a conviction. The only grounds tied to a conviction concern fraud in the naturalization itself: CA 473 Sec. 19 and RA 9139 Sec. 14, covering forged certificates and aiding unlawful naturalization. Those fall outside this indicator. CA 63 Sec. 1(5) lists cancellation of the certificate as a way of losing citizenship. Sec. 1(6) also removes citizenship from any citizen declared "a deserter of the Philippine army, navy or air corps in time of war". That ground applies equally to natural-born citizens and only in wartime. Before citizenship vests, RA 530 Sec. 1 bars a grant from becoming executory if the applicant, during a two-year probation, "has been convicted of any offense or violation of Government promulgated rules". This affects acquisition, not citizenship already held.

### q65 · Right of economic migrants to citizenship.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Revised Naturalization Law (Commonwealth Act No. 473), Sections 2–4, as supplemented by Republic Act No. 530, Section 1. Any foreigner who meets the statutory qualifications may petition a court for naturalization, whatever the basis of their stay, including work or business. Section 2 requires: - age 21; - "continuous period of not less than ten years" of residence; - good moral character; - real estate worth at least five thousand pesos, or "some known lucrative trade, profession, or lawful occupation"; - ability to speak and write English or Spanish and a principal Philippine language; - enrolment of minor children in recognised schools. Under Section 3 the ten-year residence is "reduced to five years" for, among others, a petitioner who has "established a new industry or introduced a useful invention in the Philippines", or who has taught for two years in a recognised school. Section 4 disqualifies, among others, persons "convicted of crimes involving moral turpitude" and nationals of countries "whose laws do not grant Filipinos the right to become naturalized" (reciprocity). The procedure also includes: - a declaration of intention filed one year before the petition (Sec. 5); - publication and hearing; - under RA 530 Sec. 1, a two-year period after the grant before it becomes executory. During that period the applicant must stay in the country, keep a lawful calling and not be convicted of any offence. A separate administrative route under RA 9139 is limited to aliens "born in the Philippines and residing therein since birth" (Sec. 3(a)), so it does not help first-generation economic migrants.

### q67 · Right of humanitarian migrants to citizenship.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Rarely

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Revised Naturalization Law (Commonwealth Act No. 473), Sections 2–4, together with the Supreme Court's Rule on Facilitated Naturalization of Refugees and Stateless Persons (A.M. No. 21-07-22-SC, approved 15 February 2022). Recognised refugees and stateless persons may petition for judicial naturalization under CA 473 on the same terms as other foreigners: ten years' continuous residence (five in the cases listed in Sec. 3), good moral character, a lawful occupation, language ability, and none of the Section 4 disqualifications. The Supreme Court adopted the Rule under its constitutional rule-making power. According to the Court, it "governs the procedure for the filing of petitions for naturalization by refugees and stateless persons recognized by the Philippine Government". Its stated aim is "to simplify and reduce legal and procedural hurdles in obtaining Philippine citizenship". It allows: - publication on the Supreme Court website instead of costly newspaper notices (Sec. 12); - petitions on behalf of unaccompanied children, filed by the Department of Social Welfare and Development (DSWD) or local social welfare offices. Executive Order No. 163 (2022) commits agencies to ensure refugees' and stateless persons' "participation in judicial and administrative citizenship proceedings" and encourages local integration (Sec. 6). The pathway covers only persons whose refugee or stateless status the DOJ Refugees and Stateless Persons Protection Unit has recognised. The substantive CA 473 requirements, including the ten-year residence and income tests, apply except where the Rule eases procedure. A facilitated administrative naturalization bill is still pending in Congress.

### q69 · Right of family migrants to citizenship.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in the Revised Naturalization Law (Commonwealth Act No. 473), Sections 3(3) and 15, and the Administrative Naturalization Law of 2000 (Republic Act No. 9139), Section 11. Section 15 provides that "any woman who is now or may hereafter be married to a citizen of the Philippines, and who might herself be lawfully naturalized shall be deemed a citizen of the Philippines". In Moy Ya Lim Yao v. Commissioner of Immigration (G.R. No. L-21289, 1971) the Supreme Court read this as conferring citizenship on a foreign wife who is not disqualified under Section 4. Her status is then recognised administratively, with cancellation of her alien registration, without full judicial naturalization. Section 15 also extends citizenship to minor children of persons naturalized under the law: those born in the Philippines, foreign-born minors living in the Philippines at the time of the parent's naturalization, and others on conditions. A foreign husband of a Filipina gets no automatic citizenship. Under Section 3(3) the ten-year residence for judicial naturalization is "reduced to five years" for a petitioner "married to a Filipino woman"; the other Section 2 requirements still apply. Under RA 9139 Sec. 11, once a petition is approved, the applicant's "alien lawful wife and minor children may file a petition for cancellation of their alien certificates of registration". Under Sec. 12 a woman's administrative naturalization "will not benefit her alien husband" but does extend to her minor children. Family-based pathways therefore exist, but they are sex-asymmetric, favouring foreign wives over foreign husbands.

### q71 · Right to dual nationality.
- Law: No, no migrants may obtain dual nationality by retaining their nationality of origin
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in the Philippines. Both routes to naturalization require the applicant to give up their nationality of origin. - **Judicial naturalization.** Under the Revised Naturalization Law (Commonwealth Act No. 473), Section 12, the petitioner must take an oath in open court before the certificate of naturalization is issued. In it the petitioner swears to "renounce absolutely and forever all allegiance and fidelity to any foreign prince, potentate, state or sovereignty, and particularly to the [state] of which at this time I am a subject or citizen." - **Administrative naturalization.** This route is open only to aliens born and resident in the Philippines since birth. Under the Administrative Naturalization Law of 2000 (Republic Act No. 9139), Section 5, the petition must state the "true and honest intention to acquire Philippine citizenship and to renounce absolutely and forever any prince, potentate, State or sovereign, and particularly the country of which the applicant is a citizen or subject." After the oath of allegiance, the Bureau of Immigration cancels the applicant's alien certificate of registration (Sec. 10). - **Constitutional basis.** The 1987 Constitution, Art. IV, Sec. 5, states that "dual allegiance of citizens is inimical to the national interest and shall be dealt with by law." The only statute that tolerates dual nationality is the Citizenship Retention and Re-acquisition Act of 2003 (Republic Act No. 9225). It benefits only natural-born Filipinos who became citizens of another country, not foreign migrants who naturalize. Private acts of Congress that grant citizenship to named individuals (e.g., RA 12319, 2026) also require an oath of allegiance before the Bureau of Immigration. The renunciation rule applies to all migrants, whatever their country of origin. Whether the person actually loses the original nationality depends on that country's law, but Philippine law gives no right to keep it.

### q133 · Right of irregular migrants to regularize their status.
- Law: No, there is no opportunity for irregular migrant workers to regularize
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in the Philippines. The Philippine Immigration Act of 1940 (Commonwealth Act No. 613, as amended) has no standing procedure for irregular migrants or irregular migrant workers to regularize their status. Aliens who overstay or work without a proper visa are liable to deportation under Sec. 37. The only legalization law is the Alien Social Integration Act of 1995 (Republic Act No. 7919), and it was a one-off programme: - **Who it covered.** Sec. 3 granted legal residence status to "all aliens whose stay in the Philippines is otherwise illegal under existing laws, and who have entered the country prior to June 30, 1992." Refugees were expressly excluded. - **Conditions.** Registration with five agencies, an integration fee of PHP 200,000 and a medical certificate (Sec. 4). - **Deadline.** Sec. 8 limited the benefits to applications made "from June 1, 1995 to December 31, 1996." The window has closed, so the Act gives no route to migrants now in an irregular situation. A new programme has been proposed. House Bill No. 5837 (20th Congress, filed 11 November 2025), the proposed "Special Regularization and Administrative Naturalization Act of 2025," would create a pathway for long-resident undocumented foreign nationals. It is pending and not law. Administrative routes do not fill the gap: - Visitors who overstay may sometimes pay fines and extend their visas before deportation proceedings start, but this is discretionary and not regularization. - Refugees and stateless persons recognized under DOJ Department Circular No. 024 (2022) and EO 163 get protection status, not a labour-migration regularization.


## Opinion and expression

### q83 · Right to freedom to hold opinion.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in Article III, Section 4 of the 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines (Saligang Batas ng Republika ng Pilipinas): "No law shall be passed abridging the freedom of speech, of expression, or of the press...". The clause is framed as a limit on state power, not as a citizens' right. It applies to everyone within Philippine jurisdiction, as do the Bill of Rights' due process and equal protection guarantees, which protect "any person" (Art. III, Sec. 1). Freedom to hold opinions is the core of this guarantee. The International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (Art. 19(1)), ratified in 1986 and given statute rank after Senate concurrence, adds an unqualified right to hold opinions without interference. There is one exception. Section 37(a)(8) of Commonwealth Act No. 613 (Philippine Immigration Act of 1940) makes deportable "[a]ny alien who believes in, advises, advocates or teaches the overthrow by force and violence of the Government ... or who disbelieves in or is opposed to organized government". That is a belief-based immigration sanction with no counterpart for citizens. It has not been reported in use in recent practice, and it remains subject to the constitutional free-expression guarantee. Apart from this clause, migrants of every status hold the same freedom of opinion as citizens.

### q85 · Right to freedom of expression.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines on terms commensurate with citizens. Article III, Section 4 of the 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines (Saligang Batas ng Republika ng Pilipinas) provides that "No law shall be passed abridging the freedom of speech, of expression, or of the press," with no nationality limitation, so foreigners hold a general constitutional right to free expression. Political expression by foreigners, however, is restricted by immigration rules that apply only to them. Bureau of Immigration Operations Order No. SBM-2015-025 (2015), as reported, prohibits foreigners from engaging in political activities, including "joining, supporting, contributing, or involving themselves in any rally, assembly, or gathering" for or against the government. Violations are treated as breaches of the conditions of admission, a deportation ground under Section 37(a)(7) of Commonwealth Act No. 613 (Philippine Immigration Act of 1940), and lead to deportation and blacklisting. Section 37(a)(8) of the same Act also makes deportable an alien who "advises, advocates or teaches" the forcible overthrow of government or opposition to organized government. Foreigners' participation in elections is separately barred under the Omnibus Election Code. As a result, nonimmigrant migrants (tourists, workers, students, missionaries) can be removed for political speech that citizens may lawfully engage in. The ICCPR (Art. 19(2)) and the Migrant Workers Convention (Art. 13) have statute rank but have not been applied to override the bureau's rule.


## Peaceful assembly and association

### q87 · Right to freedom of association.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines on terms commensurate with citizens. Article III, Section 8 of the 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines (Saligang Batas ng Republika ng Pilipinas) protects "[t]he right of the people, including those employed in the public and private sectors, to form unions, associations, or societies for purposes not contrary to law". Article XIII, Section 3 guarantees "the rights of all workers to self-organization, collective bargaining and negotiations, and peaceful concerted activities". But the Labor Code of the Philippines (Presidential Decree No. 442, as amended), Article 269 (renumbered Article 284), provides: "All aliens, natural or juridical, as well as foreign organizations are strictly prohibited from engaging directly or indirectly in all forms of trade union activities." The only exception covers "aliens working in the country with valid permits issued by the Department of Labor and Employment", and then only if "said aliens are nationals of a country which grants the same or similar rights to Filipino workers" (as amended by Republic Act No. 6715, 1989). Article 270 further bars foreign assistance to unions without the Labor Secretary's permission. Union rights are therefore unavailable to irregular migrants, to foreign workers without an Alien Employment Permit (including many exempt or visa-based workers), and to nationals of non-reciprocating states. Even eligible permit-holders depend on reciprocity. Membership of non-union associations is not expressly barred, but the Bureau of Immigration's political-activity ban limits foreigners' participation in political organizations.

### q89 · Right to freedom of assembly.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines on terms commensurate with citizens. Article III, Section 4 of the 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines (Saligang Batas ng Republika ng Pilipinas) protects "the right of the people peaceably to assemble and petition the government for redress of grievances." The provision is not expressly limited to citizens, and the ICCPR (Art. 21), ratified in 1986, has statute rank. However, Bureau of Immigration Operations Order No. SBM-2015-025 (2015), as reported by the Bureau, prohibits foreigners from "joining, supporting, contributing, or involving themselves in any rally, assembly, or gathering" of a political character, whether for or against the government. Such participation is treated as a breach of the conditions under which a nonimmigrant was admitted. That is a deportation ground under Section 37(a)(7) of Commonwealth Act No. 613 (Philippine Immigration Act of 1940), which may be invoked "at any time after entry" (Sec. 37(b)). Breaches are punished with deportation and blacklisting. As a result, tourists, foreign workers, students and missionaries cannot take part in political rallies that citizens may lawfully join. Irregular migrants face removal regardless of what they do. The constitutional guarantee is therefore not available to migrants on equal terms for political assemblies; peaceful non-political gatherings, such as religious or social ones, are not targeted.


## Thought, conscience, and religion or belief

### q81 · Right of freedom of thought, conscience and religion or belief.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually; Effectively Always

National law in Philippines protects this right in Article III, Section 5 of the 1987 Constitution of the Republic of the Philippines (Saligang Batas ng Republika ng Pilipinas), which provides: "No law shall be made respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof. The free exercise and enjoyment of religious profession and worship, without discrimination or preference, shall forever be allowed. No religious test shall be required for the exercise of civil or political rights." Unlike the constitutional provisions that reserve suffrage, professions and parts of the economy to citizens, Section 5 contains no nationality limitation. It sits in a Bill of Rights whose general guarantees of due process and equal protection are addressed to "any person" (Art. III, Sec. 1). Freedom of thought and conscience is protected alongside religion and by the free speech and expression clause (Art. III, Sec. 4). For refugees, stateless persons and asylum seekers, Executive Order No. 163 (2022), Section 1, lists "freedom of religion" among the minimum standards of treatment that "shall be assured." The protection therefore covers migrants generally, whatever their status, on the same terms as citizens. One limit applies: foreigners who come to do religious work need an appropriate (missionary) visa. The Bureau of Immigration treats activities outside the visa's purpose as a breach of the conditions of admission, which is a deportation ground under Section 37(a)(7) of Commonwealth Act No. 613 (Philippine Immigration Act of 1940). This regulates immigration status rather than belief or worship as such. The ICCPR (Art. 18), ratified in 1986 and given statute rank after Senate concurrence, reinforces the guarantee.


## Victims of crime

### q43 · Right of victims of crime to access criminal proceedings.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Philippines protects this right in the 1987 Constitution, Article III, Sections 1 and 11, and the Revised Rules of Criminal Procedure, Rule 110, Sections 1-5. The Constitution guarantees that no "person shall be denied the equal protection of the laws" and that free access to the courts and quasi-judicial bodies "shall not be denied to any person by reason of poverty". Under Rule 110, a criminal action is instituted by filing a complaint with the prosecutor or the court. Section 3 defines a complaint as "a sworn written statement charging a person with an offense, subscribed by the offended party, any peace officer, or other public officer". The "offended party" is not limited by nationality or immigration status, so a migrant victim may file a complaint, take part in preliminary investigation and testify, on the same terms as a citizen. Under the Rules of Court the offended party may also pursue the civil liability arising from the offence within the criminal case. Specific statutes strengthen access for some migrant victims. Republic Act No. 9208, Sec. 19, as amended by RA 11862 (2022), entitles foreign trafficking victims to "appropriate protection, assistance and services", including interpreters and embassy coordination. It permits their "continued presence" in the Philippines as needed to prosecute offenders. RA 11862 also tasks the DOJ with free legal assistance for trafficked persons. In practice, immigration enforcement can cut off access: an irregular migrant who is detained and deported may be unable to pursue a complaint.

### q45 · Right of victims of crime to immigration status or relief from expulsion.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Rarely

National law in Philippines protects this right in Republic Act No. 9208 (Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act of 2003), Section 19, as amended by Republic Act No. 11862 (Expanded Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act of 2022), Section 12, together with RA 9208, Section 17. Section 19 provides that "trafficked persons in the Philippines who are nationals of a foreign country shall also be entitled to appropriate protection, assistance and services available to trafficked persons under this Act ... Provided, That they shall be permitted continued presence in the Philippines for a length of time prescribed by the Council as necessary to effect the prosecution of offenders." Section 17 provides that trafficked persons "shall not be penalized for crimes directly related to the acts of trafficking". RA 11862 also directs the DFA, with the Bureau of Immigration and the DOJ, to "adopt measures and policies to protect the rights and needs of victims who are foreign nationals or asylum seekers, refugees, stateless applicants and stateless persons". Together these give foreign trafficking victims a statutory relief from expulsion while their case is prosecuted. The protection is narrow. It covers only trafficking victims, not victims of other crimes such as domestic violence under RA 9262. The length of stay is set by the Inter-Agency Council Against Trafficking (IACAT) and tied to the prosecution. There is no dedicated visa or route to longer-term residence. Commonwealth Act No. 613 contains no victim-based immigration status. Other relief depends on discretion, such as the President's power under Sec. 47(a)(2) to admit non-immigrants not otherwise provided for.


## Vulnerable migrants

### q3 · Right of child migrants to special protections.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Philippines protects this right in Commonwealth Act No. 613 (Philippine Immigration Act of 1940), Sec. 29(a)(11)-(12); Republic Act No. 9208 (Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act of 2003, as amended by RA 10364 and RA 11862), Secs. 2 and 19; and the Convention on the Rights of the Child, which has statute rank after Senate concurrence (1987 Constitution, Art. VII §21). The Immigration Act treats unaccompanied children as a distinct class at the border. Sec. 29(a)(12) lists "children under fifteen years of age, unaccompanied or not coming to a parent" and lets the Commissioner admit them at his discretion. Sec. 29(a)(11) treats as a special case a person who is "helpless from ... infancy" and needs the protection of an accompanying guardian. The anti-trafficking statute was enacted to combat trafficking "especially [of] women and children", and its policy clause (Sec. 2) invokes the CRC. Under Sec. 19, foreign trafficking victims in the Philippines are "entitled to appropriate protection, assistance and services" and "permitted continued presence". Child victims receive the specialised care the Act provides. The refugee procedure also contained child-specific rules. DOJ Department Circular No. 058 (2012), Sec. 18, required the Refugees and Stateless Persons Protection Unit (RSPPU) to refer unaccompanied minors to agencies for "care, welfare, and access to legal services". Sec. 31 required protection officers to respect a child applicant's "right to be heard". DC 058 was replaced by DOJ Department Circular No. 024 (2022), whose text could not be read for this coding. The protection is fragmented: CA 613 has no best-interests test, and there is no statutory bar on detaining migrant children. Treaty bodies, including CERD, have repeatedly called for specific rules for asylum-seeking and unaccompanied children.

### q5 · Right of women migrants to special protections.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Rarely; Sometimes

National law in Philippines protects this right in Republic Act No. 9208 (Anti-Trafficking in Persons Act of 2003, as amended by RA 10364 and RA 11862), title and Secs. 2 and 19, and in Republic Act No. 9262 (Anti-Violence Against Women and Their Children Act of 2004), Secs. 2-3, together with CEDAW (statute rank after Senate concurrence). The anti-trafficking law was enacted "to eliminate trafficking in persons especially women and children". Its policy clause (Sec. 2) commits the State to "mitigate pressures for involuntary migration" and invokes the Migrant Workers Convention and the Palermo Protocol. Sec. 19 extends to trafficked persons "who are nationals of a foreign country" the "appropriate protection, assistance and services available to trafficked persons". It also grants them "continued presence in the Philippines" for as long as the Inter-Agency Council Against Trafficking deems necessary for prosecution. Victims are not penalised for crimes directly related to their trafficking (Sec. 17). RA 9262 protects "a woman" and her child from physical, sexual, psychological and economic violence by a husband or partner, with no nationality condition, so foreign women in the Philippines can obtain its protection orders. The protection is not tied to migration status as such. There is no immigration-law provision for, say, an independent residence permit for abused foreign spouses, and most women-specific services are delivered through trafficking and anti-violence frameworks rather than immigration rules.

### q7 · Rights of migrants with disabilities to special protection.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Philippines. Commonwealth Act No. 613 (Philippine Immigration Act of 1940), the main immigration statute, mentions disability only to restrict entry. Sec. 29(a)(1) excludes "idiots or insane persons and persons who have been insane". Sec. 29(a)(2) excludes persons afflicted with "epilepsy" or certain diseases. Sec. 29(a)(5) excludes "persons likely to become a public charge". Under Sec. 28, medical officers certify "any and all physical and mental defects" of arriving aliens to immigration officers. Sec. 29(a)(11) also allows the exclusion of persons accompanying an excluded person "who is helpless from mental or physical disability". Executive Order No. 163 (2022) guarantees protection services to refugees, stateless persons and asylum seekers, but it contains no provision on persons with disabilities. Neither does the superseded refugee procedure, DOJ Department Circular No. 058 (2012). The general disability statutes (the Magna Carta for Persons with Disability, RA 7277 as amended) and the CRPD do not require authorities to take disability into account in entry, stay, detention or removal decisions. These general frameworks do not create a migration-specific special protection for migrants with disabilities.
