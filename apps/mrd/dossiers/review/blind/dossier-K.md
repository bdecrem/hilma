# Dossier K — Norway


## Civil and political life

### q91 · Right of regular migrants to vote in local and/or regional elections.
- Law: No right to vote in any election
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

Being lawfully present does not create a legal right for migrants to vote in Norway. The Election Act (Lov om valg til Stortinget, fylkesting og kommunestyrer (valgloven), LOV-2023-06-16-62), § 2-1, limits the vote in parliamentary (Storting) elections to Norwegian citizens aged 18 or over who are or have been registered as resident in Norway. Foreign nationals can vote only in county council and municipal council elections, and only after a qualifying period. Under § 2-2(3), persons who are not Norwegian citizens have the right to vote if they are 18 or over and either 'have been registered in the population register as resident in Norway for the last three years before election day' or 'are citizens of another Nordic country and have been registered as resident in Norway no later than 30 June in the election year'. Registration as resident under the Population Register Act (Lov om folkeregistrering (folkeregisterloven), LOV-2016-12-09-88), § 4-1, itself requires that a person 'stays lawfully in a Norwegian municipality for at least six months'. Asylum seekers awaiting a decision, short-stay permit holders and irregular migrants therefore cannot meet the requirement. Non-Nordic migrants gain the local vote only after three years of registered residence, not through lawful presence as such. Nordic citizens gain it almost at once, but that rests on nationality rather than lawful presence. No foreign national can vote in national elections. As in the MRD's coding of Denmark and Sweden, which apply the same three-year rule, lawful presence alone does not create the right.

### q93 · Right of regular migrants to stand for election.
- Law: No right to run for public office in any election
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

Being lawfully present does not create a legal right for migrants to stand for election in Norway. Under the Election Act (Lov om valg til Stortinget, fylkesting og kommunestyrer (valgloven), LOV-2023-06-16-62), § 3-1, only 'persons who have the right to vote in Storting elections' and are not excluded under § 3-3 are eligible for the Storting. Because § 2-1 restricts that vote to Norwegian citizens, no migrant can stand for national office. Under § 3-2, persons who have the right to vote in county and municipal elections are eligible for the county council or municipal council if they are registered as resident in the municipality (or a municipality of the county) on election day and are not excluded under § 3-3. Foreign nationals can therefore stand only in local and county elections, and only if they meet the voting conditions in § 2-2(3). They must have been registered as resident in Norway for the three years before election day or, for Nordic citizens, by 30 June of the election year. Registration as resident under the Population Register Act (LOV-2016-12-09-88), § 4-1, requires lawful stay of at least six months. Lawfully present migrants without three years of registered residence, including recent permit holders and asylum seekers awaiting a decision, cannot stand for any office. Following the MRD's treatment of Denmark and Sweden, which use the same three-year rule, lawful presence alone does not create the right.


## Due process

### q35 · Right to equal protection of the law before courts.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Constitution of the Kingdom of Norway (Kongeriket Norges Grunnlov, LOV-1814-05-17), §§ 95 and 98. Section 95 provides that "everyone has the right to have their case decided by an independent and impartial court within reasonable time" and that proceedings "shall be fair and public". Section 98 provides that "all are equal before the law" and that "no human being may be subjected to unfair or disproportionate differential treatment". Both provisions speak of "everyone" (enhver, alle). They are not tied to citizenship and cover all migrants, whatever their status. The same guarantee follows from ECHR Article 6 and ICCPR Article 14, which the Human Rights Act (menneskerettsloven, LOV-1999-05-21-30) makes part of Norwegian law with precedence over ordinary statutes. Before courts and tribunals the general procedural codes apply without distinction by nationality: the Criminal Procedure Act (straffeprosessloven, LOV-1981-05-22-25) and the Dispute Act (tvisteloven). Decisions of the Immigration Appeals Board can be challenged in the ordinary courts under the Immigration Act (utlendingsloven, LOV-2008-05-15-35) § 79. Supporting rules help non-Norwegian speakers take part on equal terms. The Courts of Justice Act (domstolloven, LOV-1915-08-13-5) § 135 requires an interpreter whenever someone who does not speak Norwegian takes part in proceedings. The Interpreters Act (tolkeloven, LOV-2021-06-11-79) applies to the courts and the prosecuting authority (§ 2). Some limits exist in practice. Free legal aid in immigration matters is limited to listed case types, and access to some remedies depends on status, but equal standing before the courts is not restricted by status.

### q37 · Right to government-provided counsel in proceedings related to migrant status.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (utlendingsloven, LOV-2008-05-15-35), § 92. A foreign national has the right to "free legal advice without means-testing both at first instance and on any appeal" in five kinds of case: refusal of entry (bortvisning); expulsion; revocation of a residence permit or residence document; protection or non-refoulement cases involving unaccompanied minors; and exclusion under § 31. Expulsion on criminal grounds is excluded unless the person has minor children living in Norway. Asylum seekers and others who invoke protection against return under § 73 get free legal advice, without a means test, when they appeal a negative decision by the Directorate of Immigration (UDI). In court the State must provide counsel. Under § 92(3) the court "shall of its own motion and without means-testing grant free legal representation" in four situations: when the State sues to overturn an Immigration Appeals Board decision; when the court reviews detention under § 106, where counsel should be appointed as early as possible; when it reviews seizure or reporting orders; and when the State appeals a case the migrant won. Section 82 requires the police to tell migrants facing refusal of entry, expulsion or revocation, and anyone claiming refugee status, that they have the right to legal aid. The coverage is broad but not universal. Ordinary applications for work, study or family permits are outside § 92 and fall under the means-tested Legal Aid Act (rettshjelpsloven). Adult asylum seekers at first instance rely on information and interview safeguards rather than an appointed lawyer, and the assistance is paid at a fixed number of hours.

### q39 · Right to government-provided interpretation in criminal proceedings.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Courts of Justice Act (domstolloven, LOV-1915-08-13-5), § 135, read together with the Interpreters Act (tolkeloven, LOV-2021-06-11-79), §§ 2, 6 and 7. Section 135 provides that "if anyone who does not know Norwegian is to take part in the proceedings, an interpreter appointed or approved by the court shall be used". In criminal cases the interpreter cannot be dispensed with merely because the court members know the language and the parties waive it. That waiver is allowed only "in cases other than criminal cases". Under § 136, procedural documents in a foreign language must be translated, and in criminal cases the translation must be certified by a state-authorised translator or a court-approved interpreter. The Interpreters Act applies to "the courts and the prosecuting authority" (§ 2). Under § 6 public bodies must use an interpreter where needed "to safeguard legal certainty", and under § 7 that interpreter must be qualified. The interpreter is engaged by the court or the police as a public body, so the cost falls on the State. The Criminal Procedure Act's costs provisions (§§ 436-437) let a convicted person be ordered to pay costs only according to their means; I did not find a provision listing interpreter fees as recoverable. ECHR Article 6(3)(e) guarantees the free assistance of an interpreter and is incorporated through the Human Rights Act with precedence over statute. The right covers every accused person and party who does not understand Norwegian, regardless of nationality or immigration status.

### q41 · Right to be protected from prosecution for unauthorized entry.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Norway. Unauthorised entry is a criminal offence. Under the Immigration Act (utlendingsloven, LOV-2008-05-15-35), § 108(2)(a), anyone who "intentionally or negligently" breaches § 8(1) or § 9(1) is punishable "by a fine or imprisonment of up to six months or both". Section 8(1) requires a foreigner arriving in Norway to carry a passport or other approved travel document. Section 9(1) requires a visa unless an exemption applies. Entering in breach of an entry ban (§ 71(2) or § 124(1)) carries up to two years' imprisonment under § 108(3)(e). That penalty is reduced to a fine in some situations, for example where the person has not been expelled following a criminal sentence. The Border Act (grenseloven, LOV-2018-04-20-8) adds a separate offence: § 26 penalises crossing the external Schengen border outside a designated crossing point or failing to present oneself for border control (§ 13), with a fine or up to six months' imprisonment. The protections are partial. Under Immigration Act § 9(1), a person at the Norwegian border who seeks asylum or invokes protection against refoulement has a right to enter without a visa. This does not apply where the Council of State has activated the crisis or Nordic-border rules in § 9(1)(a)-(b). Section 108(4)(b) exempts those who help a refugee reach the first safe country from the smuggling offence, but it does not exempt the migrant. Crossing internal Schengen borders is lawful anywhere when internal border controls are not in force (Border Act § 14). The law contains no general exemption from criminal sanctions for migrants who enter without authorisation.


## Education

### q127 · Right to equal access to public adult education institutions.
- Law: Yes, but only for certain visa types and migrant statuses (e.g., economic, family, humanitarian, temporary or regular migrants)
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway conditionally protects this right in the Education Act (opplæringslova, LOV-2023-06-09-30), §§ 5-1, 5-9, 18-2, 18-3, 18-9 and 18-10, and in the Universities and University Colleges Act (Lov om universiteter og høyskoler (universitets- og høyskoleloven), LOV-2024-03-08-9), §§ 2-5 and 2-6. Adults who have completed primary education, or upper secondary education abroad that is not recognised, have a right to upper secondary education, including vocational training, under § 18-3. Section 18-2 gives a right to preparatory (primary-level) education for adults. Section 18-9 makes adult education free on the same terms as for children. Section 18-10, however, makes "lawful residence in the country" a condition for these rights. Adults awaiting a decision on a residence application have no right to upper secondary education for adults. Under § 5-9, youths awaiting a decision keep their right to upper secondary education only until the school year in which they turn 18, and only until a final refusal. Irregular migrants are therefore excluded. Admission to higher education is based on qualifications, and § 2-5 makes higher education at state institutions free. Under § 2-6, though, state institutions "shall require tuition fees from students who are citizens of countries outside the EEA or Switzerland". Exempt are those entitled to equal treatment under international agreements, those eligible for state student loans and grants on the same terms as Norwegians (which covers many refugees and family migrants), doctoral candidates and exchange students. Access thus matches citizens' access for EEA nationals and settled or protected migrants, but not for other third-country nationals or irregular migrants.


## Equal protection

### q131 · Right to be free from discrimination on the basis of migrant status.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Norway. The Equality and Anti-Discrimination Act (Lov om likestilling og forbud mot diskriminering (likestillings- og diskrimineringsloven), LOV-2017-06-16-51), § 6, prohibits discrimination on a closed list of grounds. These are gender, pregnancy, parental leave, care responsibilities, ethnicity, religion, belief, disability, sexual orientation, gender identity, gender expression and age. "Ethnicity" is defined to include "national origin, descent, skin colour and language". Neither citizenship, immigration status nor migrant status is a listed ground. The preparatory works state that citizenship (statsborgerskap) is not covered. The Anti-Discrimination Tribunal has drawn the same line, noting that national origin is not the same as nationality, which usually means citizenship (DIN-2023-552, Russian national excluded from hiring on security grounds; no breach found). Distinctions based on citizenship can be challenged only indirectly, if they amount to indirect ethnic discrimination under § 8. Section 98 of the Constitution of the Kingdom of Norway (Kongeriket Norges Grunnlov, LOV-1814-05-17) provides that "all are equal before the law" and that no one may be subjected to "unfair or disproportionate differential treatment". This is a general equality clause, and no court ruling was found applying it as a ban on discrimination on grounds of migrant status. CERD is incorporated through Equality Act § 5, and ECHR Article 14 through the Human Rights Act. Both allow justified distinctions between citizens and non-citizens, and the Immigration Act is built on distinctions by status. Norwegian law therefore does not prohibit discrimination against migrants because they are migrants.


## Expulsion, asylum, and non-refoulement

### q47 · Right to maintain immigration status after convictions.
- Law: only major convictions (e.g., criminal offenses) can lead to loss of residence status
- Implementation: Sometimes

National law in Norway regulates the effect of convictions on residence status in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), chapter 8, §§ 66-70. Expulsion ends the right to stay and carries an entry ban. The thresholds rise with the strength of the person's status. A foreigner with a temporary permit may be expelled under § 67 when, within the last year in Norway, they have "served or been sentenced to a penalty … for an offence that can lead to imprisonment of more than one year". Expulsion is also possible for a listed set of lesser Penal Code offences, including threats (§ 263), bodily harm (§ 271(1)), negligent drug offences and grossly negligent fraud. Further grounds cover terrorism offences (ch. 18), exclusion grounds under § 31 and certain serious road-traffic offences. A permanent resident may be expelled under § 68 only for offences carrying "imprisonment of two years or more", or for a shorter listed set of offences. Under § 69, Norwegian citizens and foreigners born in Norway who have lived there continuously since birth cannot be expelled. Under § 70, no one may be expelled where, given "the seriousness of the offence and the foreigner's ties to the realm", it would be "a disproportionate measure" against them or their closest family. In cases affecting children, the child's best interests are a fundamental consideration. For people without a residence permit the bar is much lower. Under § 66, gross or repeated breaches of the Immigration Act itself (administrative violations) are enough, as are fines for minor theft, embezzlement or fraud. For lawful residents, only criminal convictions, mostly above a set penalty threshold, can cost them their status. EEA nationals fall under a separate public-policy test (§§ 121-122).

### q49 · Right to not return to a risk of torture or inhumane treatment.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), § 28(1)(b) and chapter 9, § 73. Under § 28(1)(b), a foreigner is recognised as a refugee and has a right to asylum where, even outside the Refugee Convention definition, they face "a real risk of being subjected to the death penalty, torture or other inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment upon return to the home country". § 73(2) then sets an absolute bar: "A foreigner cannot be sent to an area where he or she would be in a situation as mentioned in § 28 first paragraph letter b." Unlike the protection against return to persecution, this protection has no exception for criminals or security risks. Under § 73(3) it also covers sending a person to an area where they would not be safe from onward removal to such a place (chain refoulement). Under § 73(4) it applies to "all forms of decisions under the Act", including refusal of entry, expulsion and Dublin transfers. Expulsion orders against excluded persons or security risks may still be made, but they "cannot be enforced until the basis for the protection against removal has ceased". Under § 74, a person whose only basis for staying is this protection may receive a temporary permit, which may exclude the right to work. The protection covers all foreigners regardless of status, including irregular migrants and convicted persons. ECHR Article 3 also applies directly as Norwegian law through the Human Rights Act.

### q51 · Right to not return to a risk of persecution.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), § 28(1)(a), §§ 29-30 and § 73(1). Under § 28(1)(a), a foreigner is recognised as a refugee who "has a well-founded fear of persecution because of ethnicity, descent, skin colour, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or because of political opinion", expressly referring to Article 1A of the 1951 Refugee Convention and its 1967 Protocol. § 30 defines the grounds in more detail. Religion includes belief and non-belief. Nationality includes statelessness and ethnic or linguistic identity. A particular social group includes former victims of trafficking. Political opinion covers opinions attributed to the applicant by the persecutor. Under § 73(1), "a foreigner cannot be sent to an area where he or she would be in a situation as mentioned in § 28 first paragraph letter a". The only exceptions are a foreigner excluded under § 31, or one who is reasonably regarded as a danger to national security or has been finally convicted of a particularly serious crime and is therefore a danger to Norwegian society, mirroring Article 33(2) of the Refugee Convention. Even then, § 73(2) still bars return where the person would face torture or inhuman treatment. The protection covers chain refoulement (§ 73(3)) and "all forms of decisions under the Act" (§ 73(4)). It applies to all foreigners regardless of status.

### q53 · Right to not return to a deprivations of fundamental human rights.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), § 28(1)(b) and § 73(2)-(4). § 28(1)(b) grants refugee status and asylum to anyone who "faces a real risk of being subjected to the death penalty, torture or other inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment upon return". This covers the most serious threats to life and physical integrity, including the death penalty. § 73(2) bars without exception any removal to such a situation: "A foreigner cannot be sent to an area where he or she would be in a situation as mentioned in § 28 first paragraph letter b." The bar extends to onward removal from a third country (§ 73(3)) and "applies to all forms of decisions under the Act" (§ 73(4)). An expulsion order against an excluded person or security risk "cannot be enforced until the basis for the protection against removal has ceased". Under § 74, a person protected only by § 73 may receive a temporary permit until the obstacle is gone. The protection covers all foreigners, including irregular migrants and convicted offenders. The Immigration Act does not list a separate ground for other serious human-rights violations beyond those in § 28(1)(b). However, ECHR Articles 2 and 3 and ICCPR Articles 6 and 7 bind Norway domestically, with precedence over statutes, through the Human Rights Act (menneskerettsloven, LOV-1999-05-21-30). They therefore also limit removal decisions.

### q55 · Right to be advised of availability of asylum before expulsion.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35) § 82 and § 18, the Public Administration Act (Lov om behandlingsmåten i forvaltningssaker (forvaltningsloven), LOV-1967-02-10) § 11, and the Border Act (Lov om grensetilsyn og grensekontroll av personer (grenseloven), LOV-2018-04-20-8) § 8 first paragraph no. 6. Section 82 requires that "in cases of rejection [bortvisning], expulsion, revocation of a permit … and when a foreign national claims to be a refugee, the police shall give guidance" on the right to counsel and legal aid, and on the right to contact a representative of UNHCR and a Norwegian refugee organisation. Under § 18, a rejection case goes from the police to the Directorate of Immigration (UDI) once the person says they are a refugee or gives information suggesting protection under § 73. Public Administration Act § 11 adds a general duty of guidance: the authority must, on its own initiative, assess a party's need for guidance and inform them about "applicable laws and regulations and usual practice". This matches the general-guidance reasoning the MRD accepted for Denmark. Since 12 June 2026, Border Act § 8 makes the EU Screening Regulation (EU) 2024/1356 Norwegian law. Its Article 11(1)(b) requires that third-country nationals subject to screening are informed of "the right to apply for international protection and the applicable rules on making an application". Scope: the explicit asylum-information duty covers people screened at or after crossing the external Schengen border. For other people facing rejection or expulsion, the duty rests on § 82 (counsel, legal aid, contact with UNHCR) and the general duty of guidance, not on a specific statement that asylum is available.

### q57 · Right to be protected from chain refoulement.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35) § 73. The first and second paragraphs bar removal to an area where the person would face persecution (§ 28 first paragraph (a)) or a real risk of the death penalty, torture or other inhuman or degrading treatment (§ 28 first paragraph (b)). The third paragraph expressly covers chain refoulement: "The protection under the first and second paragraphs also applies to removal to an area where the person would not be safe from being sent on to such an area as mentioned in § 28 first paragraph." The fourth paragraph makes the protection apply "to all forms of decision under the Act". This includes rejection at the border, expulsion, and Dublin or safe-third-country transfers under § 32. Expulsion decisions may still be taken against a protected person on certain grounds, but they "cannot be enforced until the basis for the protection against removal has ceased". The protection against risks under § 28(1)(b) (torture, inhuman treatment, death penalty) has no exceptions. For persecution risks under § 28(1)(a), it does not apply to persons excluded under § 31 or to those who are a danger to national security or have been finally convicted of a particularly serious crime, as the Refugee Convention Art. 33(2) allows. The rule protects all foreign nationals, whatever their status, and is reinforced by ECHR Art. 3 through the Human Rights Act (menneskerettsloven).

### q59 · Right to protection against expulsion except following a legal judgment.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35) chapter 8 (§§ 66-72) and § 17, read with §§ 80-81 and the Public Administration Act (forvaltningsloven, LOV-1967-02-10). Expulsion (utvisning) is allowed only on the grounds listed in § 66 (no residence permit), § 67 (temporary permit) and § 68 (permanent permit). Each ground turns on the individual's own conduct, for example where the person "has grossly or repeatedly violated" the Act, gave materially false information, or was sentenced for an offence punishable by imprisonment. Rejection at entry (bortvisning) likewise requires a listed ground under § 17. Section 70 adds a mandatory individual proportionality test: a foreign national "cannot be expelled if, in view of the seriousness of the matter and the foreign national's ties to the realm, it would be a disproportionate measure" against the person or close family members. The best interests of the child must be a fundamental consideration. Norwegian citizens and foreigners born in Norway who have lived there continuously since cannot be expelled (§ 69). Under § 72, the Directorate of Immigration makes the expulsion decision after the police prepare the case. Through § 80 the Public Administration Act applies, so the decision is an individual administrative decision that must be in writing and give reasons. Protocol 4 Art. 4 ECHR (ban on collective expulsion) is part of Norwegian law through the Human Rights Act. The protection covers all foreign nationals, with stronger protection the more secure their residence status.

### q61 · Right to submit reasons against expulsion and have case reviewed before deportation.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35) §§ 81, 76, 77 and 79, read with the Public Administration Act (forvaltningsloven, LOV-1967-02-10) through § 80. Section 81 provides that in protection cases (§§ 28 and 73) and in cases of rejection, expulsion or revocation of a permit, "the immigration authorities shall ensure that the foreign national has the opportunity to present his or her views in a language the foreign national can communicate adequately in". This must happen "as soon as possible, and in any event before a decision is made in the case". Separate rules guarantee children's right to be heard. Under § 76, police decisions can be appealed to the Directorate of Immigration (UDI), and UDI's first-instance decisions to the Immigration Appeals Board (UNE). UNE decides "as an independent body" led by persons qualified to be judges (§ 77). Under § 79, the legality of UNE and UDI decisions can be challenged in the ordinary courts, at Oslo District Court. A person may also ask for enforcement to be suspended pending appeal or court review (Public Administration Act § 42; interim measures under the Dispute Act). Limits: § 90 c allows rejection or expulsion decisions against people without a residence permit to be enforced at once. Refusals of first-time permits may be enforced 48 hours after notice, or sooner if the application is manifestly unfounded. In those cases the appeal or court review may be decided after the person has left. The right to be heard before the decision still applies in every case.


## Family

### q73 · Right of settled migrants to family reunification.
- Law: Conditional
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway conditionally protects this right in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), chapter 6, §§ 40, 41, 41 a, 42 and 58, and in the Immigration Regulations (utlendingsforskriften, FOR-2009-10-15-1286), §§ 10-8, 10-9 and 10-11. Under § 40 a spouse "has the right to a residence permit" when the reference person is a foreign national with permanent residence, or with lawful residence on a permit that "can form the basis for permanent residence", or holds collective protection under § 34. Cohabitants of at least two years (§ 41) and unmarried children under 18 (§ 42) have the same right. The right comes with conditions. Both partners must be over 18, and in family-establishment cases over 24 unless the marriage is obviously voluntary (§ 41 a). The couple must intend to live together, and the permit can be refused for marriages of convenience. Under § 40 a, some reference persons must also have four years of work or education in Norway. Most importantly, § 58 and regulation § 10-8 require the reference person to show secured future income of at least 3.2 times the National Insurance basic amount (about NOK 400,000, raised from 2.7 G on 1 February 2025). Regulation § 10-9 also requires the same level of past income in the last tax assessment, and benefits under the Integration Act do not count. Refugees and collective-protection holders are exempt if the marriage existed before entry and they apply within six months (§ 10-8, fourth paragraph). Discretionary exemptions apply for particularly strong humanitarian grounds (§ 10-11). Parents of adult migrants and other non-dependent relatives have no general right. Only narrow avenues exist, such as § 43 for parents of minors with protection and § 46 for single parents whose children all live in Norway. Holders of temporary permits that do not lead to permanent residence (for example some students and seasonal workers) fall under separate, narrower regulation rules.

### q75 · Right to judicial remedy for denials of right to family reunification.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually; Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (utlendingsloven, LOV-2008-05-15-35), §§ 76, 77 and 79, read with the Dispute Act (tvisteloven, LOV-2005-06-17-90) and the Public Administration Act (forvaltningsloven), which applies through Immigration Act § 80. Under § 76, decisions of the Directorate of Immigration (UDI) "may be appealed to the Immigration Appeals Board". Under § 77, the Board (UNE) decides appeals "as an independent body" and is led by a director and board leaders who must meet the qualifications for judges. After the administrative appeal, § 79 provides judicial review. Lawsuits against the State over "the lawfulness of the Immigration Appeals Board's decisions" are brought against the State represented by UNE, and are filed with Oslo District Court. Appeals then lie to the Court of Appeal and the Supreme Court. Applicants can ask the courts for an interim injunction. The courts fully review whether the law was applied correctly and whether a refusal complies with the Constitution § 102 and ECHR Article 8, which is incorporated with precedence by the Human Rights Act. All applicants for family immigration under chapter 6 have this remedy, whether the reference person is a citizen, a settled migrant or a refugee. One limit is cost: free legal aid under the Legal Aid Act is not generally available for family-immigration litigation, and losing applicants may be ordered to pay the State's costs.

### q77 · Right to recognition of same-sex marital relationships.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (utlendingsloven, LOV-2008-05-15-35), §§ 40 and 41, read with the Marriage Act (Lov om ekteskap (ekteskapsloven), LOV-1991-07-04-47), § 1. Under § 40 an applicant "who is the spouse of a reference person" has the right to a residence permit when the reference person is a Norwegian or Nordic citizen, a holder of permanent residence, a lawful resident on a permit that can lead to permanent residence, or a holder of collective protection. The Act does not define "spouse" by gender. Since 1 January 2009, the Marriage Act § 1 has provided that "two persons of opposite or same sex may enter into marriage", so same-sex spouses count as spouses for family immigration in the same way as different-sex spouses. Section 41 likewise grants a right to a residence permit to cohabitants of at least two years, or with a child together, in gender-neutral terms. Same-sex couples who cannot marry in their home country can therefore use the cohabitation route. Same-sex spouses and partners face the same conditions as other couples: the income requirement in Immigration Regulations § 10-8, the 18/24-year age rules in Act § 41 a, the requirement to live together, and checks against marriages of convenience. The anti-discrimination ban on sexual orientation in the Equality and Anti-Discrimination Act (likestillings- og diskrimineringsloven, LOV-2017-06-16-51), § 6, also applies to public administration.

### q79 · Right to family reunification without resource conditions
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Norway. The Immigration Act (utlendingsloven, LOV-2008-05-15-35), § 58, makes subsistence a general condition: "requirements of subsistence and housing are imposed for a residence permit to be granted". Only people with protection under § 28 second paragraph or covered by non-refoulement in § 73 are exempt, and that exemption covers their own permits, not their family members' permits. For family immigration, the Immigration Regulations (utlendingsforskriften, FOR-2009-10-15-1286), § 10-8, require the reference person to show secured future income of at least 3.2 times the National Insurance basic amount (about NOK 400,000 a year from 1 February 2025). The income can come from work, specified National Insurance benefits, pensions or student finance, but not social assistance. Section 10-9 adds a matching past-income requirement and excludes Integration Act benefits from counting. For spouses and cohabitants, a third-party guarantee is not accepted. Housing is required only in cases under Act §§ 46-49 (regulation § 10-12). There are exemptions, but they are limited. The spouse, cohabitant or children of a refugee (§ 28) or collective-protection holder (§ 34) are exempt only if the marriage existed before entry and the application is made within six months of the sponsor's permit. Children of trafficking victims and child sponsors are also exempt. Otherwise UDI has discretion to waive the requirement for "particularly strong humanitarian considerations" or when the sponsor is obviously able to provide support (§ 10-11). Settled migrants generally have no right to family reunification regardless of resources.

### q137 · Right to citizenship for stateless migrants.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes; Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Norwegian Nationality Act (Lov om norsk statsborgerskap (statsborgerloven), LOV-2005-06-10-51) §§ 3, 4, 7 and 16, read with a 2016 Ministry instruction on stateless applicants born in Norway. § 4(2) gives automatic citizenship only to foundlings: "a foundling found in the realm is a Norwegian citizen until otherwise established". § 6 prevents loss of citizenship acquired at birth where the child would become stateless. For other stateless persons the route is an application-based entitlement. Under § 7, everyone "has, on application, the right to Norwegian citizenship" when the conditions are met. § 16 lifts the age condition and the eight-year residence condition for stateless applicants. Stateless adults "must nevertheless have resided in the realm for the last three years with residence permits of at least one year". Persons who chose statelessness "by their own act or omission", or who "can easily become a citizen of another country", are excluded. § 3 makes the Act apply "with the limitations that follow from agreements with other states and international law", which brings in the 1961 Convention on the Reduction of Statelessness. On that basis, the Ministry of Justice's instruction of 28 October 2016 removed the lawful-residence requirement for stateless applicants born in Norway. They need only be resident and to have been continuously resident for three years. Limits: acquisition is not automatic at birth, and the clarified-identity and permanent-residence conditions in § 7(1)(a) and (d) remain for stateless persons not born in Norway. Norway also has no statutory definition of statelessness and no determination procedure.


## Health

### q119 · Right of access to health care services.
- Law: Yes, but only for certain visa types and migrant statuses (e.g., economic, family, humanitarian, temporary or regular migrants)
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right for some, but not all, migrants in the Patient and User Rights Act (Lov om pasient- og brukerrettigheter, LOV-1999-07-02-63), § 1-2, and the Regulation on health and care services for persons without permanent residence in Norway (Forskrift om rett til helse- og omsorgstjenester til personer uten fast opphold i riket, FOR-2011-12-16-1255), §§ 1-6. Under § 1-2, the Act "applies to everyone staying in the realm", but the King may by regulation "make exceptions from chapter 2 for persons who are not Norwegian citizens or do not have permanent residence in the realm". Regulation § 2 sets out who gets chapter 2 rights in full. These apply "only for persons who have lawful residence in the realm" and who either have permanent residence (a stay meant to last, or that has lasted, at least 12 months), are National Insurance members entitled to health benefits, or are covered by a reciprocal agreement. These migrants get municipal and specialist care on the same terms as citizens, and the Act's § 2-1 c gives a right to a regular GP to residents and to asylum seekers who are National Insurance members. Everyone else gets less. Everyone in the realm has the right to emergency care (§ 3) and to a specialist assessment (§ 1). Under § 5 they also have a right to care that "is absolutely necessary and cannot wait without danger of imminent death, permanent severely reduced function, serious injury or severe pain". The same section covers psychiatric care when there is serious danger, care before and after childbirth, abortion and infectious-disease care. Children under 18 who do not meet § 2 get necessary municipal and specialist care (§ 4). Adults without lawful residence who cannot care for themselves get necessary care services only until they must leave (§ 6). Irregular migrants and short-stay temporary residents therefore do not have access commensurate with citizens. They may also be billed for care.

### q123 · Right of access to public housing programs.
- Law: Yes, but only for certain visa types and migrant statuses (e.g., economic, family, humanitarian, temporary or regular migrants)
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right for some, but not all, migrants. Four instruments set the rules. The first is the Act on Housing Allowance and Municipal Housing Grants (Lov om bustøtte og kommunale bustadtilskot, LOV-2012-08-24-64), § 2. The second is the Act on Municipalities' Responsibility in the Social Housing Field (Lov om kommunenes ansvar på det boligsosiale feltet, LOV-2022-12-20-121), §§ 2 and 6. The third is the Social Services Act (sosialtjenesteloven, LOV-2009-12-18-131), §§ 2, 15 and 27. The fourth is the Regulation on social services for persons without fixed abode in Norway (FOR-2011-12-16-1251), §§ 1, 1a, 2 and 4. The state housing allowance (bustøtte) covers "persons who are registered in the population register and lawfully resident in Norway" (§ 2). Regular migrants who are registered residents therefore qualify on the same terms as citizens. Students without children are excluded whatever their nationality (§ 4). The Social Housing Act obliges municipalities to give individually tailored help to people disadvantaged on the housing market (§ 6). It covers everyone staying in the realm except "persons who do not have lawful residence in the realm", asylum seekers who have or can get state accommodation, and non-citizens without connection to the realm (§ 2). Under the Social Services Regulation, asylum seekers in state reception get only information and advice (§ 2). Persons without lawful residence get the same, and in an emergency "financial support and help to find temporary accommodation ... for a short period" until they could in practice leave (§ 4). Lawfully present non-citizens without an abode in Norway get similar time-limited help (§§ 1, 1a). Regular resident migrants therefore have access to publicly supported housing commensurate with citizens. Irregular migrants and asylum seekers are excluded apart from emergency or reception accommodation.

### q125 · Right to equal access to free primary education for migrant children.
- Law: Yes (including irregular migrants)
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Education Act (Lov om grunnskoleopplæringa og den vidaregåande opplæringa (opplæringslova), LOV-2023-06-09-30), §§ 2-1, 2-2 and 2-5. Section 2-1 provides that "children have the right to public primary and lower secondary education from the year they turn six until they have completed the 10th grade". The right applies "if the child is likely to be in Norway for more than three months in continuity". It must be fulfilled "as quickly as possible and at the latest within three months after the child has arrived in Norway". Section 2-2 adds a matching duty to attend school, and § 2-5 states that "primary and lower secondary education shall be free". The municipality may not charge pupils or parents for teaching materials, transport during school hours, camp school or other school trips. The right does not depend on nationality or residence status. The Directorate for Education and Training's (Udir) guidance applies it to children of asylum seekers. The preparatory works, as cited by the Police, state that unlawful residence does not affect the right or duty for children aged 6-16. Children of irregular migrants are therefore covered on the same terms as Norwegian children. The main limit is the requirement of a likely stay of more than three months, so short-term visitors may fall outside the right. Section 2-1 also lets the Ministry temporarily extend the three-month deadline if very large numbers of displaced children arrive from Ukraine. Sections 3-6 and 3-7 add special language instruction and introductory classes for newly arrived pupils. The CRC and ICESCR, both incorporated by the Human Rights Act (menneskerettsloven, LOV-1999-05-21-30), reinforce the right.


## Labor

### q95 · Right to equal pay for the same work.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Equality and Anti-Discrimination Act (Lov om likestilling og forbud mot diskriminering (likestillings- og diskrimineringsloven), LOV-2017-06-16-51), §§ 6 and 29, and in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), § 23. Section 6 prohibits discrimination on grounds of 'ethnicity', which 'includes national origin, descent, skin colour and language'. Section 29 applies that prohibition to 'all aspects of an employment relationship', expressly including 'pay and working conditions' (letter d). Under § 4 the Act cannot be contracted out of. For non-EEA workers, § 23(1)(b) of the Immigration Act makes a work permit conditional on 'pay and working conditions [being] no worse than under the applicable collective agreement or pay scale for the industry', or, where none exists, no worse than 'what is normal for the place and occupation'. UDI sets what counts as pay (Immigration Regulations, FOR-2009-10-15-1286, § 6-10). The same requirement applies to posted workers and contractors (§ 24(1)(b)). Norway has no general statutory minimum wage. Collective agreements declared generally applicable set minimum wages in certain sectors, and the principle of equal pay is otherwise enforced through the ban on discrimination. The explicit 'equal pay for work of equal value' clause (§ 34) covers sex only, not ethnicity or nationality. The protection covers all employees regardless of nationality, since the Act 'applies in all areas of society' (§ 2). The § 23 wage floor applies specifically to permit-holding labour migrants.

### q97 · Right to freedom of choice of employment.
- Law: Workers can freely change employers within a specific sector
- Implementation: Usually; Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Regulations (Forskrift om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsforskriften), FOR-2009-10-15-1286), §§ 6-1 and 6-11, made under the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), § 23. Section 6-1, last paragraph, provides that a skilled-worker permit under its first paragraph 'applies to a specific type of work', while permits under the second and fifth paragraphs 'apply to specific work for a specific employer'. A skilled worker (the main non-EEA labour-migration track) may therefore move to another employer without a new application, provided the job is of the same type and pay and conditions stay at the § 23(1)(b) level. The worker must notify the police within seven days of starting the new job (§ 6-11(2)). If the job ends, the worker may stay up to six months to look for work while the permit runs. A change to a different type of position requires a new permit before work starts. Other categories are more tightly bound: seasonal workers (§ 6-3), group permits and other employer-specific permits are tied to the stated employer, and the Immigration Act states that the job offer 'shall as a rule be full-time work for one employer' (§ 23(1)(d)). Holders of permanent residence, protection status and family permits get an unrestricted right to work under § 60(3)(b) of the Act.

### q99 · Right of spouse/partner of principal migrant to work.
- Law: Yes, unrestricted right to work
- Implementation: Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), § 60(3)(b), and the Immigration Regulations (Forskrift om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsforskriften), FOR-2009-10-15-1286), §§ 10-18 and 10-19. Section 60 sets the default content of every residence permit: 'unless otherwise specifically provided in or under the Act ... the residence permit gives the right to take work and to carry on business throughout the realm'. A spouse or cohabitant granted family immigration under chapter 6 of the Act (§§ 40-41) therefore gets a permit that itself carries an unrestricted right to work. No separate work authorisation and no sector or occupation limits apply, and the permit does not inherit the restrictions on the principal's permit. Section 10-18 confirms this for spouses of posted workers, contractors and self-employed persons: they 'may take work without the limitations set in the reference person's permit applying', meaning 'the right to work and carry on business throughout the realm'. Spouses of students and some researchers, whose permits come under § 9-6, 'may be granted the right to full-time work' even where the principal's own right to work is limited (§ 10-19). The right attaches once the family permit is granted, and in some cases depends on the outcome of a pending application. Family members of EEA nationals fall under the separate EEA regime.

### q101 · Right to maintain immigration status after loss of employment.
- Law: Loss of employment implies loss of immigration status unless a new job is found within a specified time period
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in a limited way, through the Immigration Regulations (Forskrift om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsforskriften), FOR-2009-10-15-1286), § 6-11, read with the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), § 63. Under § 6-11 first paragraph, a residence permit "may be revoked under general administrative-law rules if the employment ends, cf. section 63 of the Act, unless the conditions in the second paragraph are met". The second paragraph lets workers holding a skilled-worker permit under § 6-1 first paragraph "stay in the country to seek work for up to six months after the employment ends, provided the original permit does not expire". Two conditions apply. The worker must notify the police within seven days of losing the job, and again within seven days of starting a new one. UDI's guidance adds that a new job of the same type needs no new permit, while a different type of position requires a new permit before work starts. The job-search period covers only § 6-1 skilled workers. Other employment-based permits are tied to a specific job and employer: seasonal workers (§ 6-3), posted workers and service providers (§§ 6-13 to 6-17), and Russian unskilled workers and border commuters (§§ 6-4, 6-5). Those permits can be revoked when the job ends, with no search period. Migrants whose permit rests on another ground (family immigration, protection, humanitarian grounds, permanent residence under § 62) and EEA nationals under the separate EEA chapter do not lose their status when they lose a job. The provision therefore matches the MRD's middle option: status is kept for a fixed period during which a new job must be found.

### q103 · Right to equal employment conditions and protection.
- Law: Yes, migrants have a right to the same employment conditions and protections
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Working Environment Act (Lov om arbeidsmiljø, arbeidstid og stillingsvern mv. (arbeidsmiljøloven), LOV-2005-06-17-62), §§ 1-8 and 1-9, and the Equality and Anti-Discrimination Act (likestillings- og diskrimineringsloven, LOV-2017-06-16-51), § 29. The Working Environment Act defines an employee as 'anyone who performs work for and subordinate to another' (§ 1-8), with no condition of nationality or residence status. Its rules on health and safety, working hours and overtime, rest periods, written contracts, whistleblowing and protection against dismissal therefore apply to migrant workers exactly as to Norwegians. Under § 1-9 the Act 'cannot be derogated from by agreement to the detriment of the employee'. Posted workers are covered for core conditions under § 1-7. Section 29 of the Equality and Anti-Discrimination Act bans ethnic discrimination in 'all aspects of an employment relationship', including recruitment, training, 'pay and working conditions' and 'termination'. For non-EEA permit holders, § 23(1)(b) of the Immigration Act (utlendingsloven, LOV-2008-05-15-35) also requires 'pay and working conditions' no worse than the applicable collective agreement or local norm. There are no migrant-specific exclusions from labour protections. The differences that remain come from the immigration rules: a permit can be revoked when employment ends (Immigration Regulations § 6-11), and some permits are tied to a type of work.

### q109 · Right to equal access to social security protections.
- Law: Equal access to all types of social security protections after some period of time
- Implementation: Usually; Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the National Insurance Act (Lov om folketrygd (folketrygdloven), LOV-1997-02-28-19), §§ 2-1 and 2-2, read with the qualifying-period rules in §§ 12-2 and 20-10. Membership of the National Insurance scheme, which is the gateway to almost all social security benefits, does not depend on nationality. Under § 2-1, persons "resident in Norway are compulsory members". A person counts as resident when the stay "is intended to last or has lasted at least 12 months", from the date of entry, and "it is a condition for membership that the person has lawful residence in Norway". Under § 2-2, a person who is not a resident member is nevertheless a compulsory member if he or she is an employee in Norway, on condition that the person "has lawful access to take up gainful employment in Norway". A migrant worker with a valid work permit is therefore a member from the first day of work and is covered on the same terms as citizens, for example for sickness and parental benefits and work-injury cover. Several long-term benefits, however, require prior membership that newcomers must first build up. Disability benefit requires membership "for the last five years up to the date of disability" (§ 12-2). The guaranteed old-age pension requires "at least five years" of insurance periods and is paid in full only after 40 years (§ 20-10). These conditions apply equally to citizens, but citizens raised in Norway normally already meet them. Workers whose stay is meant to last under 12 months, such as seasonal workers with permits of up to six months (Immigration Regulations, Forskrift om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsforskriften), FOR-2009-10-15-1286, § 6-3), are members only as employees under § 2-2 and are not "resident", so they cannot get benefits that require residence. EEA/EFTA workers are covered by the EEA social security coordination rules (§§ 1-3 a and 2-1 a), which count insurance periods from other EEA states. Irregular workers are excluded by the lawful-residence and lawful-work conditions.

### q111 · Right of equal access to unemployment benefits.
- Law: Access to some, but not all types of unemployment benefits; OR not to the same extent as citizens; OR it depends on their visa type (e.g., temporary vs. permanent, high-skill vs. low skill)
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right only partially and depending on permit type, under the National Insurance Act (Lov om folketrygd (folketrygdloven), LOV-1997-02-28-19), chapter 4, §§ 4-2 to 4-5, read with the Immigration Regulations (Forskrift om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsforskriften), FOR-2009-10-15-1286), §§ 6-1, 6-3 and 6-11. The unemployment benefit (dagpenger) rules do not refer to nationality. An insured member (see §§ 2-1 and 2-2) must stay in Norway (§ 4-2) and have lost earned income as an employee (§ 4-3). The member must also have earned at least 1.5 times the basic amount in the last 12 months or 3 times in the last 36 months (§ 4-4), and must be a "genuine job seeker" willing "to take any work", "anywhere in Norway", full-time or part-time (§ 4-5). Immigration rules limit how far permit holders can meet these conditions. A skilled-worker permit "applies to a specific type of work", and some permits apply to "a specific job for a specific employer" (§ 6-1). If the job ends, the permit "may be revoked". The worker may stay "to seek work for up to six months" only if the permit does not expire and the police are notified within seven days (§ 6-11). Seasonal workers hold permits of at most six months tied to seasonal work (§ 6-3). Third-country permit holders therefore cannot claim the benefit for as long as citizens can, and their ability to qualify as job seekers depends on their permit. During the COVID-19 pandemic a temporary rule (former § 6-11 a, since repealed) let laid-off skilled workers stay and claim dagpenger, which shows the ordinary rules restrict access. EEA/EFTA workers, who need no permit, have equal access under the EEA coordination rules (§ 4-1 a), as do holders of permanent residence and refugees.

### q113 · Right of temporary migrants to become lawfully settled.
- Law: Yes, there is an opportunity to switch to permanent status after certain conditions are
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), § 62, and the Immigration Regulations (utlendingsforskriften, FOR-2009-10-15-1286), § 6-1 and chapter 11. Under § 62 first paragraph, a foreign national who "for the last three years has stayed in the realm with a temporary residence permit that forms a basis for a permanent residence permit" is entitled, on application, to permanent residence. Six conditions apply: (a) no more than seven months in total spent outside Norway during those three years; (b) the conditions for the qualifying temporary permit are still met; (c) no grounds for expulsion and no additional waiting time still running; (d) a passed oral Norwegian test; (e) a passed civics test in a language the applicant understands; and (f) self-sufficiency for the last twelve months. Regulation § 6-1 provides that the skilled-worker permit (the main track for third-country workers) "forms a basis for a permanent residence permit". A skilled worker who keeps qualifying employment and renews the permit can therefore become a permanent resident after three years. Permanent residence gives the right to stay without time limit and stronger protection against expulsion (§ 62 and § 68). It lapses after more than two years of continuous absence. EEA/EFTA workers follow a separate route to permanent right of residence under the EEA chapter of the Act. The path is conditional and not open to every temporary worker: seasonal workers under regulation § 6-3 do not qualify (see Q60). It does exist for the core category of labour migrants.

### q115 · Right of temporary migrants to settle regardless of initial status.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Norway. Under the Immigration Act (utlendingsloven, LOV-2008-05-15-35), § 62, only time spent "with a temporary residence permit that forms a basis for a permanent residence permit" counts toward permanent residence. The Immigration Regulations (utlendingsforskriften, FOR-2009-10-15-1286) then decide, permit by permit, which labour-related permits qualify. The skilled-worker permit in § 6-1 "forms a basis for a permanent residence permit". The seasonal-worker permit in § 6-3, by contrast, "applies to a specific job for one or more specific employers, and it does not form a basis for a permanent residence permit". The Act also lets the authorities state in a permit that it will not lead to permanent residence (§ 60). Whether a temporary migrant worker can settle therefore depends directly on the type of permit held. Skilled workers (and researchers and graduates who switch into the skilled-worker track) can reach permanent residence after three years if they meet the language, civics, self-sufficiency and absence conditions. Seasonal workers, whatever the length or number of their stays, cannot count that time. Several other job-tied or purpose-limited permits in chapter 6 (for example working-holiday and au pair permits) are likewise outside the route. Since the route to settlement is conditional on visa type, the answer is No.

### q117 · Right to redress following employer breach of employment contract.
- Law: Yes, same as citizens
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Working Environment Act (Lov om arbeidsmiljø, arbeidstid og stillingsvern mv. (arbeidsmiljøloven), LOV-2005-06-17-62), §§ 1-2, 1-8, 1-9 and chapter 17, read with the Dispute Act (tvisteloven) and the Legal Aid Act (Lov om fri rettshjelp (rettshjelpsloven), LOV-1980-06-13-35), § 11. The Act "applies to undertakings that employ employees" (§ 1-2). An employee is "anyone who performs work for and subordinate to another" (§ 1-8). Neither definition depends on nationality or residence status. The Act "cannot be derogated from by agreement to the employee's detriment" (§ 1-9). Under § 17-1, lawsuits about rights and duties under the Act go to the ordinary courts under the Courts Act and the Dispute Act, and the court may also hear "claims for settlement of wages and holiday pay". Employees may demand negotiations with the employer over unlawful dismissal, temporary hiring or suspension (§ 17-3). Certain disputes go first to the Dispute Resolution Board (§ 17-2). The Labour Inspection Authority supervises compliance under chapter 18. Means-tested free legal advice is available "for an employee in a case under the Working Environment Act on whether an employment relationship exists or on compensation in connection with its termination" (Legal Aid Act § 11, second paragraph, no. 5), on the same terms for foreign and Norwegian workers. Posted workers are also covered for the conditions set under § 1-7. No provision limits migrant workers' standing. The main gap applies to citizens as well: there is no general legal aid for ordinary unpaid-wage claims.

### q135 · Right to be free from discrimination on the basis of race or ethnicity.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Equality and Anti-Discrimination Act (Lov om likestilling og forbud mot diskriminering (likestillings- og diskrimineringsloven), LOV-2017-06-16-51), §§ 1, 2, 5, 6-9 and 29. Section 6 provides that 'discrimination on grounds of ... ethnicity, religion, belief ... is prohibited', and that 'ethnicity means, among other things, national origin, descent, skin colour and language'. The ban covers existing, assumed, past or future characteristics, and discrimination by association. It covers both direct and indirect differential treatment (§§ 7-8). In employment, direct differential treatment on grounds of ethnicity is lawful only where the characteristic 'is decisive for the performance of the work or occupation' and the treatment is proportionate (§ 9(2)). The Act 'applies in all areas of society' (§ 2) and covers 'all aspects of an employment relationship', from advertising and hiring to pay and dismissal (§ 29). Employers must not ask applicants about their ethnicity (§ 30). Every employer must work actively against ethnic discrimination, and larger employers must assess risks and report (§ 26). Section 5 makes the UN Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Racial Discrimination part of Norwegian law. The protection covers everyone in Norway, migrants generally, whatever their status. The Equality and Anti-Discrimination Ombud and the Anti-Discrimination Tribunal enforce it (§ 35). Citizenship as such is not a listed ground, so differential treatment based on nationality is tested as possible indirect ethnic discrimination. Article 98 of the Constitution and ECHR art. 14, incorporated by the Human Rights Act, add general equality guarantees.


## Legal personhood

### q27 · Right not to have identity documents confiscated.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Penal Code (Lov om straff (straffeloven), LOV-2005-05-20-28), §§ 361 and 363, together with the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), § 104. Penal Code § 361 defines a "document" as "an information carrier that concerns a legal relationship or is otherwise suitable as evidence of a legal relationship". That covers passports, residence cards and other identity papers. Under § 363 anyone who "without authorisation destroys or removes [unndrar] a document or part of it" faces a fine or up to two years' imprisonment. An employer, recruiter or landlord who takes and keeps a worker's passport or residence card therefore commits an offence. On the state side, Immigration Act § 104 lets the authorities seize "travel documents ... or other material that can help clarify or document identity" only on stated grounds: doubt about identity, or concrete grounds to believe the person will evade a return decision. Criminal Procedure Act rules on carrying out seizures and returning seized items apply. Retaining documents is also treated as a means of control under the trafficking and exploitation offences, Penal Code § 257 and Immigration Act § 108 a ("undue exploitation" of foreigners with temporary permits, asylum seekers or people without permits). The protection covers all migrants, whatever their status. It is general criminal law rather than a provision written for migrant workers.

### q29 · Right to birth registration.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the Population Register Act (Lov om folkeregistrering (folkeregisterloven), LOV-2016-12-09-88), §§ 2-1, 2-2, 6-4 and 7-1. Section 2-1 says the Population Register registers "all persons who: a. are or have been resident in Norway, b. are born in Norway or c. have been assigned a national identity number or D-number". Under § 2-2, "on first registration in the Population Register, a person who is resident or born in Norway is assigned a national identity number [fødselsnummer]". Birth in Norway is therefore enough for registration and a national identity number. The parents' nationality, residence status or lawful stay plays no part. Births are reported in two ways. Section 7-1 obliges public authorities and bodies, including hospitals and health personnel, to give "the notifications and information necessary for the register", notwithstanding any duty of confidentiality. Where no doctor or midwife was present, § 6-4 obliges the mother herself to report the birth to the tax office. The rule therefore covers children of regular migrants, asylum seekers, refugees and irregular migrants alike. It is backed by the right of every child to be registered immediately after birth, which applies in Norwegian law through the Human Rights Act (CRC art. 7, ICCPR art. 24(2)).

### q31 · Right to government document recognizing residency, work status, and identity.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), §§ 64 and 64 a, with the EEA chapter (§§ 117-119) and the Population Register Act (folkeregisterloven, LOV-2016-12-09-88), § 2-2. Under § 64 a, "a foreigner who is granted a temporary or permanent residence permit shall have the permit documented by the issue of a Schengen-standardised residence card ... with electronically stored biometrics". The card records identity and residence status. It also shows the right to work, which § 60 attaches to permits unless they say otherwise. Under § 64, a recognised refugee "shall upon application also be issued a refugee travel document", and other protection holders are given a foreigner's passport where needed. Chapter 13 of the Act provides registration certificates, residence documents and residence cards for EEA nationals and their family members (§§ 117-119). Under Population Register Act § 2-2, people who are not resident may be given a D-number, which serves as their identity number. There are gaps. The card requirement in § 64 a does not cover a permit granted while an application is being processed (§ 57). Asylum seekers and irregular migrants are outside this mandate and rely on other arrangements.

### q33 · Right against destruction of document recognizing residency, work status, and identity.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Penal Code (Lov om straff (straffeloven), LOV-2005-05-20-28), § 363, read with the definition in § 361. Section 363 provides that anyone "who without authorisation destroys or removes [unndrar] a document or part of it" is punished by a fine or imprisonment for up to two years. Section 361 defines a document as "an information carrier that concerns a legal relationship or is otherwise suitable as evidence of a legal relationship". That definition covers residence cards issued under Immigration Act § 64 a, refugee travel documents and foreigner's passports under § 64, EEA registration certificates, national passports and other identity documents. The offence applies to anyone, whether a private person, an employer or an official, and protects every document holder whatever their nationality or status. Other offences add protection. Penal Code §§ 351-352 punish criminal damage to property. Section 366 punishes misuse of another person's identity document, and Immigration Act § 108 punishes handing over passports, travel documents or residence cards for unlawful entry. Authorities may lawfully seize documents only under the specific powers in Immigration Act § 104, so destruction or removal outside those powers is "without authorisation" within § 363.


## Liberty

### q15 · Right to communicate with consular officials.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Prosecution Instructions (Påtaleinstruksen, FOR-1985-06-28-1679), § 9-10, and the Regulation on the Use of Police Custody (Forskrift om bruk av politiarrest, FOR-2006-06-30-749), §§ 2-1 and 2-4. Under § 9-10, when a foreign national is arrested or remanded in custody, "he shall be informed that he has the right to ask that the relevant consular post be notified". When the person asks, "the relevant consular post shall be notified immediately" of the arrest or detention. Under § 2-4 of the police custody regulation, notice to the "consular post (embassy)" is given in line with the rules in force, any special consular agreement and the Director of Public Prosecutions' guidelines, with an interpreter where needed. § 2-1 requires talks with a "consular representative" to take place in rooms without surveillance equipment. These duties apply to every foreign national held in police custody, whatever their residence status. For people held in immigration detention under the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), the situation is different. § 106 a requires only that the detainee's household or a person they name be notified. § 107, third paragraph, gives detainees at the Police Immigration Detention Centre a general right to receive visits, make phone calls and send and receive post. The police's internal instruction for the centre (§ 10.3) states that detainees may at any time receive visits from an "official representative from the foreigner's home country", but that instruction is administrative and has no force of law. Norway is party to the Vienna Convention on Consular Relations. Because Norway is dualist and has not incorporated the Convention, the domestic obligation rests on the regulations above.

### q17 · Right of child migrants to be free from detention on the basis of migrant status.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Norway. The Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35) expressly allows children to be arrested and detained on immigration grounds. § 106 c ("Special rules on arrest and detention of minors") provides that a minor "may only be arrested if it is particularly required". A minor may be detained for identity control "in extraordinary situations where the measure is strictly necessary as a last resort", or to carry out removal or a transfer procedure where "absolutely decisive as a last resort". The best interests of the child must always be a fundamental consideration, including when deciding whether alternatives under § 105 (reporting duty, assigned residence) can be used instead. The child is a party to the case, and the police must notify the child welfare service. A child able to form views must be heard before the court decides. Detention may last up to 3 days (72 hours) at a time. The total may go beyond 3 days only for "special and strong reasons", and must then "not exceed 9 days". Some grounds are closed to children: § 106, first paragraph, letters h, i and j (inadmissible or manifestly unfounded asylum claims, refusal of entry) do not apply to minors or to parents with minor children who have also applied. These are strict limits on child detention, not a prohibition. Children, including families with children and unaccompanied minors, can therefore still be detained because of their migration status, at the family unit or at the Police Immigration Detention Centre.

### q19 · Right of migrants to be free from detention on the basis of migrant status.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Norway. The Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35) sets out in § 106 ("Conditions for arrest and detention") a list of grounds on which "a foreigner may be arrested and detained" because of their migration situation. The grounds include: the foreigner does not cooperate in establishing their identity, or there are concrete grounds to suspect a false identity (letter a); concrete grounds to believe they will evade a decision imposing a duty to leave, including while such a case is pending (letter b); evading a transfer procedure under Regulation (EU) 2024/1351 (letter c); breach of a reporting duty or assigned residence (letter d); expulsion following a criminal sentence where there is a risk of reoffending (letter e); failure to obtain a travel document, in order to present the person to their country's mission (letter f); transit at a Norwegian airport (letter g); an asylum claim likely to be found inadmissible, or manifestly unfounded and handled within 48 hours (letters h-i); and refusal of entry or expulsion (letter j). There are safeguards. Coercive measures must be necessary and proportionate (§ 99). Detention may not be ordered where a reporting duty or assigned residence under § 105 would be enough. Detention requires a court order with time limits (§§ 106 a-106 b), and detainees are generally held at the Police Immigration Detention Centre rather than in prison (§ 107). These provisions regulate detention based on migration status and do not prohibit it.

### q21 · Right to appeal legality, conditions, and length of detention
- Law: Yes, two of the three (legality, conditions, length)
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in relation to legality and length in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), §§ 106 a, 106 b and 99 a, and in the Constitution of the Kingdom of Norway (Kongeriket Norges Grunnlov), § 94. Under § 94 of the Constitution, an arrested person "shall be brought before a court as soon as possible", and others deprived of liberty "may have the deprivation of liberty reviewed by the courts without undue delay". Under Immigration Act § 106 a, if the police want to keep a detained foreign national, he or she must be brought before the district court "as soon as possible and at the latest on the third day after the arrest". Under § 106 b, the court decides by ruling (kjennelse) whether detention is lawful. The ruling must state the legal basis and the grounds and show that detention "is not a disproportionate measure". The court also sets the length: a fixed time limit that "shall be as short as possible and must not exceed four weeks". Each extension needs a new court ruling. Total detention may not exceed 12 weeks without special reasons, or 18 months unless the person has been expelled following a criminal sentence. A detention order can be reversed at any time. Release must follow once the ground for detention lapses. Under § 99 a, rulings on coercive measures can be appealed under chapter 26 of the Criminal Procedure Act. Conditions of detention at the Police Immigration Detention Centre (Trandum) are not part of this court review. § 107 lets the police use force, place a detainee in a security cell or exclude them from the community. These measures "cannot be used when [they] would be a disproportionate measure", but the Act gives no court remedy against them. Challenges to conditions go through administrative complaint, the Supervisory Council (§ 107 a), the Parliamentary Ombud or an ordinary civil lawsuit. The rules apply to all detained foreign nationals, with stricter rules for minors (§ 106 c).

### q23 · Right to an individualized detention decision.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), §§ 99, 106, 106 b and 106 c, and in the Constitution of the Kingdom of Norway (Kongeriket Norges Grunnlov), § 94. The Constitution § 94 requires that any deprivation of liberty be lawful, "necessary and not ... a disproportionate interference". Under § 99 of the Act, a coercive measure may be used only "when there is sufficient reason". It may not be used when, given the nature of the case and other circumstances, it would be a disproportionate interference. Most detention grounds in § 106 require "concrete indications" relating to the individual, for example that he or she will abscond from enforcement of a return decision or is giving a false identity. Detention may not be ordered if a duty to report or an order to stay in a specific place (§ 105) would be enough. Detention for identification may be ordered only when steps are being taken to establish identity, and detention for removal only when steps are being taken towards removal. Each detention is decided by the district court in a reasoned ruling (§ 106 b). The ruling must state the legal basis and the grounds and show that detention is not disproportionate. The time limit must be as short as possible. For children (§ 106 c), detention must be an absolute last resort and the best interests of the child a fundamental consideration. The ruling must explain how the child's best interests and alternatives were assessed. These rules apply to all foreign nationals, including asylum seekers and irregular migrants. Some 2026 grounds tied to the EU Pact (for example manifestly unfounded claims handled within 48 hours) are framed more categorically, but they remain subject to the necessity, alternatives and proportionality tests.

### q25 · Right to be free from torture and inhumane treatment.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Constitution of the Kingdom of Norway (Kongeriket Norges Grunnlov), §§ 92 and 93. Section 93 second paragraph provides that "No one may be subjected to torture or other inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment." Under the fourth paragraph, the state authorities "shall ... combat torture, slavery, forced labour and other forms of inhuman or degrading treatment". Section 92 obliges the authorities to "respect and ensure human rights" as set out in the Constitution and in binding human-rights treaties. The protection is phrased as applying to "no one" (ingen) and "every human being". It therefore covers all migrants, whatever their status: regular and irregular migrants, asylum seekers, and people held at the Police Immigration Detention Centre at Trandum. The Immigration Act (utlendingsloven, LOV-2008-05-15-35) applies the same standard inside immigration detention. Under § 107, force, security cells and exclusion from the community may be used only when "strictly necessary", only after less intrusive measures have failed, and "cannot be used when [this] would be a disproportionate measure". A doctor's opinion must be sought where possible, and the police must keep assessing whether the measure is still needed. An independent Supervisory Council oversees forced returns and the detention centre (§ 107 a). The Parliamentary Ombud visits places of detention as the National Preventive Mechanism under OPCAT. The ban is absolute, and it covers cruel, inhuman and degrading treatment as well as torture.


## Life

### q9 · Right to be free from use of force to prevent entry to territory.
- Law: No
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Norway. No Norwegian statute prohibits officers from using force to stop migrants entering the country. The Police Act (Lov om politiet (politiloven), LOV-1995-08-04-53) § 6 is the general rule for all police work. It requires officers to try milder means first: the police "shall not use stronger means unless weaker means must be assumed to be insufficient or inappropriate, or have been tried in vain". The means used must be "necessary and proportionate". The same section then authorises force: "The police may use force in the performance of their duties to the extent necessary and justifiable." The Border Control Act (Lov om grensetilsyn og grensekontroll av personer (grenseloven), LOV-2018-04-20-8) gives the police responsibility for border surveillance and entry and exit checks (§ 4). Under § 6, the Armed Forces may carry out border surveillance on the Norwegian-Russian border on behalf of the police, and "may use force within the framework of Police Act § 6". Section 12 lets the King prohibit or restrict crossing the border in extraordinary situations. The Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35) sets up refusal of entry (bortvisning, ch. 2) and coercive measures (§ 99 onward). Section 99 allows coercive measures when there is "sufficient reason" and they are not disproportionate. Taken together, these rules limit force through necessity and proportionality, but they do not forbid it as a way of stopping irregular entry. Force that is necessary and proportionate to prevent unlawful border crossing or to enforce a refusal of entry is lawful. This is the same position as the Swedish and Finnish codings.

### q11 · Right to be free from use of lethal force to prevent entry to territory.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the Police Act (Lov om politiet (politiloven), LOV-1995-08-04-53) §§ 6 and 6 b, and in the Border Control Act (Lov om grensetilsyn og grensekontroll av personer (grenseloven), LOV-2018-04-20-8) § 6. Section 6 b was added in 2020. It says firearms "may only be used when absolutely necessary and where milder means have been tried in vain or clearly will not succeed". It also limits their use to two situations. The first is where the officer or others are threatened with or exposed to serious violence or another serious violation of integrity, and use of a weapon is necessary to prevent loss of life or serious injury. The second is where it is required to arrest or stop someone convicted of, or strongly suspected of, murder or other serious violence, or someone otherwise especially dangerous to life or health, to Norway's independence or to fundamental national interests. Irregular entry or an attempt to enter without permission falls into neither category, so lethal force cannot lawfully be used to stop migrants entering. Section 6 adds the general requirements of necessity, proportionality and trying milder means first. Under Border Control Act § 6, military personnel guarding the Norwegian-Russian border on behalf of the police may use force only "within the framework of Police Act § 6", so the same limits apply to them. The protection covers all migrants, whatever their status, because it limits what officers may do rather than conferring a status-based right.

### q13 · Right to be protected from trafficking or smuggling.
- Law: No, government may punish trafficked or smuggled migrants
- Implementation: Sometimes

No protection of this right in Norway. Norwegian law has no statutory provision exempting trafficked or smuggled migrants from prosecution. The Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35) § 108 makes it an offence, punishable by a fine or up to six months' imprisonment, to breach the travel-document rule (§ 8), the visa rule (§ 9) and the residence-permit requirement (§ 55). It also covers giving materially false information in an immigration case. Entering in breach of an entry ban is punishable too (§ 108 third paragraph (e)). Smuggled migrants therefore remain liable for irregular entry and stay. The only exemptions in § 108 protect third parties. Helping a person covered by § 28 (protection) to reach the first safe country is not punishable, and humanitarian help to irregular migrants is protected. Neither exemption covers the migrant. For trafficking victims, the only route is discretion. Under the Criminal Procedure Act (Lov om rettergangsmåten i straffesaker (straffeprosessloven), LOV-1981-05-22-25) § 69, the prosecution "when special reasons so indicate" may decline to prosecute even though guilt is proven. According to GRETA, the Penal Code lets courts refrain from imposing a sentence, and guidelines from the Director of Public Prosecutions direct non-punishment in line with Article 26 of the Council of Europe Anti-Trafficking Convention. This is a prosecutorial and judicial discretion, not a legal exemption, and the person must still be identified as a victim. GRETA's Committee of the Parties (2026) has asked Norway to adopt a specific non-punishment provision.


## Nationality

### q63 · Right to maintain citizenship status after convictions.
- Law: Only major convictions (e.g., criminal offenses) can lead to loss of citizenship status
- Implementation: Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the Norwegian Nationality Act (Lov om norsk statsborgerskap (statsborgerloven), LOV-2005-06-10-51), §§ 26, 26 a and 26 b. Ordinary criminal convictions do not affect the citizenship of a naturalised person once it has been granted. A criminal record matters only before naturalisation: under § 9 an applicant who has been sentenced must wait out a qualifying period (karenstid). The only conviction-based ground for loss is § 26 a. Under it, a person who holds Norwegian and another citizenship and "has shown conduct seriously harmful to Norway's vital interests" can lose Norwegian citizenship "by judgment" if punished under Penal Code chapters 16, 17 or 18 for an offence carrying six years' imprisonment or more. These chapters cover crimes against the state's independence and security, against the constitutional order, and terrorism. Loss is barred for acts committed before age 18, and it is barred where it would be "a disproportionate interference" given the seriousness of the act and the person's ties to Norway. In cases involving children, the child's best interests are a fundamental consideration. Section 26 b, added in 2021, lets the Ministry take away citizenship from a dual national whose conduct indicates they will seriously harm fundamental national interests. This is an administrative security measure that requires no conviction, and the same age and proportionality limits apply. Section 26 allows revocation for knowingly false information in the naturalisation process, which falls outside this indicator. Neither § 26 a nor § 26 b can make a person stateless, because both apply only to dual nationals. They apply to Norwegian-born dual nationals as well as to naturalised citizens.

### q65 · Right of economic migrants to citizenship.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually; Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the Norwegian Nationality Act (Lov om norsk statsborgerskap (statsborgerloven), LOV-2005-06-10-51), §§ 7, 10 and 14, together with the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), §§ 60 and 62. Work permits under Immigration Act chapter 3 are temporary permits that, by default, "form the basis for a permanent residence permit" (§ 60). Under § 62, a foreign national who has held such permits for the last three years is entitled to permanent residence. The conditions are limited absences, passing an oral Norwegian test and a social studies test, self-sufficiency in the last twelve months, and no grounds for expulsion. Under Nationality Act § 7, "everyone" has a right to citizenship on application if they have clarified their identity, are at least 12, live in Norway and intend to stay, meet the conditions for permanent residence under § 62, and have "a total of eight years' residence in the realm during the last eleven years" on permits of at least one year. They must also meet the Norwegian-language and citizenship-test requirements (§ 8) and have no outstanding penalty or qualifying period (§ 9). Section 10, in force since 2022, cuts the residence requirement to six years in the last ten for applicants who had sufficient income in the last assessed tax year, which benefits economic migrants in particular. EEA/EFTA workers qualify under § 14 after three years' residence on their EEA right of residence, without needing permanent residence. Some short-term permits, such as seasonal work, do not lead to permanent residence. Citizenship is also refused where fundamental national interests or foreign-policy considerations weigh against it (§ 7, second paragraph). Dual citizenship has been allowed since 2020.

### q67 · Right of humanitarian migrants to citizenship.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Norwegian Nationality Act (Lov om norsk statsborgerskap (statsborgerloven), LOV-2005-06-10-51), §§ 7, 16 and 16 a, together with the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), §§ 34 and 62. Persons granted protection under Immigration Act § 28 receive temporary permits that lead to permanent residence after three years under § 62. Section 58 exempts refugees from the income requirement for the permit itself. Naturalisation then follows under Nationality Act § 7. Section 16 a gives recognised refugees a shorter route. For applicants "granted a residence permit as a refugee under Immigration Act § 28", the eight-year rule is replaced by "seven years' residence in the realm during the last ten years". Stateless persons need only three years' residence under § 16, and the minimum age does not apply to them. Persons with permits on strong humanitarian grounds (§ 38) follow the general rules, provided their permit forms the basis for permanent residence. People under collective protection, which covers those displaced from Ukraine, face a longer wait. Under § 34 their permit "does not form the basis for a permanent residence permit" for up to five years. After that they can receive a permit that does lead to permanent residence, and permanent residence must be granted after three more years if the conditions are met. All applicants must have clarified their identity (§ 7(1)(a)). They must also meet the Norwegian-language and citizenship-test requirements of § 8, unless exempted, and have no outstanding penalty or qualifying period under § 9.

### q69 · Right of family migrants to citizenship.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually; Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the Norwegian Nationality Act (Lov om norsk statsborgerskap (statsborgerloven), LOV-2005-06-10-51), §§ 7, 11, 12 and 17, together with the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), chapter 6 and §§ 60 and 62. Family-immigration permits under chapter 6 are temporary permits that, unless otherwise provided, form the basis for permanent residence (§ 60). Permanent residence follows after three years under § 62, subject to the language, social-studies and self-sufficiency conditions. Naturalisation then follows under Nationality Act § 7 after eight years' residence in the last eleven. Family migrants married to, or registered partners or cohabitants of, Norwegian citizens have a shorter route under § 12. For an applicant "married to a Norwegian citizen and sharing a residence with the spouse", the eight-year rule is replaced by "at least five years' residence in the realm during the last ten years". Residence time and time married with a shared home must add up to at least seven years. Under § 11, children who arrived before 18 need five years' residence in the last seven. Under § 17, children under 18 of a person who is acquiring or has acquired citizenship are entitled to citizenship alongside the parent, subject only to identity, residence and criminal-record conditions. All adult applicants must have clarified their identity. They must also meet the Norwegian-language and citizenship-test requirements (§ 8) and have no outstanding penalty or qualifying period (§ 9).

### q71 · Right to dual nationality.
- Law: Yes, all migrants can obtain dual nationality by retaining their nationality of origin
- Implementation: Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the Norwegian Nationality Act (Lov om norsk statsborgerskap (statsborgerloven), LOV-2005-06-10-51), as amended by Act of 20 December 2018 No. 121, in force 1 January 2020. Before that date, applicants for naturalisation had to renounce their existing nationality, and a Norwegian who took another nationality lost Norwegian citizenship automatically. The 2018 amendment repealed both rules: § 23 (loss of Norwegian citizenship on acquiring another) is marked "Opphevet ved lov 20 des 2018 nr. 121 (ikr. 1 jan 2020)". The main naturalisation rule in § 7(1) now says that "everyone has, on application, the right to Norwegian citizenship" if they meet the listed conditions: clarified identity, age, residence, eligibility for permanent residence under Immigration Act § 62, eight years' residence within eleven, oral Norwegian and the citizenship test (§ 8), and a clean criminal record (§ 9). None of these conditions asks the applicant to give up a nationality of origin. Lovdata's official summary of the Act states that since 2020 "it is lawful to obtain Norwegian citizenship and at the same time keep one's original citizenship". The rule covers every naturalising migrant, whatever their country of origin, as well as Nordic nationals acquiring citizenship by notification (§ 20). Former Norwegians who lost citizenship under the old rule can reacquire it by notification (§ 22 a). Whether a migrant actually keeps the nationality of origin depends on that country's own law, not on Norwegian law. Loss for long absence (§ 24) applies only to those who acquired Norwegian citizenship at birth.

### q133 · Right of irregular migrants to regularize their status.
- Law: No, there is no opportunity for irregular migrant workers to regularize
- Implementation: Does Not Apply

No protection of this right in Norway. The Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35) § 56(1) provides that "a first-time residence permit shall be granted before entry into the realm". The exception in § 56(3) covers only individual cases "when strong reasons of fairness so indicate". The Immigration Regulations (utlendingsforskriften, FOR-2009-10-15-1286) § 10-1(3) list the cases in which an application may be lodged from inside Norway, but make it a condition "that the applicant has lawful stay". Persons staying in connection with an asylum claim or awaiting departure after refusal are excluded. Applications that fail these conditions are refused on that ground (§ 10-1(5)). Work-based permits under §§ 23-25 are therefore not open to workers who are already irregular. The closest provisions are case-by-case humanitarian measures, not a regularisation route for workers. § 38 allows a permit "even if the other conditions of the Act are not met" where there are "strong humanitarian considerations or particular ties to the realm". Factors include victims of trafficking. Immigration-control considerations may count against the grant, including "possible consequences for the scale of applications on a similar basis" and "respect for the Act's other rules". Regulations § 8-4 grant a permit to trafficking victims who testify in a prosecution under Penal Code § 257. Labour exploitation that falls short of trafficking, or irregular employment as such, gives no entitlement.


## Opinion and expression

### q83 · Right to freedom to hold opinion.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the Constitution of the Kingdom of Norway (Kongeriket Norges Grunnlov, LOV-1814-05-17), § 100, and in the Human Rights Act (Lov om styrking av menneskerettighetenes stilling i norsk rett (menneskerettsloven), LOV-1999-05-21-30), §§ 2-3. Section 100 of the Constitution says no one may be held legally liable for having imparted or received information, ideas or messages unless this can be justified against the grounds for freedom of expression, which include "the individual's free formation of opinion" (individets frie meningsdannelse). The same article bans prior censorship and other preventive measures. It gives "everyone" (enhver) the right to speak frankly on the government of the state and any other subject. Through § 2 of the Human Rights Act, ICCPR Article 19(1) ("Everyone shall have the right to hold opinions without interference") and ECHR Article 10 (freedom "to hold opinions") apply as Norwegian law. Under § 3, they take precedence over conflicting legislation. ICCPR Article 19(1) allows no restriction on holding opinions, and nothing in the Constitution or the Human Rights Act ties the right to citizenship or residence status. Migrants of every category, including asylum seekers and people without lawful residence, therefore hold this freedom on the same terms as citizens. Section 6 of the Equality and Anti-Discrimination Act (LOV-2017-06-16-51) also bans discrimination on grounds of belief (livssyn).

### q85 · Right to freedom of expression.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually; Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the Constitution of the Kingdom of Norway (Kongeriket Norges Grunnlov, LOV-1814-05-17), § 100, and in the Human Rights Act (Lov om styrking av menneskerettighetenes stilling i norsk rett (menneskerettsloven), LOV-1999-05-21-30), §§ 2-3. Section 100 opens: "There shall be freedom of expression." It provides that "frank speech on the government of the state and on any other subject is permitted for everyone" (enhver). Limits may be set only where "particularly weighty considerations" justify them against the grounds for free expression. Legal liability should be prescribed by law. Prior censorship is prohibited except to protect children from harmful moving images. Section 100 also gives "everyone" a right of access to state and municipal documents and to follow court hearings and elected bodies. The state must "create conditions that facilitate open and enlightened public discourse". Through § 2 of the Human Rights Act, ECHR Article 10 and ICCPR Article 19 apply as Norwegian law, and under § 3 they prevail over conflicting statutes. Both guarantee the freedom to "everyone", whatever their nationality or residence status. The permitted restrictions, such as the Penal Code's hate-speech and incitement offences, apply equally to citizens and non-citizens. Migrants, including asylum seekers and people without lawful residence, therefore enjoy freedom of expression on the same terms as citizens. ECHR Article 16 (restrictions on the political activity of aliens) is part of the incorporated text, but no Norwegian statute invokes it.


## Peaceful assembly and association

### q87 · Right to freedom of association.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the Constitution of the Kingdom of Norway (Kongeriket Norges Grunnlov, LOV-1814-05-17), § 101 first paragraph, and in the Human Rights Act (Lov om styrking av menneskerettighetenes stilling i norsk rett (menneskerettsloven), LOV-1999-05-21-30), §§ 2-3. Section 101 provides that "everyone has the right to form, join and leave associations, including trade unions and political parties". The right is held by "everyone" (enhver), not by citizens alone, and it expressly includes trade unions. Through § 2 of the Human Rights Act, ECHR Article 11, ICCPR Article 22 and ICESCR Article 8 (the right to form and join trade unions) apply as Norwegian law. Under § 3, they prevail over conflicting statutes. Constitution § 92 obliges the state to respect and ensure these rights. None of the sources read contain a nationality or residence condition on membership of associations, trade unions or political parties, unlike Finland's Associations Act. Migrant workers, including EEA workers, posted workers, temporary permit holders and refugees, may therefore join trade unions and other associations on the same terms as Norwegian citizens. Section 6 of the Equality and Anti-Discrimination Act (LOV-2017-06-16-51) also prohibits discrimination on grounds of ethnicity and national origin, including in working life.

### q89 · Right to freedom of assembly.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the Constitution of the Kingdom of Norway (Kongeriket Norges Grunnlov, LOV-1814-05-17), § 101 second paragraph, and in the Human Rights Act (Lov om styrking av menneskerettighetenes stilling i norsk rett (menneskerettsloven), LOV-1999-05-21-30), §§ 2-3. Section 101 provides that "everyone may meet in peaceful assemblies and demonstrations" (Alle kan møtes i fredelige forsamlinger og demonstrasjoner). The text uses "everyone" (alle) and sets no condition of citizenship or lawful residence. Through § 2 of the Human Rights Act, ECHR Article 11 and ICCPR Article 21 apply as Norwegian law, and under § 3 they prevail over conflicting statutes. Restrictions must be prescribed by law and necessary in a democratic society for such aims as public safety and the prevention of disorder. Police rules on public order apply equally to citizens and non-citizens. Section 92 obliges the state to respect and ensure the right. Migrants of every status, including asylum seekers and irregular migrants, may therefore organise and take part in peaceful assemblies on the same terms as citizens.


## Thought, conscience, and religion or belief

### q81 · Right of freedom of thought, conscience and religion or belief.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually; Effectively Always

National law in Norway protects this right in the Constitution of the Kingdom of Norway (Kongeriket Norges Grunnlov, LOV-1814-05-17), § 16, and in the Human Rights Act (Lov om styrking av menneskerettighetenes stilling i norsk rett (menneskerettsloven), LOV-1999-05-21-30), §§ 2-3. Section 16 of the Constitution provides that "all inhabitants of the realm have free exercise of religion" and that "all religious and belief communities shall be supported on an equal basis". The protection rests on residence in the realm ("innbyggere"), not on citizenship. Section 92 obliges the state to "respect and ensure human rights" as set out in the Constitution and in binding treaties. Under § 2 of the Human Rights Act, the European Convention on Human Rights (Article 9, freedom of thought, conscience and religion) and the ICCPR (Article 18) apply as Norwegian law. Under § 3, they "take precedence" over conflicting statutes. Both treaties guarantee the right to "everyone" within the jurisdiction, so migrants are covered whatever their status: workers, students, refugees, asylum seekers and people without lawful residence. Limits are only those the treaties permit (prescribed by law and necessary for public safety, order, health, morals or the rights of others), and they apply to citizens and non-citizens alike. Section 6 of the Equality and Anti-Discrimination Act (likestillings- og diskrimineringsloven, LOV-2017-06-16-51) also prohibits discrimination on grounds of religion and belief. The Constitution keeps the Church of Norway as the "people's church" (folkekirke) supported by the state, but that status does not depend on nationality.


## Victims of crime

### q43 · Right of victims of crime to access criminal proceedings.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Criminal Procedure Act (straffeprosessloven, LOV-1981-05-22-25), chapter 8a (§§ 93a-93g) and chapter 9a, read with the Constitution (Grunnloven) § 98. Chapter 8a gives rights to every aggrieved party (fornærmede) and to bereaved relatives, without any condition of nationality or residence. Under § 93c, aggrieved parties and bereaved relatives "have the right to be present at all court hearings", including hearings behind closed doors. Under § 93b, victims with victim's counsel are notified of hearings during the investigation, and a victim who brings a civil claim under § 428 is summoned to the main hearing as a party. Section 93a ties these rights to the appointment of victim's counsel (bistandsadvokat) under § 107a, which is available, among others, to victims of trafficking, sexual offences and domestic violence. The Constitution § 98 provides that "all are equal before the law". The Interpreters Act (tolkeloven, LOV-2021-06-11-79) § 6 requires the police and prosecuting authority to use an interpreter where needed for legal certainty. Migrant victims can therefore report crimes, take part in proceedings and claim compensation on the same terms as citizens. State compensation under the Violent Crime Compensation Act 2022 also covers trafficking. The procedural codes make no distinction by status. However, police have no formal "firewall": reporting a crime does not shield an irregular migrant from immigration enforcement. This can deter reporting, although the legal right is the same.

### q45 · Right of victims of crime to immigration status or relief from expulsion.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Sometimes; Usually

National law in Norway protects this right through three avenues. The first two are in the Immigration Regulations (utlendingsforskriften, FOR-2009-10-15-1286), §§ 8-3 and 8-4, made under § 38 of the Immigration Act. Under § 8-3, where there are indications that a foreigner in Norway "has been subjected to human trafficking and is prepared to receive help and follow up measures offered by the authorities", a residence permit (the reflection period) may be granted. Further permits may follow after the reflection period ends. These permits do not count towards permanent residence. Under § 8-4, a foreigner who seeks protection after testifying as the aggrieved party in a trial where charges have been brought under Penal Code § 257 (trafficking) "shall be granted a residence permit" that does count towards permanent residence. Exceptions apply where the person qualifies for asylum or special reasons weigh against a permit. A permit may also be granted to someone who has given evidence to a court or the police, if it is shown to be probable that they were trafficked and they have broken with the trafficking network. The third avenue is the Immigration Act (utlendingsloven, LOV-2008-05-15-35) § 53(1)(b). A foreigner holding a family-based permit (§§ 40 or 41) "shall" be given an independent residence permit if cohabitation has ended and "there is reason to believe that the foreigner or any children have been abused" in the relationship. These avenues are limited to trafficking victims and victims of domestic abuse. There is no general immigration status or relief from expulsion for victims of other crimes. Those victims must rely on the general humanitarian clause in § 38 or on proportionality review in expulsion cases.


## Vulnerable migrants

### q3 · Right of child migrants to special protections.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (Lov om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsloven), LOV-2008-05-15-35), §§ 28 third paragraph, 29 second paragraph (f), 38 third paragraph, 40 fifth and sixth paragraphs, 49 third paragraph and 81, and in chapters 11 A and 12 (§ 106 c). In protection cases, § 28 requires that "it shall be taken into account whether the applicant is a child". § 29 recognises persecution in the form of "acts directed specifically at gender or at children". Under § 38, "in cases affecting children, the best interests of the child shall be a fundamental consideration". It adds that children "may be granted a residence permit ... even if the situation is not so serious that a permit would have been granted to an adult". An unaccompanied minor without adequate care on return is a listed humanitarian ground. § 49 repeats the best-interests rule in family immigration. § 40 allows a permit for a stepchild to be refused where the reference person has convictions for sexual offences against children. § 81 requires regulations on children's right to be heard, in line with CRC art. 12, and allows a conversation with the child without parental consent or presence. In that case a temporary guardian is appointed. Chapter 11 A provides a representative for unaccompanied minors. § 106 c sets special rules on arresting and detaining minors. The protections cover all migrant children: asylum seekers, children in family immigration and children facing return. The Constitution (§ 104) and the Human Rights Act (menneskerettsloven), which incorporates the CRC with precedence over ordinary statutes, reinforce them. The law still allows children to be detained for immigration purposes, and unaccompanied minors aged 16-18 can receive permits limited until they turn 18.

### q5 · Right of women migrants to special protections.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Act (utlendingsloven, LOV-2008-05-15-35), §§ 29 second paragraph (a) and (f), 30 first paragraph (c), 40 fifth paragraph, 51 second paragraph and 53 first paragraph (b), and in the Immigration Regulations (utlendingsforskriften, FOR-2009-10-15-1286), § 11-12 first paragraph (b). § 29 recognises that persecution may take the form of "physical or mental violence, including sexualised violence" and "acts directed specifically at gender". This brings gender-based persecution of women, such as forced marriage, FGM and honour violence, within refugee status. § 30 counts former victims of trafficking as a particular social group. In family immigration, § 40 allows a spouse permit to be refused where the applicant "will be ill-treated or grossly exploited". § 51 allows refusal where the marriage was "entered into against the will of one of the parties". § 53(1)(b) gives an independent residence permit when cohabitation has ended and "there is reason to believe that the foreign national or any children have been ill-treated in the relationship", including by the household or in-laws. § 53 second paragraph also covers people who would face "unreasonable difficulties in the home country" because of social and cultural conditions after a break-up, which in practice mostly concerns divorced women. Regulation § 11-12 exempts § 53(1)(b) permit holders from the self-sufficiency requirement for permanent residence. Most of these provisions are worded gender-neutrally, but § 29 expressly targets gender-specific persecution. The protection covers asylum seekers and women in family immigration. CEDAW is incorporated through the Human Rights Act.

### q7 · Rights of migrants with disabilities to special protection.
- Law: Yes
- Implementation: Usually

National law in Norway protects this right in the Immigration Regulations (Forskrift om utlendingers adgang til riket og deres opphold her (utlendingsforskriften), FOR-2009-10-15-1286), §§ 11-9 second paragraph (g), 11-10 (f) and 11-12 first paragraph (a) and (c), and in the Immigration Act (utlendingsloven, LOV-2008-05-15-35), § 38 second paragraph (b). Permanent residence under Act § 62 normally requires passing an oral Norwegian test and a civics test and being self-sufficient for 12 months. The regulations relax all three for migrants with disabilities. The language and civics requirements do not apply where the applicant documents "special health reasons or personal circumstances beyond his or her control" that make it impossible to pass the test. The self-sufficiency requirement does not apply to anyone who "is entitled to disability benefit (uføretrygd) from the National Insurance scheme". It also does not apply to anyone who shows through NAV documentation or a medical certificate that, "due to illness, injury or defect", they could not work more than 50 percent in the last 12 months. In addition, § 38 lists "compelling health circumstances" as a ground for a humanitarian residence permit when other conditions are not met. These rules cover regular migrants seeking permanent residence and, under § 38, people refused asylum. The protection is built around health and incapacity rather than a general disability-specific regime. Asylum reception adaptations, such as dedicated units, rest on UDI instructions rather than statute.
