The data is excellent.
The front door is intimidating.

The Deportation Data Project sues the government for immigration enforcement records and publishes them, free and CC-0. Its users are mostly reporters on deadline, plus organizers and curious citizens. Most of them arrive with a question about a place, not a dataset. Here is who they are, what gets in their way, and three directions for a site that meets them there.

1 · Who comes, and what they came for

Three audiences, one shared need: a number they can trust, for a place they care about, in words they can repeat.

Primary

Journalists

Local and national reporters, data desks, fact-checkers. On deadline, often not data specialists.

  • How many people has ICE arrested in my city since January?
  • Is that up? Compared to what?
  • Give me the sentence and the citation, and tell me what I can't say.
  • What's new in the latest release that's a story?
  • Can I use this chart? Who do I call to check my reading?
Secondary

Community organizers

Rapid-response networks, legal clinics, faith groups, local advocates. Often bilingual audiences.

  • What's happening in our county, in numbers we can bring to a city council meeting?
  • Does our sheriff have a 287(g) agreement?
  • Where are people taken after they're arrested here?
  • Something we can share, in Spanish too.
Also served

Citizens · researchers

Readers who saw a headline; academics and analysts who want the raw files.

  • Is this really happening more, or is it the news?
  • What is a "detainer"? What's "custodial"?
  • Where does this come from, and why should I trust it?
  • (Researchers) Give me the parquet, the codebook, and the code. This works well today; keep it.

2 · What gets in the way today

From walking the live site as each of those people.

The menus are sorted by agency acronym.Explore, Download and Documentation each open onto the same list: ICE, CBP, EOIR, EOUSA, USCIS. You have to know which agency holds your answer before you can ask the question.
The home page opens on a researcher's tool.The first thing below the mission line is a data explorer with column filters and a "has_detention_stay flag". It is powerful, and it tells a newcomer this site is not for them.
The best thing on the site is hidden.The Atlas answers most questions in plain language, by place, with definitions alongside. It is not in the main menu and not on the home page.
Place is how people think. It is not how the site starts."My city", "my county", "my state" is the most common question. The place picker exists, but only inside the Atlas sidebar.
Numbers aren't packaged to be reused.There is no ready-to-quote sentence and no citation line with the release date. The caveats a reporter needs, such as duplicate records, interior vs. border, and officer-observed sex since April 2025, live in notes further down.

Shared principles

Whichever direction wins, these carry over.

  • Sort by task, not by agency.The menu becomes Places · Reports · Data & downloads · How to use the data · About. Agencies appear inside, with plain names: "ICE (arrests and detention)", "Immigration courts (EOIR)".
  • A place search on every page.Country, state, city or ICE field office, the same index the Atlas already ships.
  • Every number travels with its sentence, its date and its caveat.Copy sentence, copy citation (with the data release date), and "before you quote this".
  • Make the Atlas the spine.It is the plain-language layer. Reports, the explorer and the downloads hang off it.
  • Definitions inline, everywhere.A word like "detainer" or "custodial" is underlined, and tapping it explains it. The Atlas's sidebar glossary already does this; extend it to the whole site.
  • Raw data one level down, never gone.Researchers keep the downloads, codebooks and code exactly as they are, under "Data & downloads".

My recommendation

Build Ask as the home page and site structure, since it serves all three audiences without a fork in the road. Add Desk as a /press section, because journalists are the primary users and the copyable sentence, citation and checklist are cheap to make and high in value. Take Local's place card and Spanish glossary into Ask's answer pages rather than building a separate map-first site.
